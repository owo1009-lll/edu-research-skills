# L-N15: Experienced teachers can still be becoming STEM teachers

Status: newly read English development article; qualitative multiple-case study using interviews and cross-case analysis. Selected passages provide useful writing moves. The whole article is not treated as an exemplary report, and its causal extensions are not adopted.

## Identity, access and reading scope

El Nagdi, M., Leammukda, F., & Roehrig, G. (2018). *Developing identities of STEM teachers at emerging STEM schools*. *International Journal of STEM Education, 5*, 36. [Publisher full text](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40594-018-0136-1), DOI 10.1186/s40594-018-0136-1; published 25 September 2018, [CC BY 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/).

The HTML headings are retained in the numbered extraction. Its SHA-256 is `91e4f1270dda07ced82265869e32cf5c17caa58647bcff4f4971911422425576`. The source HTML and extracted body match the earlier acquisition record. [Asset records](../acquisition-L-N15-assets.json) identify the separate table pages and figure. The eight teachers, four from each of two developing urban middle schools, were purposefully selected for active STEM/STEAM participation. Interviews occurred at the end of the schools' first development year. The teachers themselves had two to four years of STEM-setting experience; these time frames concern different things. Individual teachers are the cases, not just the two schools. The paper describes inductive and axial coding and a contextualized grounded theory, but the reported design is an interview-based multiple-case study. It does not present a longitudinal observation of identity formation or a test of learning effects.

## The problem develops through an unresolved role

The broad national-performance opening in B009 is less distinctive than the problem developed in B011–B012: integrated STEM schools are opening while the meaning of the teacher's job remains unsettled. Does one person teach across disciplines, or do disciplinary teachers work together? That question creates a reason to investigate how teachers understand their roles. It connects the institutional change to an object the interviews can address.

The literature review then distinguishes disciplinary knowledge, integration, team teaching and student-centered learning before introducing identity. The distinction between scientific explanation and engineering design in B026–B027 gives content to the integration problem: the disciplines use investigations for different purposes. This is more informative than repeatedly calling STEM complex. However, the extended review sometimes postpones the empirical question. A revision could retain the disciplinary contrast and its implications for teachers while shortening repeated statements of the need for integration.

B038–B044 moves from an account of identity as accumulated professional assets to a dialogical account involving personal and social dimensions. Figure 1 connects assumed STEM roles and teacher identity with a two-way arrow; it is a conceptual framing, not an estimated reciprocal causal model. The authorial position is that teachers interpret a changing role through both personal commitments and professional relationships. Editing should preserve this position rather than replacing it with a list of competencies.

## A sentence group makes an abstract distinction concrete

B077 juxtaposes years of teaching experience with teachers' descriptions of still becoming STEM teachers. Mary has taught for 28 years; Nate for 13. Their accounts make the interpretive problem visible: experience as a teacher and identification with a new interdisciplinary role are different dimensions. The text does not need a new generic sentence saying that identity is multifaceted when these details can carry that work.

The sequence first names a pattern, then gives experienced teachers as cases. The time detail matters because it changes how the reader interprets an emerging identity; it is not decorative biography. The verbs locate the evidence in teachers' self-understanding. They do not establish deficient competence or observed progress over time. B075 says all interviewed teachers saw their identity as under construction, whereas B077 specifies seven of eight. A short account should retain the explicit seven-of-eight scope and record the inconsistency instead of silently harmonizing it.

## Paragraph analysis: personal philosophy enters the explanation

B079 has five sentences. Each adds something different:

| Sentence | Added content | Function and editorial judgment |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | The teachers enter the setting with existing teaching philosophies | Establishes that the new setting meets prior commitments; they do not begin as blank slates |
| 2 | None reports a discrepancy with their understanding of STEM | States a shared perception within this selected group, not a tested condition for success |
| 3 | Yen connects alignment to everyday life and taking science beyond the laboratory | Gives the abstract alignment a substantive meaning |
| 4 | Nate also describes alignment | Adds another voice, but little detail about what alignment means for him; a possible compression point |
| 5 | Calvin describes returning to his purpose in teaching after feeling burned out | Adds a different meaning of alignment: renewed personal orientation, rather than another synonym for agreement |

The paragraph's strength is the movement from a shared relation to different meanings of that relation. The cases should not be collapsed into one composite teacher or a universal sequence. Calvin's account is his interpretation of his experience; it is not a measured effect of the school reform on burnout.

B087 uses a related progression for collaboration: a shared emphasis, a general statement about working with others, Mary's example of connecting art and science in planning, and Calvin's view that a STEM teacher is best understood within a group of teachers with varied expertise. The last account gives collaboration conceptual force: it concerns how the role is constituted, not only whether colleagues get along. Some of the surrounding results nevertheless remain short theme-and-quotation lists. The useful move is the relation developed through selected accounts, not the number of quotations or a mandatory claim–quote–theory format.

## Discussion analysis: what advances and what repeats

B097 contains seven sentences. Sentence 1 announces that participants offer a clear picture; sentence 2 relates emerging identity to the theoretical framework; sentence 3 specifies that new STEM experiences add a dimension to an existing identity. Sentence 4 returns to the important puzzle of experienced teachers seeing themselves as developing, but also asserts higher commitment and motivation without a separate longitudinal comparison. Sentences 5 and 6 restate theoretical characterizations of shifting identity. Sentence 7 returns to Mary's account.

The paragraph's substantive center is the relation between established experience and a new role. A tighter version can place Mary's experience beside that interpretation, then use the literature to explain why the relationship matters. Three successive descriptions of fluid or shifting identity do not add three distinct inferences. Removing this repetition need not weaken the authors' theoretical position. The unsupported commitment increase should be addressed specifically, not used as a reason to discard the entire interpretation.

In B099, participants' reported alignment becomes a claim that misalignment would damage the professional community, students and the teacher. This inverse consequence was not demonstrated by the eight selected accounts, which report alignment. Similarly, B104 and B106 move from participants' conceptions to reduced conflict and increased achievement. Those outcomes were not measured here. The paper itself calls for research linking reported characteristics to classroom action and students' learning in B109. The qualification belongs beside the proposed consequence when that consequence is discussed.

## Article-level organization and authorial position

The article proceeds from changing school arrangements to role ambiguity, then to identity theory, the two school contexts, teachers' accounts and implications. The school descriptions distinguish appointed teams at Falconer from voluntary teams at Noddack and describe concrete curriculum projects. These details establish different contexts for the same role question. The results do not systematically compare those recruitment arrangements, so the reader cannot infer that one arrangement caused stronger identity formation.

The three main themes organize identity, professional characteristics and personal characteristics. This is useful for navigation, but the most explanatory passages cross those headings: an existing philosophy meets a new professional role; disciplinary expertise becomes a resource in collaborative planning. A revised discussion can organize around these relations instead of repeating all three theme lists.

The authors argue that school leaders should support collaboration and professional learning and attend to teachers' understanding of STEM. That practical judgment can remain explicit as an implication of the reported role demands. It should not be rewritten into universal hesitation simply because no intervention was tested. Conversely, the proposed use of characteristics for recruitment is a recommendation, not a validated selection instrument or demonstrated route to achievement gains. These are distinct assessments.

## Same-fact English editing examples

The following are newly composed editorial illustrations based on the read article. Neither the starting text nor the revision is a quotation or an actual model-run result. They preserve the same source facts and interpretive scope.

**Starting passage: the details and the interpretation are separated**

> Seven of the eight participants described themselves as still becoming STEM teachers. Mary had taught for 28 years and said that she was working on identifying as a STEM teacher. Nate had taught for 13 years and also described himself as becoming a STEAM teacher. The teachers were experienced. Their STEM identities were emerging. This shows that teacher identity is dynamic and that new professional contexts matter for identity.

**Revision: the apparent tension does explanatory work**

> Seven of the eight participants described themselves as still becoming STEM teachers, including Mary, with 28 years of teaching experience, and Nate, with 13. Their accounts distinguish an established teaching career from identification with a new interdisciplinary role. Becoming a STEM teacher meant reinterpreting existing experience within the developing schools, which gives the authors' account of changing identity a concrete professional context.

**Compression**

> Mary and Nate had taught for 28 and 13 years, respectively, yet both described themselves as still becoming STEM teachers. Their accounts locate identity development within an established career: experience did not settle how they understood their new interdisciplinary role.

The revision links the biographical details to the interpretation. The compression keeps the two named cases and the relation between career and role; it omits the group count rather than replacing it with an inaccurate claim about all participants. It does not invent observed classroom change, deficient competence or a measured development trajectory.

**Starting passage: a general theme obscures what collaboration entails**

> Participants said that collaboration was important. Mary described planning with other teachers and integrating science into art for problem solving. Calvin described a STEM teacher as part of a group with varied academic backgrounds. These accounts show that collaboration was a characteristic of STEM teachers. They also show that working together was important for STEM teaching.

**Revision and compression**

> Mary's account of planning across art and science gives collaboration a curricular purpose. Calvin's emphasis on a group with varied academic backgrounds extends that point: the STEM role draws on expertise distributed across teachers. In these accounts, collaboration helps define how interdisciplinary teaching is organized, rather than simply naming a desirable personal trait.

The last sentence is an interpretation of the two reported perspectives. It does not claim that a team was observed to outperform a single teacher. The edit preserves a definite conceptual contribution and removes repeated approval of collaboration.

## Unresolved source issues and use decision

Table 1 lists Gary, whereas B074 attributes a statement to Jason, who is not listed. This remains an attribution query; no replacement name is guessed. The all-versus-seven wording about emerging identity also remains recorded. The paper's ethics fields state that approval and publication consent are not applicable; the main text does not explain that designation. This is a reporting limit, not evidence that consent was absent. The reference list also contains visibly irregular entries, which are not imported as verified citations.

Within the qualitative group, this study provides an interview-based counterpart to [L-N13](L-N13.md), which follows classroom activity and documents, and [L-N14](L-N14.md), which develops agency through contrasting roles and relationships. Here the strongest comparison joins career experience and current self-understanding within named accounts. It does not warrant recreating an observed interaction or forcing a temporal sequence from separate interviews.

Decision: retain selected positive writing moves and the overextended discussion as a contrast case. This adds a concrete variant to L-C01 and the existing case-comparison guidance; it does not justify a new universal structure or a runtime rule. Same-journal/team overlap is visible in the bibliography and corpus, so this is not counted as an entirely independent research tradition. No behavioral benefit or final holdout result is claimed.
