## Three concepts that do different work

The opening sections define environmental literacy as multiple practices for experiencing, understanding and acting in the world. Global citizenship education adds attention to relationships and forces beyond the immediate locality. Dialogism concerns encounters with differing perspectives and the open-ended development of understanding. The concepts overlap, but the article assigns them different explanatory roles.

The five-sentence synthesis paragraph on p. 184 makes this division useful:

| Sentence | Added work |
|---|---|
| 1 | States that the project brings the approaches together while retaining their different emphases. |
| 2 | Gives environmental literacy a practical role: students use multiple forms of meaning-making to engage with environments. |
| 3 | Distinguishes the international/intercultural reach of global citizenship from dialogism's emphasis on critical encounters, including with overseas peers. |
| 4 | Identifies the common positioning of students as knowledgeable actors and the shared educational purposes. |
| 5 | Explains what this combination changes in the intended educational process: opportunities to learn from peers' lives without prescribing every outcome. |

This is a conceptual argument for complementarity, not a statistical interaction claim. Deleting the framework merely because the concepts overlap would remove the authors' explanation of the project. Conversely, retaining three labels without their different roles would make the framework decorative. The final sentence is strongly affirmative; its language should be considered alongside the actual classroom accounts, rather than copied as a general promise about all cross-cultural projects.

The context discussion then compares the two countries as the authors understand them: different policy arrangements, shared implementation difficulties and a shortage of concrete primary-classroom examples. The need for the study is more specific than an unexplored location. It asks how teachers can enact the proposed educational approach under actual curricular conditions. The comparison does not establish a country ranking, and the later analysis explicitly resists reducing the projects to a single North–South comparison.

## Methods as a sequence of reasoned choices

On pp. 188–190, the authors connect flexibility to their theoretical position. Teachers agree on broad principles but adapt the activities, timing and curricular location. Table 1 makes the resulting variation inspectable: rainwater harvesting, food, environmental-club activity, design, local artwork and gardens appear in different school settings. The table is a map for reading later examples, not a table of equivalent intervention doses.

The analysis section also shows a decision changing during the project. Teachers' hesitation in an early online workshop leads the researchers to increase in-person contact and clarify deadlines and expectations. That detail makes responsiveness concrete; it is more informative than simply calling the process collaborative. The later shared interpretation connects inductive coding with the theoretical frameworks. A summary should preserve both stages, not call the analysis wholly theory-free merely because the initial coding avoided predetermined concepts.

There is an important tension worth preserving. Openness in classroom activity coexists with teachers' requests for clearer direction from researchers. This does not automatically refute the dialogic approach. It identifies different kinds of structure: teachers can need practical guidance while retaining room to shape curricular work. A revision that removes the hesitation to make the project appear uniformly autonomous would lose that distinction.

## From an encounter to an interpretation

The four-sentence paragraph about the video exchange on p. 193 is a compact positive example:

| Sentence | Added work |
|---|---|
| 1 | Locates the argument in children's actions during and after a particular encounter. |
| 2 | Identifies what the encounter made unfamiliar: peers did not know the ecosystems that children themselves took for granted. |
| 3 | Describes the response in each setting: a mangrove diorama and woodland animals incorporated into artwork. The children's response changes what they create. |
| 4 | Interprets the exchange in two directions: learning about another environment also makes the distinctiveness of one's own environment visible. |

The objects persist through the sentences. Familiar environments become unfamiliar to an audience; that difference motivates an artifact; making the artifact supplies a basis for the interpretation. The paragraph therefore explains an educational process rather than attaching a theory name to a finished product. The final claim is the authors' reading of the reported encounter, not a separately estimated learning effect.

The preceding sustainable-travel passage adds a different kind of evidence. A child considers reducing cars and growing trees, then raises the time required and the limits of controlling other people's choices. The authors interpret that reasoning as movement toward a more complex environmental understanding. Retaining the unresolved difficulty matters: a clean recommendation to plant more trees would erase the reasoning the passage is meant to reveal.

Elsewhere, family recollections of a water crisis enter a classroom investigation and posters for overseas peers. This gives intergenerational knowledge a visible role. It also distinguishes a contribution to the inquiry from a source that merely confirms a lesson already decided by the teacher.

## What the figures do and do not show

The four figures were read in their rendered page context:

- Figure 1, p. 192, reproduces two rainwater-harvesting posters. Written explanation and diagrams make the communicative artifact visible. The figure is not a before/after measure of knowledge or an engineering validation of the depicted systems.
- Figure 2, p. 193, shows an image of a valued local space. The text describes a process using layers for threats and alternatives; the single reproduced view does not independently display every layer or every stage of that process.
- Figure 3, p. 194, shows the mangrove diorama discussed in the encounter paragraph. The narrative supplies the occasion and intended audience; the object alone cannot establish the children's prior knowledge or subsequent interpretation.
- Figure 4, p. 197, shows the eco-dress and accompanying explanatory display. Its argumentative function is to connect materials, making and environmental inquiry, rather than serve as decoration for a general claim about engagement.

The useful writing move is to connect an artifact to the action that produced it and the question it helps answer. It is not to require an image for every qualitative claim. No attempt was made to infer unreported participant characteristics from the photographs.

## A conceptual qualification that preserves a strong position

The four-sentence discussion paragraph on p. 198 moves from the interpretation of intercultural encounters to a deliberate distinction in terminology. First it states the learning and solidarity the authors see in the material. Second it connects that reading with their account of change. Third it explains why they do not equate the encounter with fully experiencing another person's perspective. Fourth it states the more precise positive contribution: pupils reconsider what seems normal and recognize the cultural location and partiality of their own understanding.

The fourth sentence is essential. Without it, the paragraph would finish with a warning about what cannot be claimed. With it, the qualification develops the concept and clarifies what the research adds. This is not a general ban on *empathy*: it is the authors' theoretical choice in this argument. Replacing their position with a generic claim that the project improved empathy would alter the framework, while removing the initial positive claims would make the paragraph needlessly defensive.

The authorial voice remains explicit through references to their interpretation and commitments. The concepts of epistemic justice, collective responsibility and more-than-human relations should not be reduced to a neutral list of student skills. At the same time, the closing teacher reflection concerns what pupils may carry into future lives; it is a prospective judgment, not completed longitudinal follow-up.

## Whole-article organization and useful compression

The article moves from a practical need to conceptual roles, contextual differences, project development, methods and classroom accounts. Findings cover the variety of classroom approaches, students' environmental understanding, their relations and actions, and teachers' learning. Discussion then returns to the concepts with more specific examples of what they explain. This is an observed organization, not a required template or an instruction to number every qualitative research question.

The treatment of teachers extends the argument beyond student outcomes. Teachers describe following children's interests, reconsidering what art or teaching can be, and finding curricular flexibility. The researcher partnership is then examined through the same commitment to situated knowledge. This extension gives the concept consequences at several levels instead of merely repeating the concept in several sections.

Some procedural summaries repeat the full objectives and broad positive descriptions. Those could be shortened by preserving the particular explanatory addition in each section. The counterexamples should remain: not every class participated in video conferences, some teachers were reluctant to share work, and one planned exchange was cancelled during a school inspection. The authors derive a practical emphasis on relationships and early dialogue from these difficulties. That is a situated recommendation, not an experimentally validated universal condition for success.

## Original English same-fact revision and compression

The following are newly written teaching examples, not quotations from the article or outputs of a new model comparison. Both use the same reported video-exchange episode on p. 193 and the distinction between direct and indirect participation on p. 196. They address a paragraph that repeats the value of dialogue without explaining it.

**Before — the events are present but their relationship is underdeveloped**

> Cross-cultural dialogue was an important part of the project. During a video exchange, children found that their overseas peers did not know the mangroves or woodlands that were familiar to them. Maldivian children made a mangrove diorama, and English children included woodland animals in their artwork for the other class. The authors interpreted this as learning about other places and their own ecosystems. These activities highlight the importance of dialogue for environmental learning. Teachers elsewhere in the project also reported engagement when classes connected indirectly through artwork, without video conferencing. The findings therefore underline the value of cross-cultural learning in different forms.

**After — explain why the response matters**

> The video exchange made familiar environments newly worth explaining: children discovered that their overseas peers did not know their local mangroves or woodlands. Maldivian pupils responded with a mangrove diorama, while English pupils incorporated woodland animals into artwork for their counterparts. The authors interpret these responses as a reciprocal learning process: explaining a place to someone who does not know it can also bring its distinctiveness into view for the speaker. The project offered more than one route into that exchange; teachers in other classes reported engagement through shared artwork without video conferencing.

**Compressed — preserve the encounter, response, interpretation and different participation route**

> Discovering that overseas peers did not know their local ecosystems prompted children to create a mangrove diorama and woodland artwork. The authors interpret this exchange as learning about others while reconsidering one's own familiar environment. Other teachers reported engagement through shared artwork without video conferencing, keeping direct online contact from becoming the only route represented in the account.

The revision replaces repeated declarations of value with the reason the audience matters. It retains both countries' responses and attributes the conceptual reading to the authors. The additional reports concern other classes; they are not evidence that direct and indirect contact had equal effects. The wording preserves a strong educational interpretation without adding scores, a causal mechanism test, or an experience claimed for every child.

## Boundaries to retain

The project combines several forms of qualitative material. Approximate participation counts and a table of project types do not make it mixed methods. Nor does the word *significant* in the conclusion denote a significance test. The article's accounts support interpretation of activity, meaning and professional reflection; they should not be silently converted into measured comparative gains.

Some broad statements about transformation, empowerment and future action exceed the detail of the selected episodes if read as uniform, demonstrated outcomes. Keep their theoretical or prospective role explicit where it matters. The practical difficulties and differing participation routes are substantive findings, not optional disclaimers to delete for fluency.

The source reports that bilingual researchers translated Dhivehi material accurately; this note cannot independently certify that claim. It also says the coding began without prior concepts and later linked themes to theory. Retaining that sequence is more accurate than treating either description in isolation. The absence of a complete per-class material inventory limits assessment of how widely each interpretation is represented.

The methods' three agreed principles include a set of four named themes, peer learning and art. This is not an arithmetic contradiction: the four themes belong to one principle. The source's broader account of scientific knowledge, dialogism and justice should likewise be read in context rather than turned into a rule against factual teaching or a prescriptive classroom recipe.

It does not impose these authors' theoretical commitments on other traditions, require contrasting countries, or turn every artwork into evidence of transformation. Existing qualitative and author-voice guidance already permits the useful actions. This is additional development evidence and an English demonstration, not a new runtime rule, a new L-C01 result or final independent validation.
