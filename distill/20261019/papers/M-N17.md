## Why this qualifies as substantive integration

Ten vocational students completed a thinking-aloud task in April 2023. The subsequent online survey analyzed 130 participants aged 16–25. The original classroom survey was discarded as unsuitable and treated as a pilot; it is not an additional confirmatory sample. The interviews and the final survey are different phases and samples, not matched individual records.

Integration is traceable at two points:

| Connection | Evidence and location | Added knowledge or decision |
|---|---|---|
| Interview observation → instrument revision | Main pp. 4–5; S1 File p. 1 | Two pictorial icons were changed after participants treated them as bars; two pie-chart questions were aligned with the category emphasized in the title. Instructions were also changed to address reliance on expectations. The qualitative work alters what the quantitative phase asks people to do. |
| Interview interpretation → meaning of survey patterns | Main pp. 11, 16, section 5.2; Figure 7; codebook | Participants sometimes evaluated credibility or severity, rather than only numerical difference. The strong response to the dating-app privacy example is connected to the high survey ratings for that context. The accounts make the score's meaning less singular. |
| Later comparison → unresolved alternatives | Main pp. 14–16 | Lower ratings when viewing corrections are interpreted alongside the possibility that responses to the topic changed on a second viewing. This is a possible interpretation, not a participant-level causal mediation result. |

The study therefore contributes more than one numerical section and one interview section. Its strongest mixed-methods move is **changing the interpretation of the measurement**. It does not need an invented claim that interviews prove the statistical mechanism. It contains no formal qualitative–quantitative joint display; the numerical figures display survey distributions, not a merged participant matrix.

## How the introduction creates a specific problem

Pages 1–2 move from misinformation to graph corrections and then to vocationally educated young adults. The literature review does more than state that this population has been neglected: it asks whether a strategy tested in a relatively clean experimental setting works with more realistic graphics, and whether removing decoration helps. The proposed value is a usable correction for an identified audience and task.

Pages 2–4 distinguish looking at visual differences from reading values and labels, then explain how graph design can separate those impressions. Examples of truncated bars, incorrectly scaled icons and 3D pies give the concept observable content. The later debate over minimal versus embellished design creates a genuine choice: distraction and memorability need not favor the same design. Those are the authors' summaries of prior work; this reading does not independently verify each cited experiment.

The claim that this population is especially vulnerable remains a premise to examine, not an inherent characteristic to generalize. The survey's high proportion at MBO level 4 and relatively high graph-literacy distribution also matter to the population actually studied (Table 2).

## Sentence-level and paragraph-level development

Section 5.2, p. 16, is the clearest short integration passage. Its first paragraph has three sentences; the next has three. Each does different work:

| Sentence | New contribution | Reasoning carried into the next sentence |
|---|---|---|
| First paragraph, 1 | Identifies several meanings participants assigned to an evaluation task: severity, credibility and personal experience as well as numerical difference. | Turns a general context theme into a claim about how a response is produced. |
| 2 | Names the dating-app privacy graph as a case that evoked strong emotion. | Gives the interpretation a specific object that can be located in both strands. |
| 3 | Connects that context to the survey's high ratings and endpoint concentration. | The numerical pattern becomes interpretable without claiming that the samples contain the same people. |
| Second paragraph, 1 | States that ratings generally fell when corrected graphs were shown. | Returns to the intervention question rather than remaining in a qualitative aside. |
| 2 | Gives the authors' interpretation in terms of the correction. | Makes their substantive position explicit. |
| 3 | Introduces a changed response to the topic on repeated viewing as another possible contributor. | The earlier participant accounts now qualify a specific interpretation of the numerical change. |

The progression works because the topic stays concrete: what participants evaluated, which graph elicited a response, and what a lower rating can mean. The link is not the generic phrase that the findings complement one another. The second paragraph could be shortened by combining its first two sentences; its third sentence adds a distinct explanation and should survive compression.

The results' quotations have identifiable jobs. In section 4.1.5, p. 11, one participant notices where the vertical axis starts; another comments on the mismatch between apparent and numerical differences. The comparison supports a distinction between noticing a feature and recognizing its misleading consequence. Later examples show changed language after correction (pp. 11–12). This develops an interpretation rather than letting a string of quotations stand in for analysis. It also does not establish durable learning outside the task.

The codebook makes the distinction inspectable: level 1 is spotting a relevant element, while level 2 involves its effect on evaluation. It separately codes severity, credibility, numerical difference and visual focus. These are analytic distinctions supported by the presented examples, not measured psychological stages that every learner must traverse.

## Whole-article organization and author position

| Part | What it adds |
|---|---|
| Introduction and framework, pp. 1–4 | Defines the correction problem and competing design considerations, then gives qualitative and quantitative questions distinct jobs. |
| Methods, pp. 4–9 | Explains why the final survey differs from the classroom pilot and separates baseline, correction and new-graph comparisons. Figure 2 makes the task sequence concrete; Figure 5 shows which comparisons answer which hypotheses. |
| Qualitative results, pp. 10–12 | Develops strategies, preferences, context and recognition. Disagreement about visual design is retained instead of choosing only favorable quotations. |
| Quantitative results, pp. 12–15 | Separates whether a graph misleads, what happens when a correction is shown, and responses to new graphs. Detailed model tables are delegated to supplements. |
| Discussion, pp. 15–17 | Connects responses to contexts, revisits the theoretical reading sequence and argues for greater inclusion of vocational students. Some abstract claims are broader than the later discussion and tables warrant. |

The position in favor of including an often-neglected population is an explicit research priority. Editing the statistical claims need not remove this position. By contrast, claims about what such students generally care about or the unique power of personal contact require more than recruitment impressions. Keep the normative commitment and the observed recruitment experience distinguishable.

The opening of the discussion repeats several general statements about graph reading already developed in the framework. A shorter bridge could move directly to the practical correction question. Context matters is worth repeating only when its job changes: first as a participant finding, then as a specific interpretation of a rating. Those are different functions, not automatically redundant caveats.

## Source checks that constrain the positive example

1. **Looking versus speaking.** The abstract describes a contradiction with the proposed reading sequence, but section 5.1 acknowledges that audio cannot determine when participants first looked, and that thinking aloud may change what they verbalize. Preserve the observed verbal strategies without claiming to have disproven an unobserved eye-movement sequence.
2. **Reference-category coefficients.** S3 File Table 1 includes graph-type interactions and uses bars as the reference. Its −10.00 coefficient is therefore not automatically the effect averaged over all graph types. Likewise, the 8.43 and 6.01 coefficients in S4 File Table 1 are bar-reference contrasts, although the main text calls the latter an all-types difference. Nonsignificant interactions do not turn a reference coefficient into a marginal average.
3. **Immediate change versus learning.** The reported 2.42-point reduction subtracts two coefficients from separate baseline and new-graph models. The supplied tables do not provide a direct uncertainty estimate for that subtraction. Different graph contexts occur at the two stages, everyone sees corrections, and there is no delayed assessment or no-correction control. The recognition examples remain meaningful, but the result should not be recast as established durable transfer.
4. **Design comparison.** S3 File Table 2 does not find a significant clean/full contrast for the immediate correction comparison. However, the blanket later no-difference wording must also account for S4 File Table 2: misleadingness × full design is reported at p = .011, and its pictorial-area three-way term at p = .025. These are conditional model terms, not by themselves an overall ranking of the designs. No unreported omnibus result or simple-effect analysis is invented.
5. **Graph literacy and dependence.** The displayed exploratory literacy models include an additive score, not every interaction required to conclude that literacy offers no protection against distortion. The pooled graph-type t-tests also display degrees of freedom above 500 despite 130 people rating several graphs; their independence assumption needs checking. The later mixed models explicitly include participant and context effects. These are recorded questions about interpretation and analysis, not silently recomputed results.

These limitations do not nullify the instrument revisions or the demonstrated interpretive role of the interviews. They explain why this article supplies selected writing actions and counterexamples rather than a whole-paper template.

## Original English same-fact edits

The following paragraphs are newly authored demonstrations, not excerpts or outputs from a controlled skill comparison. They use the same factual set: the two sample sizes, topic-based interview responses, the dating-app example, the survey pattern and the untested repeated-viewing explanation. Locations: main pp. 5, 11, 14, 16 and Figure 7.

**Before: separate summaries with a generic connection.**

> The interviews with ten vocational students showed that context was relevant to graph evaluation. Students sometimes judged the seriousness or credibility of the topic rather than just the numerical difference. The dating-app privacy graph evoked strong emotions. The online survey of 130 participants showed very high ratings for that context. Ratings generally decreased when corrected graphs were shown. The qualitative and quantitative results therefore provide complementary information. The authors also suggested that a lower rating on a second viewing could reflect a changed emotional response to the topic. This explanation was not independently tested, and the two samples were not matched at the individual level.

**After: make the interpretive increment explicit.**

> What participants judged helps explain what a graph-evaluation score can mean. In interviews with ten vocational students, responses sometimes concerned the seriousness or credibility of a topic rather than its numerical difference. Strong reactions to the dating-app privacy graph give one concrete example; that context also received very high ratings in the separate survey of 130 participants. Read together, these findings complicate the interpretation of lower ratings when corrections were shown: a second viewing might also change the emotional response to the topic. This is an explanatory possibility across the two samples, not a tested individual pathway.

**Compressed.**

> Interviewees sometimes judged a graph's topic rather than only its numerical difference. Their strong reactions to the dating-app privacy example help interpret the very high ratings for that context in the separate survey. The comparison also changes how lower ratings after correction are understood: they may partly reflect a changed emotional response on a second viewing. That possibility remains untested; the two strands were not linked at the individual level.

The after version makes the contribution the opening claim: the meaning of the measure changes. It replaces a generic assertion of complementarity with an explicit connection. Compression omits sample sizes already supplied in methods but keeps the concrete context, direction of the numerical pattern, alternative explanation and cross-sample boundary. It does not add causation, delete an inconvenient finding or confuse design preference with effectiveness. A neighboring results paragraph would still need to report the main quantitative contrasts and the unresolved design-interaction issue; this paragraph is not a replacement for the whole results section.

## Relation to existing mixed-methods guidance

M-N04 and M-N15 already illustrate qualitative evidence shaping a quantitative instrument; M-N17 adds a traceable change to particular graph items and an interpretation of what the response scale measures. Unlike M-N15's joint display, its integration is largely procedural and narrative. It supports the existing principle of choosing an integration form for its actual job. The source is added to development comparisons; there is no new runtime requirement to include a joint display, invent disagreement, alternate paragraphs or repeat a design disclaimer. Whether a compact positive example improves current writing remains a separate empirical question.
