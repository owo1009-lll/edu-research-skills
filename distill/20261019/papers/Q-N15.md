# Q-N15: Turn an adverse overall result into specific explanatory questions

Status: new English development analysis of a quantitative cluster-trial subgroup report. Selected argument moves are useful; substantial interpretive and reporting problems prevent treating the whole article as a model. This is the same LEIP intervention as the cited main report and protocol, not another independent trial.

## Problem, comparison and contribution

The introduction does something more useful than hiding the unsuccessful intervention behind a generic need for more research. It identifies an adverse overall result, makes the possibility of harm consequential, and asks which pupils and implementation conditions are associated with different outcomes. The study's question remains educational: can a programme intended to keep vulnerable pupils in school do harm, and what should be understood before using it again?

The background gives the intervention a causal rationale: interpersonal communication and broader social skills were expected to reduce disruptive behavior and exclusions. The baseline-characteristics discussion then introduces competing expectations. Greater initial problems could leave more scope for improvement, whereas fewer problems could make it easier to benefit. That opposition gives subgroup analysis an explanatory purpose. It does not require inventing a directional hypothesis when the literature, as reviewed by the authors, supplies two plausible possibilities.

The programme combined weekly group and one-to-one work over 12 weeks. Schools were assigned using minimization with a random component: 17 treatment schools and 19 control schools. Control pupils received a one-hour employability workshop. Describing this as a comparison with no intervention would change the study. The current report examines school exclusions from pupil reports, teacher reports and administrative records; it is quantitative despite using several information sources.

The measurements do not share identical observation windows. Baseline reports concern nine months, post-intervention questionnaires four weeks, and administrative post-intervention records six weeks. Tables 1–2 distinguish available-case populations and loss to follow-up. Keeping those distinctions makes the results intelligible: the three sources are related views of exclusion, not interchangeable measurements from one perfectly aligned sample.

The positive writing action is to name the condition, comparison and outcome source before interpreting the pattern. It is not to organize the discussion only around significant coefficients. Estimates in different directions and results without clear moderation also bear on the question.

## Sentence and paragraph work

The short baseline-problems paragraph on p. 3 has three sentences with distinct functions. First, it presents a rationale for expecting greater benefit among pupils with more initial difficulties. Second, it introduces the contrary rationale that fewer difficulties could make participation more productive. Third, it turns that unresolved opposition into analyses of behavior and communication. The second sentence does not merely list another study: it changes what can reasonably be predicted. The third uses that change to justify an open comparison.

The discussion paragraph about one-to-one engagement on p. 15 develops a more ambitious explanation. Its six sentences perform the following work:

| Sentence | Contribution to the paragraph |
|---|---|
| 1 | Announces implementation as a candidate explanation and identifies the one-to-one results as the evidence to consider. |
| 2 | States the adverse self-report association in the relevant subgroup. |
| 3 | Contrasts the practical demand of engaging one pupil with managing a larger group, making the proposed explanation concrete. |
| 4 | Introduces a mentoring study with a comparable format, as described in the authors' literature review. |
| 5 | Extends the explanation to the format of the sessions. |
| 6 | Reconciles design and delivery as potentially connected explanations instead of presenting a forced either/or choice. |

This paragraph gives literature and practical detail jobs in an explanation. The comparison of session demands is understandable because it names what workers must actually do. However, the programme included both session formats for the same population. The observational engagement comparisons do not isolate their effects, and the cited mentoring study was not independently read here. The paragraph is useful as an example of developing a hypothesis, not as proof that the preferred mechanism has been identified.

The preceding peer-influence discussion is strongest when it defines the proposed process in ordinary actions—peers rewarding behavior and forming norms—and then asks what the available measures actually capture. It becomes less secure when session behavior or time on task stands in for measured peer reinforcement. An edit should retain the comparison of explanations and the authors' reasoned preference, while accurately naming the evidence supporting that preference.

At sentence level, the article's clearest result descriptions keep pupils and teachers visible as subjects: teachers report an outcome for pupils at a specified baseline level. Some later sentences replace that relation with an unqualified claim that pupils benefited or did not benefit. The shorter wording loses an important part of the result. Preserving the reporting source can improve specificity without repeating the complete limitations section.

## Whole-article movement and author position

The introduction moves from an unsuccessful overall trial to plausible variation in response. Methods separate baseline attributes, post-allocation participation and demographic characteristics, then explain the different exclusion sources. Results give the estimates. Discussion compares selection, peer influence, programme content and implementation, and returns to the practical obligation to examine harm.

The sustained position is that a well-intentioned activity is not justified solely by the desire to act. That position can remain firm. It is compatible with acknowledging that this analysis cannot select a single mechanism or certify a superior alternative. The final recommendation to consider school-wide approaches relies on the authors' broader literature argument; this trial did not directly compare the programme with a school-wide intervention.

There is useful repetition when the discussion revisits attendance to explain selection and implementation. There is less added value when it repeats several nonsignificant comparisons as proof of no benefit. A tighter discussion would retain the adverse findings, explain what the competing accounts would require, and distinguish the practical judgment about evaluation from empirical claims about an untested replacement programme.

The contribution is an examination of where an unsuccessful programme's patterns warrant attention, not a newly verified mechanism of harm. That is still a substantive contribution: it gives subsequent evaluation more specific questions than whether the programme worked on average.

## Issues retained rather than learned as rules

- The abstract reports 373 treatment and 369 control pupils; Figure 1 displays 373 and 365 among 738 consenting pupils. Table 1 also reports 685 in teacher FIML analyses while surrounding text mentions 689 with teacher data. These population labels and denominators need reconciliation, not silent replacement.
- Provider-attendance prose uses at least five group and six individual sessions; Table 3 uses greater-than signs. The high-attendance rule requiring 10–12 sessions of each format is also different from later wording that divides pupils by total attendance of 19–24 versus 1–18. The latter can select a different set of pupils. These definitions must not be collapsed into a single precisely verified dosage rule.
- Engagement scales are described with worse behavior/off-task time at higher raw values, while the subsequent classification calls higher scores greater engagement. A reversal may be missing from the description; neither the raw scoring nor the subgroup labels can be corrected from this report alone.
- Tables label entries OR (se) without clearly identifying the standard-error scale. The note does not manufacture confidence intervals by treating those SEs as odds-ratio-scale errors. Source values are retained as reported.
- The discussion repeatedly turns nonsignificance into no difference or no benefit. The power section itself says smaller moderation effects may not be detected. The estimate, inferential comparison and uncertainty should govern the claim.
- Higher between-school ICC in teacher reports does not by itself establish unreliable reporting. It can reflect shared school conditions. Neither an unreliability claim nor its proposed explanation is verified here.
- The text alternates beta labels for moderation and defends a modeling choice in terms of reduced standard errors and power. Without code or reanalysis, this note neither validates the specification nor supplies a corrected p value. The absence of multiplicity adjustment and the exploratory interpretation remain relevant.
- A significant result in one source and a nonsignificant result in another does not establish a between-source difference. Conversely, matching directions do not establish replicated effects. The administrative school-year estimate is an explicit contrary pattern that the final summary should preserve.

## Original English same-fact edits

These newly authored demonstrations use the teacher-reported baseline-behavior pattern and the other sources' nonsignificant interactions. They are not source quotations or outputs of a controlled skill experiment. No confidence intervals or mechanisms are invented.

**Before: report the ingredients but leave their relationship implicit.**

> Baseline antisocial behavior moderated the intervention association with teacher-reported exclusion (interaction OR = 0.38, p = .012). The treatment-versus-control odds ratios were 1.75 at one SD below the baseline mean, 0.96 at the mean, and 0.52 at one SD above it. The corresponding interaction was not significant in pupil reports or administrative records. These findings are relevant to understanding different responses to the intervention. The teacher-report results show a pattern that should be examined further.

**After: state the pattern, then define the remaining explanatory question.**

> In teacher reports, the estimated intervention association changed direction across pupils' initial antisocial behavior: treatment-versus-control odds ratios fell from 1.75 at one SD below the mean to 0.96 at the mean and 0.52 at one SD above it (interaction OR = 0.38, p = .012). This pattern directs attention to how initial difficulties relate to subsequent exclusion. The interaction was not significant in pupil reports or administrative records, leaving its consistency across sources unresolved.

**Compressed.**

> The teacher-report interaction (OR = 0.38, p = .012) associated greater baseline antisocial behavior with a smaller treatment-versus-control exclusion contrast: the estimated odds ratios were 1.75, 0.96 and 0.52 at one SD below, at and one SD above the mean. Pupil and administrative records did not establish the same moderation.

The edit makes the changing comparison the paragraph's subject and removes generic relevance statements. It retains all three simple-effect estimates, the interaction, the outcome source and the unresolved corroboration. It does not infer that one subgroup benefited significantly from the significance of the interaction, or convert an odds ratio into an absolute risk difference.

## Place in the developing quantitative comparison

[Q-N16](Q-N16.md) connects a theory-driven manipulation to different measured outcomes. [Q-N12](Q-N12.md) connects a learning comparison to its educational task. Q-N15 adds an adverse-result case: the next question can concern variation and implementation rather than a rescued average benefit. Its strongest local move is the use of competing substantive expectations to frame a comparison; its weakest move is converting limited subgroup evidence into settled causal explanations. The current quantitative route already distinguishes direct comparisons, sources and interpretation. This reading supports a conditional illustration and counterexamples, not a new universal discussion sequence or an adopted rule without behavioral evidence.
