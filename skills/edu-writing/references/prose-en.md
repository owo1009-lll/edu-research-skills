# How English SSCI articles are written: paragraphs and sentences

Figures come from 50 empirical articles published 2024–2026 (repository `distill/results/writing_moves_en.md`): 22 quantitative, 15 qualitative and 13 mixed-methods.
- **Top tier (35 articles):** from journals such as AERJ, Journal of Educational Psychology, Contemporary Educational Psychology, Learning and Instruction, Computers & Education, Educational Researcher, Sociology of Education, AERA Open, Journal of Teacher Education, Teaching and Teacher Education, JRST, Science Education, BERJ, Higher Education, Studies in Higher Education, BJET, JLS, Psychology of Music and the British Journal of Music Education.
- **Other 15 articles:** from Computers & Education, BJET, ETR&D, IJETHE, Education and Information Technologies, TATE, IJME and Music Education Research.

Figures are medians unless stated. Where the groups differ, write to the top tier; where designs differ, follow §7.

[voice-en](voice-en.md) covers stance: where to state and where to hedge. This file covers how paragraphs move, how literature is used and how sentences are built. Read both.

## 1. Each paragraph completes its own argument

- A paragraph makes one claim. It opens with a topic sentence (a cited claim, the writer's own claim, a definition or the aim) and continues with cited evidence. It ends on the evidence itself, a specific inference, the gap or the aim.
- **Paragraphs end on substance.** Generic value sentences ("…highlights the importance of…", "…provides valuable insights…", "…plays a crucial role…", "more research is needed") end:
  - 1.8% of Introduction paragraphs;
  - 1.3% of review paragraphs;
  - 3% of Discussion paragraphs;
  - 6% of implication paragraphs.

  The conclusion is the only unit where they are common (26% of its paragraphs). One evaluative sentence may close the paper; 13 of 50 articles end that way.
- Paragraphs run about 140 words (published range 83–228). Split a paragraph that grows past about 230 words; merge paragraphs shorter than about 75 words into a neighbour.

## 2. Using the literature

- **Claim first, citation at the end of the clause.** Write "[claim] ([Author], [year]; [Author], [year])".
  - About 57% of Introduction sentences and 60% of review sentences carry a citation: about 23 citations per 1,000 words in both.
  - An Introduction is not uncited background.
- **Few "Author (year) found" sentences, depending on design.** Narrative citations are 0% of Introduction citations and 14% of review citations (qualitative 24%).
  - **In the Discussion:** quantitative papers almost never use them (median 2.5%); qualitative papers use them for about a third (33%), mixed-methods papers for about a sixth (16%).
  - **When to name the author:** keep narrative citations for studies whose design is itself the evidence, such as one experiment, a meta-analysis or a contrary result.
  - **In a quantitative Discussion, compare information-prominently:** "This is consistent with earlier studies of [population] (A, year; B, year)", not a string of "A (year) found …; B (year) showed …".
- **Synthesise, then illustrate.** In reviews, synthesis sentences (claims across studies or about the state of the field) outnumber single-study sentences about 2:1 (63% synthesis).
  1. Open the paragraph with the synthesis: "Studies consistently show [X] (A; B; C)".
  2. Then give one or two studies whose design matters, in the past tense with the author named: "[Author] ([year]) found that [result] in [sample]".
  3. Then say what follows for this study.

  Do not walk through studies one per sentence. Repeated listing occurs in 16 of 50 reviews, mostly qualitative ones, and is not a model.
- **Tense.** Established claims with a parenthetical citation take the present or present perfect (about 90%). The verb after "Author (year)" is past in 60% of cases; *found* is the most common.
- **Bundles.** Put three or more works in one parenthesis for consensus claims only (about one parenthesis in ten in reviews, one in six in Introductions).
- **Counter-evidence.** Quantitative reviews almost always give contrary or mixed evidence (21/22); qualitative (8/15) and mixed-methods (7/13) reviews do so where the literature is contested. Devices:
  - "findings on [X] are mixed: [A] reported …, whereas [B] …";
  - "although [consensus] (cites), [study] found [exception]";
  - "However, [Author] ([year]) showed …".
- **Gap.** State the gap as a specific absence plus a contrast, a design weakness or a practical problem, and restate it before the aims. Published papers make a median of 4 gap statements, and 30 of 49 restate the gap at the end of the review. Templates:
  - "Most studies have [examined A in B]; [C] remains [unexamined]."
  - "Prior studies relied on [design or measure], which cannot [show X]."

  Make each gap match a research question.
- **Use only verified sources.** Use only references that have been verified (see [reference verification](reference-verification.md)). When the materials give few references, cite them where they support a claim and bundle them where several support the same claim; never invent citations to reach a density. A thin reference list is an author to-do item.
- **Each citation carries its claim.** Cite a work only for what it reports or argues, as far as its abstract or the materials show.
  - Do not attach a real source to a background claim it does not make, for example a study of consequences cited for how common a practice is.
  - Do not cite a work that does no work in the argument.
  - Use a study as support only when its finding points the way you argue; a reverse or reciprocal effect is not evidence for the forward one.
  - The published densities come from papers with 50–80 references. With a small reference pack, a lower density is correct. Stretching citations to reach the median is a more serious fault than citing too little.
  - **Problem entries in a reference pack:**
    - an abstract that belongs to another paper;
    - an entry with a title only;
    - an author list cut short with "et al.";
    - an online year that differs from the issue year.

    Put each one on the author to-do list. Until it is checked, cite the work only for the central claim stated in its title, or for a canonical claim the field attributes to it. Never cite details from a mismatched abstract.
- **Introduction and review.** Do not repeat the same sentence or the same citation group in both. The Introduction gives the point and its citations; definitions, detailed evidence and examples belong in the review.

## 3. Where the numbers go

- **Results.** See §7 for the design-specific openings.
  - Statistics follow the claim, in parentheses or set off by commas.
  - A quantitative Results sentence with numbers carries a median of 2.
  - About 3% of quantitative Results paragraphs open with a number.
- **Discussion and conclusion.** Restate findings in words: 98% of Discussion points restate the finding, 9% with a statistic, and only about 3% of Discussion sentences contain a number. Keep at most one effect size per point.

## 4. What stays out of the paper

- **Proposed analyses.** Sentences like "this could be tested with the present data" are notes, not prose. Either run the analysis and report it, or move the test to future research.
- **Roadmaps and cross-references.** 32 of 50 articles have no signposting, and 7 give a short roadmap at the end of the Introduction. Do not write "In this section, we …", "As noted above" or "The remainder of this paper is organised as follows" elsewhere.
- **Writing notes.** Missing sources, items to verify and term choices go to `rationale.md` and the author to-do list.

## 5. Sentences

- **Length.** Aim for a median of about 25–27 words; the published median is 27 (22–33). Keep sentences over 40 words to about 1 in 7 (15% published; every article has at least 5%). Results sentences are shortest (22) and Introduction sentences longest (30.5). Too short is also a departure: when fixing long sentences, do not chop the text into 15-word sentences. A median near 20 and almost no sentence over 40 words reads as clipped.
- **First person.** Use *we* for the study's actions and decisions: 4.8 per 1,000 words. It is most frequent in the present-study section (9.5), limitations (9.3) and Discussion (6.1); review prose rarely uses it (1.2). Impersonal prose is also within range (8 of 50 articles stay below 1 per 1,000).
- **Passive.** About a quarter of sentences use the passive (24%, range 10–46%). The measure counts *was related to* and *was associated with*, which survey designs need (see voice-en). A high figure in a short abstract is therefore normal. Across the argued sections, change passives that hide who acted (*it was decided*, *data were collected by us*), not the relational verbs.
- **Connectives.** *However* is the main contrast word (about 14 per 10,000 words). *Furthermore*, *moreover*, *additionally* and *in addition* together run at about 13 per 10,000, about five in a 4,000-word text (qualitative papers 8). About one paragraph in six opens with a connective. Vary them; do not open consecutive paragraphs with them.
- **AI-typical words.** These words already occur in published papers, but thinly:

  | Words | Published | Use |
  |---|---|---|
  | *delve*, *in today's*, *pave the way*, *a growing body of*, *it is worth noting*, *noteworthy*, *realm*, *paramount*, *in conclusion* | 0–3 occurrences per paper in total | avoid |
  | *crucial*, *highlight*, *foster*, *essential*, *insights*, *notably*, *underscore*, *pivotal*, *nuanced*, *multifaceted*, *leverage*, *navigate*, *robust*, *comprehensive*, *shed light on*, *play a crucial role* | median about 18 per 10,000 words for the whole set | use the plain word (*important* → say why; *highlight* → *show*; *foster* → *support*) |

- **Abbreviations.** Abbreviate only recurring core constructs and define each once (10 abbreviation tokens per 1,000 words). When a core construct's established name is itself an abbreviation (*GenAI*), keep it per the term table even if the density rises. Cut the other abbreviations instead, such as model labels like TS, SE and EN in prose.

## 6. Headings and lists

- **Discussion.** Discussion subheadings are absent in 23 of 50 articles and topic labels in 21 ("Teacher support and engagement"). Claim headings appear in only 4. Unlike Chinese articles, do not phrase English Discussion subheadings as claims; put the claim in the first sentence of the point.
- **Implications.** Write implications as prose paragraphs; only 4 of 50 articles number them.
- **Limitations.** In quantitative papers, number them "First, … Second, …" when there are several (12/22).

## 7. By design

| | Quantitative (22) | Qualitative (15) | Mixed methods (13) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Questions and hypotheses | Hypotheses (19/22), derived from theory plus evidence and usually stated in a present-study section; first-person aim (17/21) | Numbered RQs (11/15) at the end of the Introduction (13/15); no hypotheses; a present-study section is rarely used (2/15) | Numbered RQs (10/13), usually in a present-study section (9/13) |
| Review | Counter-evidence almost always (21/22) | Narrative citations up to about a quarter; still synthesise rather than list | As quantitative, with counter-evidence where the literature is contested |
| Results paragraphs | Open with the procedure, the finding in words, a table pointer or a check (procedure 32%, finding 27%); 56% of sentences carry numbers | Open with a theme claim (62%), then quotes with participant codes (21% open with a quote); numbers in about 12% of sentences | Alternate theme-led and procedure- or finding-led paragraphs; numbers in about 42% of sentences |
| Discussion | About half the points deal with null or contrary results; compare information-prominently | Narrative citations about a third; points may end on an implication (41% contain one) | Implications inside about a fifth of points |
| Limitations and conclusion | Median 5 limitations, numbered | Median 2 limitations; always write a conclusion (15/15); keep generic closings rare | As quantitative |

## 8. Check

After drafting, run `python <this skill>/scripts/check_prose.py --draft <manuscript>`. It detects English and reports, section by section, the measures above against the published medians:
- citation density, cited sentences, narrative and bundled citations;
- numbers in the Discussion and Results;
- sentence and paragraph length, *we*, passive voice and abbreviations;
- connectives, paragraph-final value sentences, signposting and AI-typical words.

A draft that states hypotheses is treated as quantitative for the Discussion's narrative citations.

Flags fire only beyond the published extremes. A value far from the median but inside the range still deserves a reread, especially:
- cited sentences in the Introduction and review;
- narrative citations in the Discussion, compared with the design's median.
