# How English SSCI articles are written: paragraphs and sentences

Figures come from 31 empirical articles published 2024–2026 (repository `distill/results/writing_moves_en.md`). Of these, 16 are from AERJ, Journal of Educational Psychology, Contemporary Educational Psychology, Learning and Instruction, Computers & Education, Educational Researcher, Higher Education, Studies in Higher Education, BJET, JLS and Psychology of Music. The other 15 are from Computers & Education, BJET, ETR&D, IJETHE, Education and Information Technologies, TATE, IJME and Music Education Research. Where the two groups differ, write to the first group. Figures are medians unless stated.

[voice-en](voice-en.md) covers stance: where to state and where to hedge. This file covers how paragraphs move, how literature is used and how sentences are built. Read both.

## 1. Each paragraph completes its own argument

- A paragraph makes one claim. It opens with a topic sentence (a cited claim, the writer's own claim, a definition or the aim) and continues with cited evidence. It ends on the evidence itself, a specific inference, the gap or the aim.
- **Paragraphs end on substance.** Generic value sentences ("…highlights the importance of…", "…provides valuable insights…", "…plays a crucial role…", "more research is needed") end:
  - 1.5% of Introduction paragraphs;
  - 2% of review paragraphs;
  - 3% of Discussion paragraphs;
  - 7% of implication paragraphs.

  The conclusion is the only unit where they are common (27% of its paragraphs). If you want one evaluative closing sentence, it goes at the very end of the paper.
- Paragraphs run about 130 words (published range 94–207). Split a paragraph that grows past about 230 words; merge paragraphs shorter than about 75 words into a neighbour.

## 2. Using the literature

- **Claim first, citation at the end of the clause.** Write "[claim] ([Author], [year]; [Author], [year])".
  - About 57% of Introduction sentences and 60% of review sentences carry a citation: 23 and 25 citations per 1,000 words.
  - An Introduction is not uncited background.
- **Few "Author (year) found" sentences.** Narrative citations are 0% of Introduction citations, 12% of review citations and about 7–10% of Discussion citations. Keep them for studies whose design is itself the evidence: one experiment, a meta-analysis or a contrary result. In the Discussion, compare information-prominently as well: "This is consistent with earlier studies of [population] (A, year; B, year)", not a string of "A (year) found …; B (year) showed …".
- **Synthesise, then illustrate.** In reviews, synthesis sentences (claims across studies or about the state of the field) outnumber single-study sentences about 2:1.
  - Open the paragraph with the synthesis: "Studies consistently show [X] (A; B; C)".
  - Then give one or two studies whose design matters, in the past tense with the author named: "[Author] ([year]) found that [result] in [sample]".
  - Then say what follows for this study.
  - Do not walk through studies one per sentence. A run of three single-study sentences appears at most about once per review.
- **Tense.** Established claims with a parenthetical citation take the present or the present perfect (about 90%). The verb after "Author (year)" is past in 67% of cases; *found* is the most common.
- **Bundles.** Put three or more works in one parenthesis for consensus claims only (10% of review parentheses in the top-tier group, 18% in the other).
- **Counter-evidence.** 23 of 31 reviews give at least one contrary or mixed finding, median 2:
  - "findings on [X] are mixed: [A] reported …, whereas [B] …";
  - "although [consensus] (cites), [study] found [exception]";
  - "However, [Author] ([year]) showed …".
- **Gap.** State the gap more than once: median 5 gap statements, 31/31 in the Introduction, restated at the end of the review in 20/31. Combine an absence with a specific contrast or design weakness rather than resting on "few studies" alone:
  - "Most studies have [examined A in B]; [C] remains [unexamined]."
  - "Prior studies relied on [design or measure], which cannot [show X]."

  Make each gap match a research question.
- **Use only verified sources.** Use only references that have been verified (see [reference verification](reference-verification.md)). When the materials give few references, cite them where they support a claim and bundle them where several support the same claim; never invent citations to reach a density. A thin reference list is an author to-do item.
- **Each citation carries its claim.** Cite a work only for what it reports or argues, as far as its abstract or the materials show.
  - Do not attach a real source to a background claim it does not make, for example a study of consequences cited for how common a practice is.
  - Do not cite a work that does no work in the argument.
  - Use a study as support only when its finding points the way you argue; a reverse or reciprocal effect is not evidence for the forward one.
  - The published densities come from papers with 50–80 references. With a small reference pack, a lower density is correct. Stretching citations to reach the median is a more serious fault than citing too little.

## 3. Where the numbers go

- **Results.** Open a paragraph with the analysis step ("To test H1, we …", 30%) or the finding in words (27%); 4% open with a number. Statistics follow the claim, in parentheses or set off by commas. A sentence with numbers carries a median of 2.
- **Discussion and conclusion.** Restate findings in words: 98% of Discussion points restate the finding, 88% without any statistic, and only 4% of Discussion sentences contain a number. Keep at most one effect size per point.

## 4. What stays out of the paper

- **Proposed analyses.** Sentences like "this could be tested with the present data" are notes, not prose. Either run the analysis and report it, or move the test to future research.
- **Roadmaps and cross-references.** 19 of 31 articles have no signposting, and 4 give a short roadmap at the end of the Introduction. Do not write "In this section, we …", "As noted above" or "The remainder of this paper is organised as follows" elsewhere.
- **Writing notes.** Missing sources, items to verify and term choices go to `rationale.md` and the author to-do list.

## 5. Sentences

- **Length.** Aim for a median of about 25 words; the published median is 26.5 (22–32), and 25 in the top-tier group. Keep sentences over 40 words to about 1 in 8 (top tier 12%, all 15%). Results sentences are shortest (22) and Introduction sentences longest (29.5).
- **First person.** Use *we* for the study's actions and decisions: 4.8 per 1,000 words, or 6.9 in the top tier. It is most frequent in the present-study section (9.7), limitations (8.9) and Discussion (6.4); review prose rarely uses it (1.3).
- **Passive.** About a quarter of sentences use the passive (24%, range 10–45%).
- **Connectives.** *However* is the main contrast word (13 per 10,000 words). *Furthermore*, *moreover*, *additionally* and *in addition* together run at about 15 per 10,000, about six in a 4,000-word text. About one paragraph in five opens with a connective. Vary them; do not open consecutive paragraphs with them.
- **AI-typical words.** These words already occur in published papers, but thinly:

  | Words | Published | Use |
  |---|---|---|
  | *delve*, *in today's*, *pave the way*, *a growing body of*, *it is worth noting*, *noteworthy*, *realm*, *paramount*, *in conclusion* | median 1 occurrence per paper in total, max 3 | avoid |
  | *crucial*, *highlight*, *foster*, *essential*, *insights*, *notably*, *underscore*, *pivotal*, *nuanced*, *multifaceted*, *leverage*, *navigate*, *robust*, *comprehensive*, *shed light on*, *play a crucial role* | median about 20 per 10,000 words for the whole set | use the plain word (*important* → say why; *highlight* → *show*; *foster* → *support*) |

- **Abbreviations.** Abbreviate only recurring core constructs and define each once (15 abbreviation tokens per 1,000 words; 9 in the top tier).

## 6. Headings and lists

- **Discussion.** Discussion subheadings are absent in 15 of 31 articles and topic labels in 13 ("Teacher support and engagement"). Claim headings appear in only 1. Unlike Chinese articles, do not phrase English Discussion subheadings as claims; put the claim in the first sentence of the point.
- **Implications.** Write implications as prose paragraphs; 28 of 31 do. Numbered or bold-headed lists appear in only 3.
- **Limitations.** Numbering limitations "First, … Second, …" inside one paragraph or section is common (11/31).

## 7. Check

After drafting, run `python <this skill>/scripts/check_prose.py --draft <manuscript>`. It detects English and reports, section by section, the measures above against the published medians:
- citation density, cited sentences, narrative and bundled citations;
- numbers in the Discussion and Results;
- sentence and paragraph length, *we*, passive voice and abbreviations;
- connectives, paragraph-final value sentences, signposting and AI-typical words.

Flags fire only beyond the published extremes. A value far from the median but inside the range still deserves a reread, especially:
- cited sentences in the Introduction and review;
- narrative citations in the Discussion.
