# Attributed secondary-writing exercise based on Ruth Unsworth's *Teaching through the cloud: An ethnography of the role of cloud-based collaborative technologies in the formation of teachers' classroom practices* (2023), https://doi.org/10.1111/aeq.12471

At Highland School, cloud-based documents participated in forming classroom practice by connecting teachers' planning agreements with instructional routines and pupils' contributions. School leaders expected weekly plans in Google Docs and lesson materials in Google Slides, making these technologies an obligatory passage point for collective work. Unsworth's combination of actor-network theory and literacy studies locates agency in relations among teachers, pupils, texts and institutional expectations. Creating, editing and reading documents were consequently part of how teaching took shape.

Planning made this participation visible. Teachers worked at separate computers with the same slides open, speaking toward their screens while editing together. When Frances proposed using real fruit and inserted fruit pictures, Benny typed that children would cut it; Frances changed the actor to the teacher. The proposed activity developed through this exchange of speech, images and edits. The shared slide was involved in negotiating the agreement that it would subsequently carry into teaching. Frances valued images because they helped colleagues show what they meant, so this organization around screens also supported communication. The document's participation was compatible with teachers actively proposing and revising the lesson.

The shared deck then connected planning to instruction through what Unsworth calls delegation. In the early-years classrooms, Jamie used slides for number-line activities, while Meri could be heard through the connecting rooms conducting the same lesson with the same instructional language. Their classes were at slightly different points. This combination of shared wording and varied pace shows how the deck carried a common instructional arrangement across classrooms while teachers continued to enact it locally. Standardization emerged through the continuing use of documents produced collectively under school expectations.

Documents also organized how pupils entered classroom discussion. In Laurie's lesson, pupils accessed slides on laptops, while he read their work, typed comments and displayed selected contributions. These practices made pupils' ideas available for feedback and shared attention, but organized that availability around typing and the preplanned visuals. Unsworth interprets the documents as opening opportunities for pupils to have a say while setting the parameters of discussion, with agency favoring the visuals prepared in advance. The educational significance lies in this simultaneous opening and direction of participation. Across planning and lessons, collaborative documents helped negotiate what would be taught, carry those decisions between classrooms and shape the forms in which pupils could contribute. Their influence depended on the people and institutional arrangements that sustained their use.
