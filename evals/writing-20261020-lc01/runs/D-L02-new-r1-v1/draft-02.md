# Attribution: Secondary-writing exercise based on Ruth Unsworth's *Teaching through the cloud* (2023)

In Unsworth's 2018 ethnography at Highland School, cloud-based documents helped form classroom practice through connections between planning, teaching and pupil participation. School leaders expected weekly plans in Google Docs and lesson content in Google Slides, establishing an obligatory passage point in teachers' work. The author's combination of actor-network theory and literacy studies locates agency in associations among people, texts, technologies and institutional arrangements. From this perspective, editing and reading shared documents helped organize what teachers and pupils did.

The mathematics-planning episode shows how a document participated in the formation of an agreement. Working at separate computers, Frances and Benny spoke toward their screens while editing the same material. Frances added fruit pictures and proposed using real fruit; when Benny typed that children would cut it, she changed the actor to the teacher. Their spoken exchange and visible edits jointly developed the proposed activity. In her interview, Frances valued images because they helped colleagues show what they meant. Her positive account coexisted with the author's analysis of collaboration organized around separate screens. The slide gave proposals a visible form that colleagues could encounter and revise as their agreement developed.

A separate early-years episode shows how shared documents carried planning agreements into instruction. While observing Jamie's number-line lesson, the researcher heard Meri using the same instructional language in the connected classroom. Both teachers used the shared deck, with their classes at slightly different points. Unsworth interprets the continued organization of teaching through the deck as delegation: agreements made outside the classroom remained active in the lesson through the document. The difference in pace shows how common content coexisted with variation in its enactment. The deck helped coordinate teaching across classrooms while remaining bound up with teachers' use of it.

Pupil participation brought a different relationship with prepared material into view. In Laurie's classroom, pupils accessed slides on laptops while he viewed their work, typed comments and displayed selected contributions for discussion. Unsworth interprets these literacy practices as foregrounding typing, with little spoken interaction outside the document spaces. Pupils gained opportunities to contribute, while the preplanned visuals set the parameters of discussion and weighted agency in their favour. The comparison with planning clarifies this relationship: images helped teachers make proposals negotiable, whereas prepared slides organized the terms of pupils' contributions. The documents' participation in classroom practice took shape through these changing associations. Teachers' continued use sustained relations through which shared documents coordinated instruction and gave preplanned content a structuring role in participation.
