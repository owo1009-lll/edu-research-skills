# Attribution: Secondary-writing exercise based on Ruth Unsworth's *Teaching through the cloud* (2023)

At Highland School, cloud-based documents participated in forming classroom practice by connecting the negotiation of lessons with their subsequent enactment. School leaders expected teachers to prepare weekly plans in Google Docs and lesson content in Google Slides, making shared documents an obligatory passage point in collective work. Through the author's combination of actor-network theory and literacy studies, agency takes shape in relationships among teachers, pupils, texts and institutional arrangements. Reading, editing and discussing documents were therefore activities through which teaching was organized.

The mathematics-planning episode shows how a document entered the agreement itself. Working at separate computers, Frances and Benny spoke toward their screens while editing the same material. Frances added fruit pictures and proposed using real fruit; when Benny typed that children would cut it, she changed the actor to the teacher. The visible wording and spoken exchange together developed the activity that would become available for teaching. Frances's interview account also valued images because they helped colleagues show what they meant. Her positive assessment coexisted with a spatial arrangement that centred collaboration on screens. In this episode, the shared slide provided a site where teachers' intentions were expressed, encountered and revised.

Shared documents also carried these agreements across classroom boundaries. During number-line activities, Jamie and Meri used the same deck and instructional language in connected early-years classrooms, although their classes proceeded at slightly different points. Unsworth interprets this continuation of planning agreements as delegation: decisions made outside the lesson remained active through the documents used in teaching. The difference in pace matters because shared content organized instruction without making every aspect of classroom activity identical. Standardization emerged through the repeated use of the deck alongside teachers' enactments of it.

The organization of pupil contributions extended this pattern. In Laurie's classroom, pupils accessed slides on laptops while he viewed their work, typed comments and displayed selected contributions for discussion. These practices offered pupils opportunities to enter ideas into a shared space, but they also gave the preplanned visuals a role in setting the parameters of participation. Unsworth's account foregrounds typing and describes little spoken interaction beyond these document spaces. Participation thus developed through the relations between pupils, teacher and planned texts: opportunities to contribute were accompanied by an organization of what could be discussed and how. Across planning and lessons, the documents helped form classroom practice as teachers and pupils continued to use them, with agency distributed through these relations and weighted toward the preplanned visuals in the observed pupil activity.
