# Attributed secondary-writing exercise based on Ruth Unsworth's *Teaching through the cloud* (2023)

Cloud documents at Highland School connected the negotiation of lessons with the organization of classroom participation. Leaders seeking greater standardization expected teachers to prepare weekly plans in Google Docs and lesson content in Google Slides, making these technologies an obligatory passage point in their work. Unsworth's combination of actor-network theory and literacy studies locates agency in associations among people, texts, technologies, places and institutional arrangements. Reading, editing and discussing shared documents were practices through which these relations took shape.

During mathematics planning, Frances added fruit images to a shared deck and proposed using real fruit. Benny typed that children would cut it; Frances changed the actor to the teacher. Spoken contributions and visible edits jointly developed the activity, giving the document a part in forming the agreement. Teachers worked at separate computers, orienting discussion toward screens while editing the same files. Frances valued images as a way for colleagues to show what they meant. Her positive account complements the author's relational interpretation: collaboration developed through the combined activity of speaking, viewing and editing, with the document helping to organize the encounter.

In a separate early-years episode, a shared deck carried planning agreements into classroom instruction. Jamie used it for number-line activities, while the researcher heard Meri use the same instructional language in the connected classroom. Their classes were at slightly different points. Unsworth's concept of delegation explains how the deck allowed agreements made outside the classroom to continue organizing instruction within it. The combination of common wording and different pacing specifies the standardization at work: the shared text sustained a common instructional pattern through teachers' situated enactment of the lesson.

The organization of pupil participation further develops this account. In Laurie's lesson, pupils accessed slides on laptops, he typed comments into their online work, and selected contributions were displayed for discussion. The documents opened opportunities to contribute while setting the parameters of that contribution. Unsworth interprets the predominance of typing and the limited spoken interaction outside those spaces as placing agency in favor of the preplanned visuals. Images that helped teachers negotiate lesson content thus also framed the space in which pupils' ideas were expressed and discussed. Across these episodes, collaborative participation and standardization were produced through connected literacy practices. Documents gained influence through continued teacher participation, enabling collective work while carrying earlier decisions into what pupils were invited to do.
