# Attributed secondary-writing exercise: Ruth Unsworth, *Teaching through the cloud* (2023)

At Highland School, cloud documents participated in forming classroom practices through the relationships they sustained between planning, teaching and pupil participation. School leaders expected teachers to prepare weekly plans in Google Docs and lesson content in Google Slides, making these technologies an obligatory passage point in teachers' work. Unsworth's combination of actor-network theory and literacy studies locates agency in associations among people, texts and technologies. This perspective makes the everyday work of creating, editing and reading documents central to explaining how lessons took shape.

During mathematics planning, Frances added fruit images to a shared deck and proposed using real fruit. Benny typed that children would cut it; Frances changed the actor to the teacher. Spoken contributions and visible edits jointly developed the activity, so the document participated in establishing the agreement. Teachers worked at separate computers with the same documents open, orienting their discussion toward screens. Frances valued the images because colleagues could show one another what they meant. Her positive account helps explain the appeal of this arrangement, while the observed episode shows how collaborative decisions were worked out through the document itself.

The shared deck also carried agreements into instruction. Jamie used it for number-line activities, while the researcher heard Meri use the same instructional language in the connected classroom. Their classes were at slightly different points, combining common lesson content with differences in pace. Unsworth's concept of delegation explains this continuity: the deck gave decisions made during planning a continuing role in organizing classroom activity. The documents' participation therefore extended beyond the teachers who initially produced them, linking collective planning to subsequent encounters with pupils. Standardization arose through these continuing connections between the shared text and its classroom use.

Pupil participation was organized through related literacy practices. In Laurie's lesson, pupils accessed slides on laptops, he typed comments into their online work, and selected contributions were displayed for discussion. These arrangements opened a space for pupils to contribute while directing their ideas through the prepared documents. Unsworth interprets the predominance of typing and the limited spoken interaction outside those spaces as placing agency in favor of the preplanned visuals. The same documents that supported contributions thus set the terms on which those contributions entered classroom discussion. Within this account, collaboration and standardization were closely connected: teachers and pupils participated through texts that also organized their activity, with the documents' influence sustained through continued use.
