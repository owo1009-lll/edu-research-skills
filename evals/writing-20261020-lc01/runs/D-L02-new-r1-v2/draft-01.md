# Secondary-writing discussion based on Ruth Unsworth (2023)

At Highland School, cloud-based documents participated in classroom practice by connecting collaborative planning, shared instruction and pupil contributions. Unsworth's combination of actor-network theory and literacy studies, ANT(LS), locates agency in associations among teachers, pupils, texts, technologies and institutional arrangements. Leaders' expectations that teachers prepare weekly plans in Google Docs and lesson content in Google Slides made these documents recurring points through which teaching was organized. Their role developed through the practices of creating, editing, reading and discussing them.

The mathematics-planning episode shows how an activity took shape through talk and visible edits. Frances inserted fruit pictures and proposed using real fruit; Benny typed that children would cut it, and Frances changed the actor to the teacher. The document participated in reaching agreement as colleagues spoke toward their screens and edited the shared material. Frances valued the images because they helped colleagues show what they meant. Her account gives this arrangement a collaborative value, while the observed seating at separate computers shows how that collaboration was organized spatially around screens.

Shared content also connected resource selection to subsequent instruction. Teachers compared online resources and chose material for their slides, although the circumstances of its production generally stayed outside their discussion. Unsworth's concept of blackboxing explains how these resources circulated as available teaching options. Once assembled, the documents carried agreements into classrooms. Jamie and Meri used the same early-years number-line slides and instructional language, although their classes progressed at slightly different rates. Delegation describes how the deck allowed planning agreements to organize teaching beyond the meeting in which they were made, with shared content accommodating variation in pace.

Google Classroom extended this organization to pupils' contributions. In Laurie's lesson, pupils accessed slides on laptops while he viewed their work, typed comments and displayed selected examples for discussion. The documents provided a space for pupils' ideas, but they also set the parameters within which those ideas were expressed. Unsworth interprets the foregrounding of typing and the limited spoken interaction outside the documents as placing agency in favor of preplanned visuals. Participation opportunities therefore developed within an arrangement already shaped by the shared material.

Across these episodes, the documents' role depended on teachers repeatedly enrolling them in their work. Their agency was relational: leadership expectations, planning conversations, shared texts and classroom exchanges worked together to organize what teachers and pupils did. Collaboration and standardization were connected through the same literacy practices, as agreements made on screen became resources for coordinating lessons and structuring participation.
