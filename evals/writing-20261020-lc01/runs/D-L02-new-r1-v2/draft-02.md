# Secondary-writing discussion based on Ruth Unsworth (2023)

At Highland School, a primary school in northern England, cloud-based documents helped form classroom practices through their creation, circulation and use. Unsworth's 2018 ethnography approaches these relations through actor-network theory combined with literacy studies, ANT(LS). Leaders' expectations for weekly plans in Google Docs and lesson content in Google Slides made the technologies an obligatory passage point in teachers' work. Documentary agency took shape within institutional expectations and the literacy practices through which teachers enacted them.

In mathematics planning, Frances added fruit pictures and proposed an activity using real fruit. Benny typed that children would cut it; Frances changed the actor to the teacher. Talk, images and visible edits jointly settled the proposed activity and who would perform it. The document participated in forming the agreement. Frances valued images for helping colleagues show what they meant, while their simultaneous editing at separate computers organized collaboration around screens. Her positive account and the spatial arrangement together show how collaboration was accomplished through relations among colleagues, images and documents.

The shared material also connected resource selection with instruction. Teachers compared online resources and selected content, while its production circumstances generally remained outside their discussions. Unsworth describes this obscuring of production circumstances as blackboxing: resources circulated as available teaching options. Documents then carried planning agreements into lessons. In connected early-years classrooms, Jamie and Meri used the same number-line slides and instructional language, with their classes at slightly different points. Delegation explains how the deck sustained the organizing force of agreements beyond the planning meeting. Standardized content coexisted with differences in classroom pace.

In Laurie's lesson, pupils accessed slides through Google Classroom on laptops while he viewed their work, typed comments and displayed selected examples for discussion. The documents opened a space for pupils' contributions while setting the parameters within which those contributions were made. Unsworth interprets the foregrounding of typing, with little spoken interaction outside the online spaces, as placing agency in favor of preplanned visuals. Pupils' opportunities to express ideas therefore belonged to an arrangement in which existing images and texts organized the discussion. Their participation and the authority of planned content developed together.

These episodes give documentary agency different forms: teachers negotiated an activity through edits during planning, while classroom documents carried agreements forward and organized pupils' contributions. Those roles depended on teachers continuing to enroll the technologies in their work. The literacy practices that supported collaboration also gave prior planning a continuing authority in classroom activity.
