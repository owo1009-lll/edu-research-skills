## Study and conceptual position (PDF pages 2–10)

The study asks how Google's cloud-based collaborative technologies participate in forming teachers' classroom practices. It combines actor-network theory and literacy studies as ANT(LS). Agency is understood through associations among people, texts, technologies, places and institutional arrangements. Literacy studies directs attention to creating, reading, editing and discussing texts. Concepts of translation, delegation and blackboxing help describe changes in those relationships.

Fieldwork ran from April to July 2018 in Highland School, a three-form-entry primary school in northern England, serving ages 3–11. The researcher was a former primary teacher entering an unfamiliar school. The wider project involved ten teacher participants followed for a week each, plus a senior leader and professional development consultant joined as schedules allowed; twelve interviews and 86 documents were collected. This article draws on eight participants' data. Observation, interviews and documents form this ethnographic account; there is no experiment or quantitative estimate of learning effects. Professional activities at home were discussed in conversations and interviews, not directly observed. The fieldwork preceded the pandemic.

The author describes a partial actor-network, rather than claiming to trace every influence. School leaders sought greater standardization and, after training, expected teachers to create weekly plans on Google Docs and lesson content on Google Slides. Year-group teachers created documents together and used the same documents in lessons. The technologies thus became an obligatory passage point in work organized by school leadership.

## Planning through a shared document (PDF pages 11–12)

Teachers sat at separate computers, with the same files and slide deck open, rather than around the available round table. They spoke toward screens and edited simultaneously while agreeing lesson content.

In an observed mathematics-planning episode, Frances added fruit pictures and proposed an activity using real fruit. Benny typed that children would cut the fruit; Frances changed the actor to the teacher. Figure 1 displays the resulting slide. Their brief spoken exchange and visible edits jointly developed the proposed activity. The author interprets the document as participating in their agreement, rather than just recording an agreement made elsewhere.

Frances valued the arrangement: in her interview she said images let colleagues show what they meant while discussing ideas. The author also describes spatially separated collaboration organized around screens. These are the participant's positive account and the author's relational interpretation, respectively. Not all lessons used the platform throughout, although all observed team-planning discussions did, and most lessons began and ended with its documents.

## Resources, choices and circulation (PDF pages 13–14)

Teachers searched for and compared online resources, selecting material for a shared deck. School leaders assembled important files or links in online folders. The production circumstances of these resources generally did not enter the planning conversation. The author uses blackboxing to explain how those circumstances become obscured while the texts circulate as available teaching options. Teachers did discuss and select among the resources; the author does not describe them as blindly accepting everything online.

Documents carried planning agreements into subsequent lessons. In the early-years classrooms, Jamie used a shared deck for number-line activities. From the connected room, the researcher heard Meri conduct the same lesson using the same instructional language, with the classes at slightly different points. The open archways explain the observer's access to this comparison. The author uses delegation to explain how agreements made outside the classroom continued to organize instruction through the deck. Shared content coexisted with some difference in pace.

## Student participation and its organization (PDF pages 14–15)

Slides displayed prompts, images and short texts; teachers and pupils annotated them during classroom talk. For pupils aged 7–11, Google Classroom further organized participation through shared online documents. In the observed episode, pupils accessed slides on laptops, Laurie viewed work on his computer, typed comments directly into the online space and displayed selected student work for discussion.

The author interprets these literacy practices as foregrounding typing, with thoughts and ideas largely entered into documents and little spoken interaction outside those spaces. The paragraph supplied in original.md says every pupil could have a say while the documents set the parameters of discussion. Its final claim places agency in favor of the preplanned visuals. This is an interpretation of organized participation; no distribution of individual speaking/typing amounts, equality measure or learning-effect estimate is reported.

The paragraph relates the observation to Colton (2019, p. 432), as the author reads it: technology can open participation while human and nonhuman elements organize what participants do. Colton's original work was not supplied or independently checked. Its quoted wording in original.md is reproduced from Unsworth's paragraph. The cited item in Unsworth's reference list is Colton, *Breaking Out, Finding, and Using Information: Theorising Learner Identities in Assemblages of Teaching and Learning with Technology*, *Technology, Pedagogy and Education* 28(4), 425–434.

## Continued use and resistance (PDF pages 16–17)

Leaders connected access to policies and preparation of school reports to the platform. The author argues that its influence depended on people enrolling it in their work and on continued teacher participation.

Alex worried that others could edit reports and considered preparing them offline; the article does not document that Alex actually did so. Separately, Jamie described pressure to check evening messages and keep up with work. These are different participants' accounts, not one person's trajectory. The discussion identifies potential burnout risk; a later conclusion calls it burnout more directly, without a diagnostic measure or clinical finding. The source does not establish burnout as an observed outcome.

The conclusion argues that collaborative technologies reorganize literacy practices and agency through sociomaterial relations. Its analysis of standardization is compatible with participants' positive uses of images and participation opportunities. Suggestions concerning post-pandemic platforms and future generative AI research are extensions, not observations from the 2018 fieldwork.
