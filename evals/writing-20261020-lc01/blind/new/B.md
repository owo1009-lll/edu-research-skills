In Unsworth's 2018 ethnography at Highland School, cloud-based documents participated in forming classroom practice through the relations they established between planning, instruction and pupil participation. Leaders expected weekly plans in Google Docs and lesson materials in Google Slides, making these technologies an obligatory passage point for collective work. Unsworth's combination of actor-network theory and literacy studies connects this institutional arrangement to the everyday creation, editing and reading of texts through which teaching took shape.

Within this arrangement, teachers negotiated lesson content through shared documents. Sitting at separate computers with the same slides open, they spoke toward their screens while editing together. Frances inserted fruit pictures and proposed using real fruit; when Benny typed that children would cut it, she changed the actor to the teacher. Speech, images and visible edits jointly developed the activity. The slide participated in the formation of agreement as teachers worked on it. Frances's positive account of images helping colleagues show what they meant identifies a communicative benefit of this organization around screens. Teachers' active proposals and revisions were part of the relations through which the document acquired its instructional significance.

Documents carried planning agreements into lessons through what Unsworth calls delegation. Jamie used a shared deck for early-years number-line activities, while the researcher heard Meri through the connected classrooms conducting the same lesson with the same instructional language. The classes were at slightly different points. This combination of common wording and varied pace shows agreements made outside the classroom continuing to organize instruction through the deck. Standardization depended on teachers taking up the shared content in their classrooms, where some variation in enactment remained.

Pupil participation was likewise opened and directed through documents. In Laurie's lesson, pupils accessed slides on laptops while he viewed their work, typed comments and displayed selected contributions for discussion. Unsworth interprets these literacy practices as foregrounding typing: ideas largely entered the documents, with little spoken interaction outside those spaces. Pupils had opportunities to contribute, while the documents established the parameters within which their contributions were discussed. Agency consequently favored the preplanned visuals in the author's account. The same relations that made ideas available for feedback and shared attention also organized their expression. Read alongside the planning and number-line episodes, this account locates the documents' educational significance in their participation in negotiating content, sustaining common instruction and structuring pupil contributions. Their influence was maintained through teachers' continued participation and the school's expectations for their use.
