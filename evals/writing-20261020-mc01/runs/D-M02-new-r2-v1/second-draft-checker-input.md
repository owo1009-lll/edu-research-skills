# Discussion

Adl-Amini and colleagues' (2024) study gives classroom substance to the distinction between arranging group work and teaching cooperation. In selected German lessons intended to use cooperative learning, only two of 30 observed teachers provided all four assessed elements; 11 provided none. Those counts locate an implementation problem even where teachers had chosen a lesson for observation. The interview comparison then brings a specific educational question into focus: whether teachers regard cooperation as a competence they should help develop or a prerequisite students should already possess.

The two teachers whose observed lessons contained all four elements described planning for cooperation, attending to students' needs, and adjusting groups or roles when difficulties arose. Reflection on group processes formed part of their accounts of learning. The two contrasting teachers treated academic goals as paramount and assumed social competence as a prerequisite. One judged the lesson successful because academic objectives had been met even though most groups had not worked together. In this case, the criterion for success allowed unaddressed difficulties in cooperation to coexist with a positive judgement of the lesson. These contrasting accounts give content to the authors' argument that responsibility for social learning belongs within implementation.

The comparison concerns four teachers selected from observed extremes within a volunteer sample, which constrains claims about the prevalence of these orientations. The observations also assessed instructional provision: an element counted as present when any of its indicators occurred, and students' social skills were not assessed directly. The evidence therefore supports a distinction in how teachers understood their work, while leaving the direction of the relationship between beliefs and practice, and its consequences for students, unresolved.

For teacher education, this distinction supports connecting knowledge of cooperative learning techniques with examination of teachers' responsibility for developing cooperation. Alongside learning to structure interdependence and individual accountability, teachers should examine what they plan to teach when students struggle to communicate and what evidence they use to judge group work. Teacher educators could connect these questions to lesson analysis, considering how role assignments support contributions and how group reflection makes cooperation an explicit focus of learning. The proposed approach would need evaluation that includes students' perspectives and actual cooperation; the study did not compare training programmes. Its contribution is to specify what such preparation should address: the decisions through which teachers make learning to cooperate part of the lesson's purpose and their own instructional work.
