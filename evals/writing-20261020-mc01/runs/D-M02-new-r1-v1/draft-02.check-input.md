# Discussion

Adl-Amini, Völlinger, and Eckart's (2024) study directs attention to what teachers understand themselves to be teaching when they organise cooperative learning. Among 30 teachers observed in German classrooms, only two provided all four assessed elements, while 11 provided none. These selected lessons expose a gap between arranging group work and providing the conditions for cooperation. The observation scores concern instructional provision: an element counted when at least one indicator appeared, and the scale did not directly assess social skills. The findings therefore identify omissions in provision without establishing their consequences for students' learning.

The comparison of four teachers' interviews gives this implementation problem an educational meaning. The two teachers whose lessons contained all four assessed elements described cooperation as a competence to develop and part of learning itself. They reported attending to students' needs and adjusting groups or roles when difficulties arose. The contrasting teachers treated social skills as a prerequisite and academic goals as paramount. One judged a lesson successful because academic goals had been reached, even though most groups had not worked together. The difference concerns which difficulties teachers regard as instructional responsibilities and which outcomes they consider when judging success. In that teacher's account, academic completion was sufficient despite the failure to cooperate.

The mixed methods contribution lies in connecting observed provision with these accounts of responsibility and success. Observation scores guided the selection of interviews already conducted immediately after lessons. This comparison identifies a distinction in teachers' understandings; it does not establish that changing those understandings improves practice. Voluntary participation, teacher-selected lessons and the four extreme cases restrict claims about the prevalence of these patterns. Examining students' perspectives and their actual cooperation would extend the account beyond the instructional arrangements captured by the observation scale.

For teacher education, the contrast supports addressing responsibility for social learning alongside knowledge of cooperative techniques. Teacher educators should ask prospective and practising teachers to examine what they planned for cooperation, what they noticed during group work and how they responded when it faltered. Planning tasks could connect members' interdependence and individual accountability with decisions about grouping, roles and reflection. Lesson review should consider students' cooperation alongside academic completion. The study provides grounds for this emphasis, although it did not compare training programmes. Its contribution is to make teachers' responsibility for developing cooperation concrete in the planning, support and evaluation of classroom learning.
