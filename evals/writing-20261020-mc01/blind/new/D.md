Adl-Amini, Völlinger, and Eckart (2024) sharpen the distinction between arranging group work and teaching students to cooperate. Only two of 30 observed teachers in their German study had lessons containing all four assessed elements of cooperative learning; 11 had none. Teachers had chosen the observed lessons and had been asked to use cooperative learning. The implementation problem thus appeared even on these selected occasions, directing attention to the arrangements for interdependence, accountability, interaction, and reflection within a group task.

Observation scores guided the selection of four interviews for comparison, connecting contrasting instructional provision with teachers' accounts of cooperation. The two teachers whose lessons contained all four assessed elements described cooperation as a competence to develop and discussed adjusting groups or roles in response to students' needs. The two contrasting teachers treated social skills as a prerequisite and prioritized academic goals. One judged a lesson successful because academic objectives had been achieved, although most groups had not worked together. The contrast makes responsibility for social learning visible in decisions about what to plan, monitor, and revise when students struggle to work together. It also shows why academic task completion can be an insufficient criterion for evaluating a cooperative lesson.

The limits of this interpretation follow from volunteer recruitment, selected lessons, and a comparison of four extreme cases. Their contrast identifies orientations toward cooperation without establishing how beliefs and practice influence one another. The observation scale also records instructional provision: one indicator suffices for an element to count as present, while students' social skills and learning gains were not directly assessed. Understanding how this provision relates to students' actual cooperation calls for their perspectives and closer examination of group interaction.

Teacher education should therefore connect knowledge of cooperative learning techniques with examination of responsibility for social learning, as the authors propose. Where teachers treat cooperation as a prerequisite, teacher educators can use problems such as a group struggling to communicate to examine decisions about role assignments, group composition, and reflection. Trainees can consider how to respond and what evidence would indicate that cooperation is occurring. This recommendation develops the case contrast; the study did not test training approaches or demonstrate the effects of changing beliefs. Its practical contribution is to make support for cooperation an explicit criterion for planning and evaluating cooperative lessons.
