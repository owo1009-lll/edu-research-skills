Adl-Amini, Völlinger, and Eckart's study shows why classroom implementation of cooperative learning needs to be examined through teachers' purposes as well as their instructional arrangements. Only two of the 30 observed teachers provided all four assessed elements, while 11 provided none. Although teachers had been asked to use cooperative learning and the selected lessons included group work, its defining arrangements were often absent. The scores register whether at least one instructional indicator of each element occurred; they describe teachers' provision without establishing students' cooperation or learning gains. This distinction directs attention to what teachers organise within group work.

The comparison of four cases selected through observation scores explains what those differences in provision can mean. The two teachers whose lessons contained all assessed elements treated cooperation as a competence to develop. They described observing students' needs and adjusting groups or roles when difficulties arose. The contrasting teachers treated social skills as a prerequisite and prioritised academic goals. One judged the lesson successful despite most groups failing to work together. In discussing a communication difficulty, that teacher assigned the problem to students and acknowledged having omitted it from planning. Connecting these accounts to observed provision identifies a difference in what teachers regard as their instructional responsibility: cooperation itself can be an object of teaching.

This interpretation supports the authors' call to address beliefs alongside techniques in teacher education. Its scope rests on a volunteer sample, selected lessons and an extreme-case comparison. These observations offer a limited view of everyday practice, and the comparison leaves unresolved whether changing beliefs improves implementation. The observation scale also omits direct assessment of social skills. Research incorporating students' perspectives and actual cooperation would help establish how the opportunities teachers provide are experienced in groups.

For teacher educators, the practical priority is to connect planning decisions with teachers' criteria for successful cooperative learning. Because the case contrast concerns responsibility when cooperation becomes difficult, preparation should include examining how teachers plan for and respond to such difficulties. Planning activities could ask teachers to explain how individual accountability and reflection on group processes serve their learning aims, then revise group or role arrangements in response to communication problems. These are applications of the case interpretation whose effectiveness requires evaluation. Their educational rationale is to make the teaching of cooperation visible in planning and lesson appraisal, so that achieving academic goals does not by itself settle whether cooperative learning has been supported.
