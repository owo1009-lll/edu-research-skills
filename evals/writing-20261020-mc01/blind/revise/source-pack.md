# Cooperative learning implementation and teacher beliefs

This public-source pack supports an attributed writing exercise based on Adl-Amini, K., Völlinger, V. A., & Eckart, A. (2024; published online 2023), *Implementation quality of cooperative learning and teacher beliefs—a mixed methods study*, European Journal of Psychology of Education, 39, 2267–2281. [Public article](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10212-023-00769-3), DOI 10.1007/s10212-023-00769-3, [CC BY 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/). The pack paraphrases the read main text, six tables and observation-scale supplement. It is not raw data or a private manuscript. No browsing is needed or permitted in this exercise.

## Question and concepts

The authors investigate how cooperative learning (CL) is implemented in German classrooms and how teachers with different implementation quality understand it. Earlier effectiveness research, as reviewed by these authors, connects benefits to implementation. Those cited originals have not been independently read for this exercise.

CL involves students working in small groups to help one another learn. The theoretical account identifies five elements: positive interdependence, individual accountability, promotive interaction, social skills and group processing. Positive interdependence makes members need one another's contributions through resources, roles or goals; individual accountability makes each member responsible for contributing and learning. Promotive interaction involves exchanges that support learning. Group processing involves reflection on how the group worked. Group seating alone does not establish these arrangements.

The observation scale assesses four elements and does not assess students' social skills directly. Eleven instructional indicators cover these four elements. Observers count an element as present when at least one of its indicators occurs; a maximum score therefore does not establish every aspect of each element or student learning gains.

## Design and correspondence of sources

The study took place in 2018–2020 through courses associated with two German universities. Participation was voluntary, with teachers mostly recruited by student teachers. Forty-nine teachers completed the questionnaire. Thirty teachers' CL lessons were observed: 25 classroom observations and five existing video recordings. Sixteen teachers had questionnaire, observation and interview data. These are overlapping, differently sized samples, not 49 complete cases across all sources.

Teachers chose the subject and time for observation and were asked to use CL. Lessons without a group-work phase were excluded. The questionnaire asks about implementation in teachers' typical practice, including indicators of five theoretical elements. The observation records instructional indicators in a selected lesson, covering four elements. Their reference periods and operational coverage differ.

Semi-structured interviews occurred immediately after the observed lesson, asking about practice, difficulties, what teachers did to make CL work and satisfaction with that lesson. Observation scores subsequently guided selection for qualitative comparison: the two teachers whose lessons contained all four assessed elements and two teachers whose lessons contained none. Analysis compared their interviews. The sequence concerns analytic selection; interviews were not newly collected after completing quantitative analysis.

The four cases span primary and secondary settings. The article calls them matched by subject and grade, but describes a secondary pair from mathematics and geography in Grades 10 and 12. Do not describe the cases as an exact same-subject/same-grade match or as randomized groups.

## Findings available for writing

Only two of the 30 observed teachers implemented all four assessed elements; 11 implemented none. Structured group reflection was observed in nine lessons. The article contains an inconsistent count for those implementing some elements: the prose says 12, whereas its listed categories are 5, 6 and 6. The two endpoint counts are consistent; do not silently choose a replacement for the inconsistent partial-implementation total.

Questionnaire analyses report associations among teachers' self-efficacy, their evaluations of CL's suitability and their self-reported implementation quality. These are survey relationships, not demonstrated relationships with observed implementation or student learning. Exact coefficients are not needed for this task.

The four interview cases differ in the value assigned to social learning and responsibility for cooperation. The two teachers whose observed lessons contained all four assessed elements described cooperation as a competence to develop and part of learning itself. They emphasized exchange, members' strengths and reflection on group processes. They described planning, observing students' needs, and changing groups or role assignments when difficulties occurred.

The contrasting teachers treated academic goals as paramount and social skills more as a prerequisite. They did not devote time to developing those skills or reflecting on group processes. One judged the observed lesson successful because academic goals had been reached, even though most groups had not worked together. In discussing a group unable to communicate, that teacher attributed the problem to the students and acknowledged not having incorporated it into planning. These are case-specific observations and reported perspectives; the study does not follow changes in beliefs or experimentally manipulate them.

## The published paragraph's context

Discussion paragraph B068, supplied separately as original.md, considers the difference between self-reported and observed implementation. The article reports generally higher self-ratings, then proposes limited knowledge and teachers' interpretations of responsibility as possible explanations. Most participants had not received CL training; training attendance is not a direct measure of their knowledge.

The article cites Hennessey and Dionigi (2013) in support of the relevance of teacher knowledge to assessment. That citation belongs to the published authors' review. The cited original has not been independently read for this exercise; retaining it does not mean its support has been reverified.

The four contrasting cases' individual self-reported implementation-quality scores are not displayed. Table 3 reports self-efficacy, which is a different construct. Thus the interviews can inform a possible interpretation of the source difference, but the supplied article does not show that the particular teachers who blamed students also rated their own implementation highly. Its final explanatory sentence moves beyond the displayed individual correspondence.

A footnote prints the cross-source correlation as `r = 361, p = .17, N = 16`, leaving the coefficient's decimal position unresolved. It describes no correlation, but the reported non-significance does not establish absence of a relationship. Do not supply an invented corrected coefficient. The distinct reference periods and indicator sets also matter when interpreting the two sources.

## Authorial position, implications and scope

The authors argue that teacher education should address beliefs and teachers' responsibility for promoting social learning, alongside knowledge of CL techniques. The case contrast gives content to that position: teaching cooperation and supporting it when difficulties arise are different from treating cooperation as an assumed student prerequisite. The study does not compare training programmes or demonstrate that changing beliefs causes better implementation.

Its volunteer recruitment, small extreme-case comparison and selected observed lessons constrain population claims. The observations concern teachers' instructional provision, not a comprehensive assessment of how students cooperated or how much they learned. The authors recommend future work including students' perspectives and actual cooperation. They also suggest closer examination of how teachers and observers assess CL.

Other source issues include a suitability-scale upper bound reported as 3 despite a mean of 3.10, and a claim that beliefs resist change inferred from comparison with a different trained sample. Neither exact coding nor resistance to change is independently established here. These issues remain in source notes; they need not all be recited in a short passage that does not rely on those claims.
