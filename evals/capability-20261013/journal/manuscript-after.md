# Synthetic proposal paragraph after source integration

The educational value of automated feedback depends on how students judge and act on it. Carless and Boud's (2018) account of feedback literacy connects these activities with understanding feedback and managing affect. In language learning, Li and Kim (2024) also highlight critical engagement and teacher guidance in students' use of automated feedback. Their study concerns existing automated feedback systems; it provides a process-oriented starting point for examining generative AI, rather than evidence about its effects.

Recent findings make the distinction between feedback quality, uptake and revision consequential. Banihashem et al. (2024) found different functional profiles in peer and ChatGPT feedback on argumentative essays, while leaving feedback use and subsequent revision unexamined. Studying the provision of peer feedback, Pozdniakov et al. (2026) reported limited uptake of AI suggestions for revising the feedback students were composing, despite the suggestions' generally positive, structured presentation. A randomized comparison by Weidlich et al. (2025) further found that less favourable perceptions of teacher feedback could coexist with stronger improvements in students' work. These studies concern different stages: producing feedback, responding to it, and revising work. Together, they show why favourable output or reception alone is an incomplete basis for evaluating a feedback design.

Our proposed study will examine how undergraduate writers evaluate AI-assisted peer feedback and connect those judgments to substantive revisions. Combining feedback and revision coding with stimulated recall will allow us to examine the relation between an offered suggestion, a student's decision and the resulting change in the essay. Students should retain responsibility for judging feedback. Accordingly, we will evaluate the design through students' decisions and revisions, rather than treating automated comment delivery as the educational outcome.
