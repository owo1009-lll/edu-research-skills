## Discussion

This study offers a curriculum-level account of how undergraduate medical students come to recognize the value of research through applying it. Across cohort accounts from the five-semester research module at Mohammed Bin Rashid University, unfamiliar methods gained relevance in supervised projects. The 8A-Model organizes this experience into four pairs: Attend–Acquire, Accumulate–Assimilate, Apply–Appreciate and Articulate–Affect. These pairs propose a process in which knowledge, participation and appreciation develop together. Although presented sequentially, the model allows overlapping and iterative learning, with earlier knowledge revisited as students undertake new tasks.

The accounts give substance to the proposed transition. Participant 6.1 described early biostatistics and software exercises as confusing or disconnected, whereas participants 7.2 and 8.2 recognized their relevance during project work. Students also described learning professional communication through supervisor exchanges and scrutinizing research quality and reported numbers. These examples connect the module's situated-learning rationale to participation in a community of practice: methods acquire purpose within authentic tasks supported by experienced researchers. Accounts of accomplishment and using presentation skills during internships extend the model toward articulation and reported transfer. Affect expresses the aspiration to contribute to medicine and public health.

The grade pattern provides a second perspective on this curriculum transition. Mean research-course grade point average (GPA), on a 0–4 scale, was 3.01, 2.94 and 2.95 in the first three semesters, followed by 3.35 and 3.58. The reported analyses detected an early/later course contrast, with a similar pattern within the oldest cohort, and no detected differences within either cluster. Assessment tasks changed from examinations and assignments to project-based outputs, while cohort composition also changed. The later grades therefore represent performance under different assessment conditions. The joint display aligns this pattern with cohort-level perceptions without linking individual interviewees' accounts to their own grade changes. This convergence locates application as a plausible interpretive turning point; it does not establish appreciation as the cause of higher grades.

The model's conceptual emphasis is the delayed recognition of research's value. Otaki et al. (2023) contrast this with their reading of Kirkpatrick and learning-transfer models, which they interpret as locating appreciation immediately after a learning experience. Their comparison directs attention to the time needed to find meaning in unfamiliar methods. Students in this program entered after secondary school, making gradual assimilation a plausible explanation for delayed appreciation. The framework thus invites attention to when students can apply knowledge, rather than treating their initial reactions as a complete account of its educational value.

Undergraduate medical programs should include opportunities to conduct supervised research alongside instruction in methods. The rationale rests on what authentic participation makes available: problems requiring methodological judgment, guidance on research practices and attitudes, and occasions to communicate findings. In programs able to sustain expert supervision and project opportunities, educators should connect earlier content with current research decisions and encourage analogical reasoning and reflection as students revise their mental models. The 8A-Model provides a vocabulary for examining these connections across a curriculum. Early uncertainty about relevance should not be read automatically as failure of research education.

The study's single-institution setting and cohort-based reconstruction define the scope of this interpretation. One focus group per cohort captured accounts at different curriculum stages; the same individuals were not interviewed repeatedly. Four oldest-cohort participants checked the framework, providing member feedback rather than prospective validation. Without a comparison program, the design cannot isolate the contribution of project work. Longitudinal studies linking individual accounts to comparable assessments would clarify how application, appreciation and performance develop. Research across programs should examine the framework's applicability and how supervision and curriculum structure shape these relationships. Follow-up should assess subsequent research practice, career participation, healthcare costs and patient outcomes, none of which were measured here.

## Conclusion

In this undergraduate medical curriculum, participants' accounts connect research application with developing appreciation of its value. The 8A-Model brings those accounts and course-stage grade patterns into a framework for examining how learning acquires purpose through use. Undergraduate medical programs should provide sustained, supervised research opportunities grounded in authentic participation and reflection. Longitudinal investigation should examine how the proposed process develops within individuals and whether its benefits extend to research practice beyond the module.
