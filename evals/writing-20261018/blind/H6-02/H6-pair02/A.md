Secondary writing exercise based on Kestin et al. (2025); this text reports a published study, not research conducted by the writer.

## Introduction

The instructional value of artificial intelligence (AI) depends on how its use is organized around a learning task. For teachers considering AI tutoring, a consequential question is whether it can improve students' initial learning relative to a well-taught class with active participation. In their review, Kestin et al. (2025) describe limited reflection or weaker performance in earlier ChatGPT activities, including studies by Krupp et al. (2023) and Forero et al. (2023). These reports motivate evaluating a deliberately designed tutor against an established classroom approach while distinguishing the instructional arrangements being compared.

Kestin et al. (2025) tested that comparison in Harvard's introductory physics course for life-science students. Existing peer groups were randomized in a crossover covering surface tension and fluid flow across consecutive weeks. Both conditions used the same worksheet content and objectives: the class offered live explanations, peer problem solving and instructor support, while students using the tutor worked individually at home after a recorded introduction. Expert-written solutions, question-specific prompts and programmed sequencing guided their work. The study therefore addressed whether this structured tutor package improved immediate topic learning relative to active classroom instruction.

## Discussion

The tutor package yielded higher immediate post-test performance, with reported medians of 4.5 in the AI condition and 3.5 in the classroom condition. An adjusted analysis accounting for starting performance and other measured differences also favored AI (standardized regression coefficient = 0.63). Common learning objectives and experienced, highly rated classroom instructors strengthen the educational relevance of this comparison: the tutor's advantage emerged against an established active-learning alternative.

Timely feedback and flexible pacing are plausible explanations, because the tutor combined guidance during problem solving with individual progression. Yet location, peer interaction, the recorded introduction and instructional support changed together, so the trial identifies the effect of the instructional package. Component comparisons would be needed to distinguish these explanations. This also qualifies comparisons with the less structured activities described in Kestin et al.'s (2025) review: the studies support attention to implementation, but their differing findings do not establish which design feature accounts for the contrast.

The time comparison is encouraging but uses different measures: median logged AI activity was 49 minutes, whereas the 60-minute classroom duration was assumed rather than measured for individual students. Comparable time-on-task measures would be necessary to quantify efficiency. Students also reported higher engagement and motivation with the tutor; enjoyment and confidence in learning difficult physics with effort showed no statistically detected differences. These single-item responses describe students' experience and leave broader or lasting motivational change unresolved.

For teachers, the result justifies evaluating structured tutoring for initial engagement with material, provided that expert content preparation, sequencing and feedback can be supported. The authors found prompting alone insufficient to maintain the intended sequence, making reliable delivery of the learning activity a practical design concern for institutions considering adoption. This is a resource commitment: lessons took days to prepare and platform development took months. Kestin et al. (2025) propose pairing AI-supported initial learning with higher-order collaborative classroom work. That arrangement merits a course-level trial, since the study did not evaluate the combined approach over a complete course.

With immediate assessments of two topics in one course, the evidence leaves retention, complex transfer and performance in other settings open. A course-level evaluation should examine those outcomes alongside implementation costs and access, so that decisions about integrating the tutor reflect both learning and the resources needed to sustain it.

## Conclusion

In this introductory physics course, structured AI tutoring improved immediate learning relative to an established active-learning class. The contribution is a concrete demonstration that carefully prepared tutoring can strengthen initial learning under these conditions. The next teaching decision concerns how to combine that preparation with collaborative classroom work, supported by evidence about learning over time and implementation demands.
