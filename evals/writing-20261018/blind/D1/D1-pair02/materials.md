# D1：教学模拟材料（本项目新编，不是真实研究）

All data and comparison-study cards below are fictional, supplied only for testing writing from a closed evidence set. Do not turn them into real bibliographic entries. Citation labels [R1] and [R2] refer to these supplied fictional cards. No human participant data are used.

## Study record

Question: Does a six-week low-stakes retrieval routine improve delayed conceptual transfer compared with rereading worked examples in lower-secondary science?

Three schools, eight classes each, 480 enrolled pupils. Classes were randomly allocated within each school, 12 classes per condition. Both conditions used the same content and 15 minutes of scheduled lesson time. Retrieval pupils first attempted an explanation without viewing the example, then checked a worked example. Rereading pupils studied the same worked example throughout. Both received the same teacher explanation afterward. These procedures were planned before outcomes were examined. The analysis used the class as the randomization unit and accounted for school and baseline performance. 456 pupils completed both post-tests; 12 missing pupils in each condition. This balance does not show that missingness was ignorable.

Primary outcome: conceptual transfer test three weeks after the intervention, standardised adjusted difference d = 0.08, 95% CI [-0.07, 0.23], favouring retrieval. No equivalence margin was prespecified; there is no basis for claiming equivalence. The interval includes little difference and a benefit relevant to the teaching question. Secondary outcome: immediate conceptual-transfer test d = 0.24, 95% CI [0.09, 0.39]. The difference between immediate and delayed effects was not tested.

Process records: pupils attempted an explanation before checking the example in 81% of scheduled retrieval activities. This is completion of an attempt, not its correctness or depth. Pupils in the rereading condition reported greater ease during the activity; no validated learning measure was based on this item. Teachers recorded median preparation of 10 minutes per lesson in retrieval and 6 in rereading; this descriptive comparison has no inferential test. No interview, motivation scale or mediation analysis was collected.

The authors' educational position: delayed transfer should remain the main criterion when deciding whether to replace the existing routine. The immediate result justifies investigating how to retain an early advantage, rather than claiming that retention was established. Teachers may retain the retrieval routine where its short-term learning purpose and preparation cost fit the lesson. Ease of activity should not substitute for the intended learning outcome. Do not treat the preparation difference alone as proof of which routine is more cost-effective.

## Supplied comparison cards

[R1] Fictional study, a single-session individually randomised university laboratory comparison of retrieval and rereading: delayed factual recall favoured retrieval, d = 0.35, 95% CI [0.18, 0.52]. It did not measure conceptual transfer or classroom implementation. The record supplies no evidence about pupil age as a causal moderator.

[R2] Fictional three-week school study with intact classes and teacher choice of routine: immediate conceptual performance was higher under retrieval, adjusted d = 0.19, 95% CI [0.02, 0.36]. Teachers chose their condition. No delayed test. It proposed effortful reconstruction as an explanation but did not measure a mediating process.

Conceptual frame: retrieval asks learners to reconstruct an explanation; rereading keeps the explanation available. The process record identifies an opportunity to reconstruct, not whether productive reconstruction actually occurred. An instructional activity, the experience of effort and durable learning are related questions, not interchangeable measurements.
