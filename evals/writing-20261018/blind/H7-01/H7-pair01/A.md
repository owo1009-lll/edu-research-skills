## Discussion

This study addresses how undergraduate medical students come to recognize the value of research while learning to conduct it. The 8A-Model organizes their experiences across a compulsory five-semester research module at Mohammed Bin Rashid University (MBRU): Attend–Acquire, Accumulate–Assimilate, Apply–Appreciate and Articulate–Affect. Its contribution is an account of how applying research methods can make earlier instruction meaningful. Although presented sequentially, these activities can overlap and recur as learners revisit knowledge through project work. "Affect" expresses the intended contribution of research beyond the learner, including aspirations to improve medical practice.

Students' accounts give this interpretation substance. One student described early biostatistics and software exercises as disconnected and confusing; others recognized their relevance during project work. Participants described learning to communicate with supervisors, appraise research quality and question reported numbers. Mentor support was associated in one account with asking research questions. Others associated completing a project with accomplishment and reported using presentation skills during internships. These accounts locate learning in authentic research supported by mentors and a community of practice, consistent with the module's situated-learning rationale [26, 61]. They concern reported development and transfer rather than independently assessed subsequent competence.

The course records show a corresponding distinction between earlier instruction and later projects. Mean research-course grade point averages (GPAs), on a 0–4 scale, were 3.01, 2.94 and 2.95 in the first three semesters, followed by 3.35 and 3.58. Reported analyses distinguished the early and later course clusters, detected no differences within either cluster, and described a similar pattern within the oldest cohort. These grades assess different tasks, with different contributing cohorts across semesters; they do not measure growth on a common assessment of research competence. The joint display connects cohort-level accounts to course-stage patterns, locating application as a meaningful curricular transition. It does not link interviewees' experiences to their own grade changes, leaving the relationship between appreciation and performance unresolved.

Delayed recognition of value is central to the authors' interpretation. In their reading of Kirkpatrick and learning-transfer evaluation models [43, 53], appreciation is treated as an immediate response to a learning experience. They use the delayed appreciation reported here to question that timing assumption. For students encountering research after secondary school, the authors propose that making unfamiliar content usable takes time. Evaluating appreciation only when content is introduced could therefore miss value recognized through later application. This interpretation directs attention to when and in what circumstances students judge the relevance of research learning.

Undergraduate medical programs should provide opportunities to conduct supervised research alongside instruction in methods [59, 60]. Authentic projects give learners a purpose for revisiting concepts and opportunities to exercise judgment with expert support. Where programs can sustain supervision and continuity across courses, they should connect foundational teaching with mentored projects, support professional communication, and encourage reflection and analogical reasoning across experiences [63]. The 8A-Model can guide curriculum design by asking where students encounter ideas, where they use them, and how they articulate their findings. Purposeful revisiting of these activities preserves the original rationale for an integrated, iterative curriculum.

The single-institution, nonexperimental design limits conclusions about developmental sequence and effects. Fifteen participants in three cohort focus groups reconstructed experiences across the curriculum rather than being followed individually over time. Four oldest-cohort participants checked the framework against their experiences; this was member checking, not prospective validation. Longitudinal studies across programs should examine individual learning and the framework's applicability using comparable assessments and repeated interviews. Research practice, research careers and patient outcomes remain priorities for longer-term evaluation. The original expectations of improved care and lower costs extend beyond the outcomes measured here.

## Conclusion

The 8A-Model interprets undergraduate research learning as a process in which applying methods can reveal the value of earlier instruction. Student accounts and course-stage grade patterns support attention to that transition, while the framework allows overlapping and iterative learning. Undergraduate medical curricula should make supervised research integral to learning methods, with opportunities to reflect on practice and communicate findings. The model provides a basis for organizing those opportunities and investigating their longer-term consequences.
