## Discussion

Food insecurity at this university was associated with financial strain, housing insecurity and students' characteristics; written accounts described the practical difficulties of obtaining suitable meals. The April 2021 survey yielded 2,116 complete responses and 176 written statements. Among undergraduate and graduate respondents, respectively, 15.9% and 16.5% were classified as having low or very low food security, termed food insecurity here. Together, these findings connect the question of who needs support with how students obtain and prepare food. They reinforce the interconnected food, housing and financial difficulties described in earlier research reviewed by Hall et al. (2024).

First-generation and transfer students, students with disabilities, Black or African American students, and students with lower grade point averages were among the groups experiencing greater food insecurity. Universities should communicate available resources through student groups and established points of contact throughout students' studies. Continuing outreach is needed because unexpected expenses and changing financial resources can alter access after initial screening. Advising and mental-health services offer opportunities to connect students with food assistance without repeatedly surveying the entire student body. The association with academic performance gives academic advisors a specific reason to include food access in student support.

The written accounts clarify difficulties that the binary food-security classification cannot describe. One respondent with a disability described the time needed to shop and cook, food spoiling before preparation, and competing demands of eating and studying. Another described campus dining restrictions that left their dietary needs unmet. Universities should review food offerings for dietary suitability and consider preparation demands, transport, service locations and course schedules when organizing assistance. A student's suggestion of vouchers instead of fixed packages identifies a practical issue: provision needs to fit what recipients normally eat and know how to prepare. Student feedback should guide these decisions.

Housing insecurity provides a strong reason to coordinate material support. Compared with respondents in steady housing, those worried about losing their home had higher odds of food insecurity (odds ratio 7.91; 95% confidence interval 5.67–11.07). Financial strain and an account of couch-surfing reinforce the need to link food assistance with emergency grants, affordable housing and graduate compensation. Institutions should consider dining subsidies and campus employment alongside these measures. Mental-health services should connect students with food and housing support because respondents described overlapping pressures, including pandemic-related difficulties. The university's 2022 commitment to equitable graduate assistantships is a policy response, not a measured improvement in compensation or food security.

Recipients' accounts identify experienced benefits and unmet needs. One described reaching three meals a day with the food service and a friend who cooked; another welcomed reduced concern about meals while academic stress continued. These experiences support maintaining food assistance and improving its fit with students' circumstances. The pilot began in 2020, before the survey, and formally launched in 2022. Although food insecurity was lower than in some studies reviewed by Hall et al. (2024), differences in populations, sampling and methods complicate that comparison. The observed prevalence cannot be attributed to the service: other support and selective participation during the pandemic also offer possible explanations.

The single-university setting, self-report and approximately 6% voluntary response rate constrain prevalence estimates and generalization. Graduate and first-generation students were overrepresented. Cross-sectional associations leave the direction of relationships with academic performance and mental health unresolved. The modified food-security instrument's 12-month recall period may combine summer and semester experiences, despite administration at the end of spring. Written accounts illuminate experiences rather than their frequency or average program effects. Longitudinal research should examine transitions in food security, and evaluations should test whether coordinated services improve access. Measurement aligned with the academic calendar and investigation of disability-related barriers would help clarify when and how students need support.

## Conclusion

At this university, food insecurity was associated with interconnected material pressures, while written accounts showed how obtaining and preparing suitable meals could compete with studying. Universities should connect food assistance with housing, financial and mental-health support, sustain outreach throughout students' studies, and use student feedback to improve provision. The institutional priority is coordinated support that students can use to obtain suitable food within the practical demands of university life.
