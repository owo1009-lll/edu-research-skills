## Discussion

Guo et al.'s (2024) review helps distinguish two homework policy decisions: whether to assign homework and whether to extend its duration. The homework-versus-none comparison favoured homework (Hedges' g = 0.45, 95% confidence interval [0.24, 0.66]), providing a basis for considering supplementary assignments. Optimal duration remains unresolved. Earlier correlational evidence, as described by Guo et al., concerns time that may reflect task difficulty, prior attainment, or effort. Comparisons of assigned work add evidence relevant to instructional decisions, while keeping the question of additional minutes separate from that of introducing homework.

The learning outcomes specify what this positive average means. Arithmetic computation and arithmetic problem-solving had positive estimates, whereas the arithmetic concepts estimate was close to zero (g = −0.02, 95% confidence interval [−0.22, 0.18]). The latter interval permits negative and positive effects. These are separate outcome estimates, without a formal test establishing differences between domains. Thus, the average supports considering homework, but offers no assurance that a particular assignment will develop conceptual understanding. Curriculum decisions require attention to the skill being assessed as well as the presence of an assignment.

The amount comparisons clarify why that distinction matters. As summarized by Guo et al. (2024), Koch (1965) compared roughly 20–30 with 10–15 minutes of arithmetic work: longer assignments produced greater reported growth in concepts, while a problem-solving difference was not detected. This concepts result concerns increasing homework, a different contrast from introducing it. In Dolean and Lervag (2021), writing, balanced, and mathematics groups averaged 27, 20, and 21 minutes, respectively. The writing group did better than the mathematics group in short-term editing and spelling; differences were no longer clear at four months. Additional mathematics homework yielded no detected mathematics benefit. Here, subject allocation and time varied together, preventing attribution to additional minutes alone. Across these comparisons, what is assigned, what is measured, and when it is measured remain integral to interpreting duration.

The evidence also has limits of reliability and present applicability. No included study was rated low risk overall; outcome-measurement and confounding concerns weaken the basis for causal interpretation. All included publications concerned paper-and-pencil homework, and most dated from 1939–1995. Inaccessible eligible sources and the absence of searches in non-English databases restrict coverage. The search ended in November 2021, more than two years before publication, leaving the synthesis incomplete as a guide to contemporary practice.

Guo et al.'s recommendation to use homework as a supplement for primary-school achievement, particularly computation and problem-solving, is a reasonable starting point for curriculum specialists. Teachers adopting supplementary assignments should evaluate the intended skills and their retention, because completion time alone cannot resolve the outcome and follow-up questions exposed by the review. This supports evaluating purposeful assignments without using the pooled average to justify increasing daily minutes. For researchers, the next step is randomized comparisons of several amounts of comparable work, recording completion time while keeping subject content and teaching conditions comparable. Measuring both immediate and retained learning would help distinguish short-term gains from durable achievement.

## Conclusion

The review supports considering supplementary homework, especially for the primary-school arithmetic outcomes emphasized by its authors. Its contribution to duration policy is to show the gap between evidence for assigning homework and evidence for prescribing minutes. Linking assignments to intended learning is defensible; a general daily duration recommendation awaits stronger comparisons of amount, content, and retained outcomes.
