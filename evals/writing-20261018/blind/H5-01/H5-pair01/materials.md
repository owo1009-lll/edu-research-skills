# H5 — systematic-review evidence packet

Source: Guo, L., Li, J., Xu, Z., Hu, X., Liu, C., Xing, X., Li, X., White, H., & Yang, K. (2024). *The relationship between homework time and academic performance among K-12: A systematic review*. Campbell Systematic Reviews, 20(3), e1431. https://doi.org/10.1002/cl2.1431. Full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11409198/. CC BY 4.0.

The developer read the full main text including Tables 1–3, notes and all figure captions, and visually inspected Figures 6 and 8. This is not an independent reanalysis: primary studies, their raw data and the preregistered protocol were not separately read. The notes below report and locate the review's evidence. Citations to individual primary studies refer to **Guo et al.'s extraction and account**. Any direct claim to have assessed those original papers would exceed this packet.

## Question and scope

The review asks how homework duration relates to achievement and seeks useful guidance on duration. Its intervention is schoolteacher-assigned work completed outside school, excluding guided in-school study, extracurricular teaching and flipped-classroom instruction. Eligible participants are K–12 students without the special-education/disability groups excluded by the authors. The search was last run on 5 November 2021 across databases, publisher platforms, organizations and supplementary sources; the review was published in September 2024. [Background; Methods; B196]

The authors contrast this question with earlier correlational reviews. Time spent may reflect task difficulty, prior attainment or effort, not simply a chosen dose of otherwise identical work. Guo et al. describe earlier positive correlations, including Fan et al.'s math/science synthesis, and proposals for nonlinear relations. Those reviews have not been independently checked here. No single earlier estimate should be treated as directly comparable to an intervention effect expressed in Hedges' g. [B023–B024, B035–B037, B209–B211]

## Evidence and results

- Eleven publications were included. All involved paper-and-pencil homework, with substantial differences in subjects, assignments and outcome tests. Ten publications fed the homework-versus-no-homework comparison, reported as 14 independent reports; two examined different amounts of homework, with one publication contributing to both questions. The latter evidence was narrated, not pooled into a duration-response curve. Most sources date from 1939–1995; one is from 2021. [Table 2; B140–B158, B171–B175, B184–B187]
- For **homework versus none**, the reported random-effects pooled estimate is g = 0.45, 95% CI [0.24, 0.66]. Reported subdomain estimates: arithmetic computation g = 0.46 [0.17, 0.75]; arithmetic problem-solving g = 0.17 [0.02, 0.33]; arithmetic concepts g = −0.02 [−0.22, 0.18]. These are separate outcome estimates. The review supplies no formal test establishing differences between these subdomain effects. [B175; Figure 6]
- For **more versus less homework**, Guo et al. describe Koch (1965) comparing roughly 20–30 versus 10–15 minutes of arithmetic work. Greater growth was reported for arithmetic concepts with longer assignments; a corresponding difference in problem-solving was not detected. There is no estimated full curve or optimum in that narrow comparison. [Table 2; B184–B185]
- The review describes Dolean and Lervag (2021) as assigning different balances of writing and mathematics work. The writing, balanced and mathematics groups averaged 27, 20 and 21 minutes respectively. The writing group did better in short-term editing and spelling than the mathematics group; differences were no longer clear at four months. Increasing mathematics homework did not produce a detected benefit in mathematics skill. Amount, subject allocation and time should remain identifiable when describing this comparison; the packet does not isolate a pure effect of minutes with content held constant. [Table 2; B152–B153, B186]
- Planned subgroup and moderator analyses could not all be carried out because evidence was sparse. The authors did not establish a linear or nonlinear duration-response relation or optimal minutes per day. [B187, B193–B194, B210–B211]

## Quality, author position and source issues

The review rated neither the randomized nor nonrandomized studies as low risk overall. It identifies outcome-measurement and confounding concerns. Several eligible sources were inaccessible, non-English databases were not searched, and the publication followed the last search by more than two years. These limits affect the strength and current reach of the synthesis. The authors recommend homework as a supplement for primary-school achievement, especially arithmetic computation and problem-solving, while calling for better duration studies and evidence for other age groups. This is their practical conclusion, not proof of a universally optimal daily schedule. [B196–B213]

The source has unresolved reporting inconsistencies: Table 2 and narrative sections differ in study design labels and age/grade coverage; the discussion's claim that coverage was only grades 2–8 sits alongside high-school entries in the table. The pooled heterogeneity statistics require checking, and the sensitivity range printed in B181 does not match Figure 8. We have not silently repaired these items or re-estimated results. The pooled estimate above agrees with Figure 6 after rounding (figure: 0.447 [0.238, 0.656]). For this exercise, avoid unsupported exact totals by school level, a claimed sensitivity range or new certainty ratings. Document source questions separately where appropriate.

No data in this packet establish improved mental health, a causal fatigue mechanism, an ideal timetable, effects of online homework or a general dose recommendation for all K–12 students. The review's theoretical account of readiness, practice and feedback is an explanatory framework, not a measured mediation result.
