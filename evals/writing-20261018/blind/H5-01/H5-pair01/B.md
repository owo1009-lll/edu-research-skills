## Discussion

Guo et al. (2024) provide stronger grounds for considering homework as a supplement than for prescribing its duration. Their pooled homework-versus-none estimate favored homework (Hedges' g = 0.45, 95% CI [0.24, 0.66]), whereas the evidence comparing different amounts did not establish a duration-response relation. Curriculum policy therefore faces two distinct decisions: whether to assign work and how much to assign. The review brings assigned-homework comparisons to a debate previously informed by correlations between time and achievement. That shift matters because time spent may reflect task difficulty, prior attainment or effort, complicating its interpretation as a dose of instruction.

Achievement also needs to be specified. Arithmetic computation and arithmetic problem-solving had positive estimates, while the estimate for arithmetic concepts was close to zero, with an interval spanning negative and positive effects (g = −0.02, 95% CI [−0.22, 0.18]). No formal comparison established differences between these domain effects. The concepts interval accommodates both lower and higher achievement; it does not establish that homework is ineffective for conceptual learning. Curriculum specialists consequently need to examine the outcome an assignment is intended to support, alongside the overall achievement estimate.

The duration evidence adds a different comparison. In Guo et al.'s account, Koch (1965) reported greater growth in arithmetic concepts with roughly 20–30 versus 10–15 minutes of work, without a detected corresponding difference in problem-solving. This concerns longer versus shorter assignments, whereas the pooled concepts estimate concerns homework versus none. Dolean and Lervag (2021), also reported through the review, varied the balance of writing and mathematics work. Their writing group showed short-term editing and spelling advantages over the mathematics group; differences were no longer clear at four months. Increased mathematics homework produced no detected mathematics-skill benefit. Here, time changed alongside subject allocation, leaving the contribution of additional minutes unresolved. These findings make task content and retention central to the duration question.

The synthesis has limitations affecting confidence and current applicability. No included study received an overall low-risk rating; outcome-measurement and confounding concerns weaken causal interpretation. All assignments were paper-and-pencil, and most sources dated from 1939–1995, constraining transfer to contemporary online homework. Several eligible sources were inaccessible, non-English databases were not searched, and the final search preceded publication by more than two years. Inconsistent age coverage in the source also complicates school-stage conclusions. These limits concern the reliability and reach of the positive average, beyond the separate shortage of duration comparisons.

Research should connect duration to identifiable tasks and outcomes. Randomized studies should vary amount within comparable subject content and task types, recording actual completion time, prior attainment and task difficulty. This would help distinguish the effect of assigning more work from differences in the time students need to complete it. Broader duration contrasts would permit tests of linear or nonlinear patterns, while follow-up assessments would address whether gains persist. For primary-school practice, the authors' recommendation of supplementary homework, particularly in arithmetic computation and problem-solving, provides a basis for reviewing assignments. Teachers should specify the intended skill and assess whether assignments support it. A universal daily quota requires evidence that directly addresses duration under relevant teaching conditions.

## Conclusion

The review supports considering paper-and-pencil homework for primary-school arithmetic while leaving optimal duration unresolved. Its contribution to policy is to distinguish the case for assigning homework from the case for increasing it. Curriculum decisions should connect assignments to specific learning goals; research should test how amount, content and time relate to gains that persist.
