## Study record

Eight undergraduate courses at one institution. 214 students were offered two channels: automated draft comments plus a teacher question session, or written teacher comments plus the same question session. Students chose the channel; 126 selected automated comments and 88 written teacher comments. A click opening a comment was logged for 117/126 and 82/88 students respectively. This records opening, not attentive reading. A substantive revision was coded in 74/126 and 55/88 final submissions, respectively, using the same rubric; no adjusted group comparison or causal analysis was performed. The records do not identify whether any particular comment caused a revision. Teacher sessions were available in both channels. No attainment test or student-confidence measure was collected.

Voluntary interviews: 16 students, 9 from the automated channel and 7 from the written-teacher channel. Twelve described a question they could discuss with a teacher; four wanted to ask but reported a timetable clash. Interviewees in both channels sometimes questioned a comment's relevance. One student in the automated channel had a proposed terminology change reversed by the teacher; another in the teacher-comment channel persuaded the teacher that an unusual paragraph order served the argument. These are two different students. Interviews support reported opportunities and difficulties, not the proportion of all students with those experiences. The questions session was introduced before this study; it is not a new recommendation invented from the results.

Comparison card [C1], fictional: an optional-feedback service in a different institution recorded faster first responses after adding automated comments. It did not record whether students could question the comments, revisions or learning outcomes. Its finding concerns response delivery, not the availability of reasons. ## Author draft

### Introduction: Response is not yet answerability

Digital feedback is often evaluated by how quickly a learner receives an answer. That is an understandable priority where students otherwise wait until a deadline has passed. It is nevertheless an incomplete account of a feedback relationship. A response can arrive promptly and still leave a student unable to ask why a proposed change would improve the work. We call the availability of a reasoned response to such a question answerability. This does not mean that teachers must approve every objection. It means that a learner can bring a judgement back into discussion and receive reasons for maintaining or revising it.

The optional-feedback service described in [C1] improved response speed after adding automated comments. That finding identifies a gain in delivery. It leaves open the educational question of what happens when a student disagrees with a comment or cannot see its relevance. Our study examines feedback arrangements in which students could choose automated or teacher comments and both channels retained a teacher question session. We are interested in whether this shared arrangement made a consequential distinction between obtaining comments and being able to question them. The distinction also connects feedback with an obligation already placed on students: they must be able to explain and defend their own academic decisions.

### Discussion: Keep the route back to reasons

In eight courses, 126 of 214 students chose automated draft comments and 88 chose written teacher comments. Almost all students read their feedback: opening logs covered 117 in the first channel and 82 in the second. The final submissions showed substantive revision in 74 and 55 cases respectively. Both delivery modes therefore improved students' capacity to revise. Yet these figures by themselves are not the strongest reason for treating answerability as part of the feedback design. A changed paragraph is not a record of whether the learner understood the comment, accepted it uncritically, or developed a better reason for keeping the original version. Counting revisions cannot settle those differences.

The interviews give the distinction a practical form. One student was able to discuss a terminology change, and another defended a less familiar paragraph order. What mattered in these accounts was not simply that a second opinion was available. A reason could be requested, considered and, where appropriate, used to correct a recommendation. Twelve interviewees described a question they could discuss with a teacher. Four reported wanting to ask but being unable to attend because of timetable clashes. These latter cases are not peripheral complaints. A route that exists on a timetable may still be unavailable to the student who needs it.

Automated comments are always insensitive to an author's intentions, whereas teachers can recognise what a student is trying to do. We therefore defend the teacher's role as the place where feedback becomes answerable. But answerability should not be confused with permission to overrule students. The case in which a teacher reconsidered the paragraph order matters because it shows that the teacher's original comment could itself be questioned. The same procedure can make judgements accountable regardless of which channel first generated them.

The findings come from one institution and students chose their channel. They do not resolve which mode of comment is better. Our claim concerns the educational organisation of feedback: students should retain an accessible route to ask for reasons in assessed work. Protecting that route includes making it usable by students whose schedules prevent attendance at the existing session. This proposal extends the purpose of a procedure already in place; it is not a claim that the study has measured the learning benefit of a new intervention.

### Conclusion: Protect answerability

Institutions should protect a route for questioning feedback in assessed work. The present study makes that requirement concrete by distinguishing the delivery of a comment, a subsequent revision and the possibility of asking for reasons. These events may coincide, but they answer different educational questions. Keeping students responsible for their academic choices also requires taking their questions seriously. Our study does not establish that one feedback channel produces greater attainment. It shows why choosing a delivery channel should not remove the opportunity to discuss a consequential judgement.
