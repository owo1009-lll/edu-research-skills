# Later school starts, sleep opportunity and educational outcomes

*Secondary writing exercise based on Dunster et al. (2018). No new study is reported.*

## Introduction

School start times shape adolescents' opportunity to obtain sleep before attending lessons. In their review of sleep research, Dunster et al. (2018) describe a developmental shift towards later sleep timing that can make early school mornings difficult to reconcile with sufficient sleep. A later timetable offers a practical response, but does it allow students to sleep longer, and what changes in participation and performance accompany it? Answering these questions connects the organization of schooling with the conditions under which students learn.

Seattle's move from 07:50 to 08:45 provided an opportunity to examine these relationships. Dunster et al. (2018) compared independent tenth-grade biology cohorts at two public high schools before the change and seven months after implementation. Two weeks of objective wrist recordings, or actigraphy, provided sleep estimates alongside biology grades and school attendance records. Examining sleep under an established school timetable adds field evidence to earlier reports based on sleep surveys and helps distinguish additional sleep from a simple shift in its timing.

## Discussion

Later starts were associated with longer school-night sleep: the median increased from 6 h 50 min to 7 h 24 min, a 34-minute difference. Median waking occurred 44 minutes later. Sleep onset also tended later, although the difference did not meet the adjusted significance threshold; nonschool-night sleep measures showed no significant between-year differences (Dunster et al., 2018). In the cohort comparison, later sleep onset did not wholly offset later waking. This pattern supports the authors' interpretation that easing the early-morning constraint creates an opportunity for more sleep. Lower reported sleepiness adds a further reason to consider school scheduling when addressing students' readiness for lessons.

The observation period also distinguishes Seattle from some earlier studies. Dunster et al.'s (2018) review describes about ten additional minutes of sleep after a 45-minute delay at a Singapore girls' school, compared with almost an hour during a one-week middle-school delay of one hour. Seattle contributes an observation months after a district-wide change. The differing ages, participants, designs and observation periods make these comparisons informative about the settings studied, but do not explain why the sleep gains differed or establish a ranking of policies.

The educational findings require separate interpretations. Median biology grades increased from 77.5% to 82.0%, a 4.5-percentage-point difference. Longer sleep is a plausible contributor, but the study did not identify a causal pathway from sleep to grades. These were teachers' unstandardized second-semester marks, and teachers knew about the schedule change (Dunster et al., 2018). Higher marks therefore describe performance within those classes, with grading practices also relevant to interpreting the comparison. Evaluating achievement with measures comparable across implementation years would help clarify the educational meaning of the grade difference.

School-wide first-period records showed significant reductions in absences and tardies at Franklin, which served more economically disadvantaged students, but no significant changes at Roosevelt (Dunster et al., 2018). These records covered a different population from the actigraphy sample. Reduced missed classroom time at Franklin gives attendance a concrete place in evaluating later starts. Whether benefits differed between schools or by individual socioeconomic circumstances remains unresolved without corresponding comparisons. The findings support examining access to the beginning of the school day at individual schools, with both absences and tardies included.

Comparable teachers, grade level and season strengthen the before-and-after comparison. Independent annual cohorts and the absence of schools retaining the earlier schedule nevertheless leave cohort differences and other year-specific changes as alternative explanations. Following students over time alongside schools with unchanged schedules would help distinguish these possibilities. Such comparisons should retain separate measures of sleep, achievement and attendance so that evidence about one outcome does not stand in for the others.

## Conclusion

The Seattle study associates later school starts with additional school-night sleep under ordinary school conditions, observed months after implementation. Its educational contribution is to make school scheduling part of the practical question of students' sleep opportunity. Higher biology grades and improved first-period attendance at one school warrant evaluating educational outcomes alongside sleep. Schools considering later starts have reason to prioritize sleep opportunity while assessing learning and participation with measures appropriate to each outcome.
