## Background and question

In the authors' account of prior sleep research, adolescents' circadian timing shifts later and sleep pressure accumulates differently from that of younger children. An early school morning can therefore reduce sleep when bedtime cannot advance correspondingly. The policy question is whether moving the school day later adds sleep, and what educational changes accompany it. The paper distinguishes objective wrist recordings from prior reports based on sleep surveys. These background claims and literature comparisons are the source authors' review; the cited studies have not been separately read for this packet. [Introduction B005–B007; Discussion B020]

## Design and observations

- Seattle moved secondary-school starts from 07:50 to 08:45 in 2016–2017. Two public high schools contributed independent tenth-grade biology cohorts in spring 2016 and spring 2017. Classes, teachers, grade level and season were held comparable; the 2017 observations occurred seven months after implementation. There was no concurrent school group retaining the old schedule. [Results B009; Participants B028]
- Wrist actigraphy lasted two weeks and was checked against diaries, event markers and visual inspection. Valid school-night analyses included 94 students in 2016 and 84 in 2017; valid nonschool-night analyses included 81 and 76. These are outcome-specific samples, not the total enrolment of the schools. [Figure 1; Methods]
- Median school-night sleep was 6 h 50 min in 2016 and 7 h 24 min in 2017, a 34-minute difference. Median waking shifted 44 minutes later, from 06:24 to 07:08. Sleep onset tended later but did not meet the paper's multiplicity-adjusted significance threshold. Nonschool-night onset, waking and duration did not show significant between-year differences. [Results B010–B011, B015; Discussion B019]
- Median social jet lag, the adjusted difference between school- and nonschool-day sleep timing, was 1.60 h versus 1.25 h. Median Epworth sleepiness scores were 7 versus 6. These measurements are distinct from direct measures of biological clock phase. [Results B012, B015]
- Median second-semester biology grades were 77.5% versus 82.0%. This is a **4.5-percentage-point difference**, not a 4.5% relative increase. The source reports a significant between-year grade difference. Grades were teachers' absolute, unstandardized marks; teachers knew about the schedule change. [Figure 3A; Data collection B025; Academic performance B045]
- School-wide **first-period** attendance records had a different coverage from the actigraphy sample. At Franklin, annual absences per student were 15.5 versus 13.6, and tardies 6.2 versus 4.3; both reductions were reported significant. At Roosevelt, the corresponding values were 13.0 versus 12.8 and 3.5 versus 3.4, with no significant changes. Franklin served more economically disadvantaged students. No formal between-school interaction estimate or individual-level socioeconomic moderation estimate is supplied. [Figure 3C–D; Results B017; Methods B046]

## Source interpretation and nearby work

The authors argue that a later start creates a practical opportunity to obtain more sleep. They connect the school-day pattern to reduced conflict between schedules and adolescent sleep timing. They regard a sleep-related explanation of higher grades as plausible, while explicitly saying that attributing the grade difference to longer sleep is harder than explaining reduced sleepiness. They recommend pairing later starts with advice about evening sleep habits and screen exposure. This advice is a practical judgment, not a tested additional intervention in this study. [Discussion B019–B020]

Dunster et al.'s own discussion compares three studies: a transition to an earlier start in Rhode Island accompanied shorter objectively recorded sleep; a 45-minute delay at a Singapore girls' school accompanied about ten more minutes of sleep; and a one-week controlled middle-school delay of one hour accompanied almost an hour more sleep. Their account notes differences in ages, participant composition, repeated versus independent cohorts, recording periods and time since implementation. This packet supplies those comparisons only as **Dunster et al.'s review**, not as independent readings or identified explanations for different effect sizes. The Seattle result is a field observation months after a district-wide change, rather than a test of every reason why earlier studies differed. [B020]

## Verification notes available to the writer

The publication contains reporting issues that remain unresolved here: it names a signed-rank procedure for comparisons of independent annual cohorts; Figure 1's caption and the results paragraph do not report all tests identically; the results and methods describe the direction of the year-prediction model differently. We have not reanalysed raw data, corrected tests or created confidence intervals. The descriptive values above are checked against text and figures. Do not silently supply missing inferential statistics. These notes document the source's limits; they do not require reproducing every audit issue in a short manuscript.
