*Secondary writing exercise based on Dunster et al. (2018); the research described below is their published study.*

## Introduction

School start times influence when students must wake, making the timetable relevant to their opportunity to sleep. In their account of adolescent development, Dunster et al. (2018) explain why students may not become sleepy early enough to accommodate an early school morning. Advice to go to bed earlier therefore leaves a policy question unresolved: is a later school day accompanied by additional sleep, and what changes in attendance and academic performance accompany it?

Seattle's move from 07:50 to 08:45 starts provided an opportunity to examine this question. Dunster et al. (2018) compared tenth-grade biology cohorts in two public high schools before the change and seven months afterward. Different students participated in the two years. Two-week wrist actigraphy estimated sleep from wrist recordings, adding objective evidence to earlier survey-based work discussed by the authors. Considering these observations alongside educational records connects the scheduling question to both sleep opportunity and school participation.

## Discussion

Median school-night sleep was 7 h 24 min in the later-start cohort, compared with 6 h 50 min in the earlier cohort. This 34-minute difference accompanied waking 44 minutes later. Sleep onset tended later but did not meet the adjusted significance threshold; nonschool-night sleep measures showed no significant between-year differences. The pattern supports the authors' interpretation that later starts offer additional sleep opportunity by easing conflict between school schedules and adolescent sleep timing (Dunster et al., 2018). Lower reported sleepiness also connects the observed sleep difference to students' daytime experience.

The amount of sleep added remains an empirical question. Studies summarized by Dunster et al. (2018) reported about ten additional minutes after a 45-minute delay at a Singapore girls' school and almost an hour after a one-week, one-hour delay in a controlled middle-school study. Seattle adds objective observation seven months after a district-wide change. Together, these accounts show why the scheduled delay and the sleep gained should be considered separately. Differences in ages, designs and observation periods leave the reasons for the different gains unresolved.

The accompanying grade difference was 4.5 percentage points: median biology marks were 77.5% before the change and 82.0% afterward. As Dunster et al. (2018) argue, a sleep-related explanation is plausible, although the study did not identify a causal pathway from additional sleep to higher grades. These were teachers' unstandardized classroom marks, and teachers knew about the schedule change. The finding therefore concerns assessed performance in these biology classes. Establishing how sleep relates to academic improvement requires evidence beyond the two outcomes changing together.

Attendance adds a question about participation across schools. The records covered school-wide first-period absences and tardies, extending beyond the actigraphy participants. The source reported significant reductions at Franklin, which served more economically disadvantaged students, and no significant changes at Roosevelt. Different significance labels do not establish a greater benefit for disadvantaged students; that inference requires direct comparisons between schools or across students' socioeconomic circumstances. The pattern gives districts a reason to monitor first-period attendance across schools when evaluating a common scheduling policy.

The comparison held teachers, classes, grade level and season comparable, but independent cohorts and the absence of a concurrent unchanged-schedule group leave cohort differences and other annual changes unresolved. Its clearest contribution is an observation of additional recorded sleep under an implemented school policy. For districts seeking to protect adolescent sleep, this supports including start times in policy deliberations. The authors' recommendation to accompany later starts with advice about evening habits and screen exposure offers a practical addition, although its added benefit was not tested.

## Conclusion

In two Seattle high schools, later starts were associated with more recorded school-night sleep months after implementation. Higher biology marks and improved first-period attendance at one school broaden the educational interest of that observation while leaving their causal explanation unresolved. The study brings a concrete scheduling decision into discussions of students' sleep opportunity. Districts considering later starts should evaluate sleep, classroom marks and first-period attendance as distinct outcomes, with attention to their different measurement and school contexts.
