## Discussion

Food insecurity among respondents at this southeastern US land-grant university involved interrelated financial, housing and practical barriers. Low/very low food security was reported by 15.9% of undergraduate and 16.5% of graduate respondents. Combining survey associations with students' written accounts shows why universities need to consider both who struggles to obtain food and whether available provision fits students' circumstances.

First-generation and transfer students, students with disabilities, Black or African American students, and students with lower academic performance were among groups associated with food insecurity. Universities should work through established student groups and advising services to make assistance visible throughout students' studies. Information on syllabi and routine referrals offer opportunities to seek help beyond entry screening. Feedback through these channels can also identify barriers without repeated large surveys. The association with lower academic performance makes food access relevant to academic advising.

Practical barriers help explain why provision alone may be insufficient. One respondent with a disability described the additional time required to shop and cook, food spoiling before preparation, and a conflict between eating and studying. A different respondent described restrictions in campus dining alongside weight loss. These accounts give concrete reasons to review dietary suitability, preparation demands and service accessibility. Universities should align food offerings with allergy, religious and cultural needs, while considering transport, service locations and course schedules.

Housing and financial difficulties strengthen the case for coordinated support. Compared with having a steady home, concern about losing housing was associated with higher odds of food insecurity (odds ratio 7.91, 95% confidence interval 5.67?11.07). For temporary or unstable housing, the odds ratio was 12.88, but its magnitude was imprecise (95% confidence interval 2.58?94.58). One respondent's account of couch-surfing and seeking free food illustrates the overlapping demands. Earlier research reviewed by Hall et al. (2024) likewise connects food access with housing, transport and financial assistance. Universities should connect food assistance with affordable housing and emergency funds, and consider dining subsidies, campus employment and graduate compensation in addressing living costs. The university's 2022 assistantship resolution represents a policy commitment whose consequences for food security require evaluation.

Emotional distress also accompanied food and other basic-needs difficulties, making mental-health services another entry point for support. Students' accounts show both the value and the limits of food assistance. One respondent reported reaching three meals daily with the university service and a friend who cooked. Another welcomed relief from worrying about the next meal while academic stress persisted. These experiences support coordinated referrals across food and mental-health services, with attention to needs that continue after meal access improves. A third respondent suggested store vouchers because fixed packages did not always match preferred foods or cooking knowledge. That concern gives universities a concrete reason to offer greater choice within food assistance.

Reported prevalence was lower than in other institutions reviewed by Hall et al. (2024), but differing populations, recruitment and methods complicate comparison. The food-service pilot began in 2020, before this survey, and respondents described helpful support. These accounts do not establish that the service or pandemic relief explains the lower prevalence. Survey selection is another possible explanation: students under the greatest strain may have been less able to respond. Sustaining and refining assistance should be accompanied by evaluation of its reach and outcomes.

The April 2021 survey covered one university, drew an approximately 6% voluntary response and overrepresented graduate and first-generation students. Selection and self-report limit prevalence estimates, while cross-sectional timing leaves the direction of associations unresolved. The modified food-security instrument used 12-month recall, potentially combining summer and academic-term experiences. Written accounts illuminate particular experiences without estimating their frequency. Longitudinal research should examine changes across the academic calendar; further research should distinguish disability types, which this study did not record, to clarify associated barriers. Evaluation should also test whether the proposed measures improve food security or educational outcomes.

## Conclusion

At this university, food insecurity was associated with financial and housing insecurity, alongside emotional distress and practical barriers to obtaining suitable food. The study connects these patterns with decisions about outreach, food choice and coordinated support. Universities should make assistance accessible throughout students' studies and connect immediate food provision with action on living costs and other basic needs.
