## Discussion

Food insecurity among respondents at this southeastern US land-grant university involved interrelated financial, housing and practical barriers. Low/very low food security was reported by 15.9% of undergraduate and 16.5% of graduate respondents. Combining survey associations with students' written accounts shows why universities need to consider both who struggles to obtain food and whether available provision fits students' circumstances.

First-generation and transfer students, students with disabilities, Black or African American students, and students with lower academic performance were among groups associated with food insecurity. Universities should work through student groups and advising services to make assistance visible throughout students' studies. Information on syllabi and routine referrals would extend opportunities to seek help beyond entry screening, while feedback from these channels could identify barriers without requiring repeated large surveys. Academic difficulty warrants attention alongside food access; the association does not establish which came first.

Practical barriers help explain why provision alone may be insufficient. One respondent with a disability described the additional time required to shop and cook, food spoiling before preparation, and a conflict between eating and studying. A different respondent described campus dietary restrictions alongside weight loss. These accounts give concrete reasons to review dietary suitability, preparation demands and service accessibility. Universities should align food offerings with allergy, religious and cultural needs, while considering transport, service locations and course schedules.

Housing and financial difficulties strengthen the case for coordinated support. Concern about losing a steady home was associated with higher odds of food insecurity than having a steady home (odds ratio 7.91, 95% confidence interval 5.67?11.07). Temporary or unstable housing had a higher point estimate, but substantial uncertainty (12.88, 2.58?94.58). One respondent's account of couch-surfing and seeking free food illustrates the overlapping demands. Earlier research reviewed by Hall et al. (2024) likewise connects food access with housing, transport and financial assistance. Universities should connect food assistance with affordable housing, emergency funds and mental-health services. Dining subsidies and adequate graduate compensation address the costs students face; the university's 2022 assistantship resolution represents a commitment requiring implementation and evaluation.

Students' experiences also support adapting how assistance is delivered. One respondent reported reaching three meals daily with the university service and a friend who cooked. Another welcomed relief from worrying about the next meal while continuing to experience academic stress. A third suggested store vouchers because fixed packages did not always match preferred foods or cooking knowledge. These accounts show valued benefits and a reason to offer greater choice, rather than a single standard package. Coordinated referrals can address overlapping needs while allowing food and mental-health services to serve their distinct purposes.

The reported prevalence was lower than in other institutions reviewed by Hall et al. (2024), but differing populations, recruitment and methods prevent a straightforward comparison. The food-service pilot began in 2020, before this survey, and respondents described helpful support. Nevertheless, the survey cannot attribute the lower prevalence to that service or pandemic relief. Selection into the survey offers another explanation: students under the greatest strain may have been less able to respond. The evidence supports sustaining and refining assistance, alongside evaluation of its reach and outcomes.

Several limitations shape interpretation. The April 2021 survey covered one university, drew an approximately 6% voluntary response and overrepresented graduate and first-generation students. Self-report and selection limit prevalence estimates, while cross-sectional timing leaves the direction of associations unresolved. The modified food-security instrument used 12-month recall, potentially combining summer and academic-term experiences. Written responses illuminate particular experiences without estimating their frequency. Longitudinal research should examine changes in food access across the academic calendar, including disability-related barriers that this study could not differentiate by disability type. Evaluation is also needed to establish whether the proposed measures improve food security or educational outcomes.

## Conclusion

At this university, food insecurity was associated with financial and housing insecurity and accompanied by difficulties obtaining and preparing suitable food. The study links these patterns to specific institutional decisions about outreach, food choice and coordinated support. Universities should make assistance accessible throughout students' studies and connect immediate food provision with action on living costs and other basic needs. Attention to students' actual use of support is central to that responsibility.
