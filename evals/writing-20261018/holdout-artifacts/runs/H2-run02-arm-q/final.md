## Discussion

Food access connects university students' financial resources, housing, health and capacity to obtain suitable meals. In this April 2021 survey at a southeastern US land-grant university, 15.9% of undergraduate and 16.5% of graduate respondents with food-security classifications had low or very low food security. The associations reinforce the interconnected difficulties described in earlier research reviewed by Hall et al. (2024). The 176 written statements collected in the same survey explain what these difficulties involved: obtaining food, preparing it and fitting meals around study. Together, these findings direct attention to whether available assistance becomes usable food.

First-generation and transfer students, Black or African American students, students with disabilities and those with lower grades were among groups associated with food insecurity. Universities should sustain outreach through student groups, advisers and existing services throughout students' studies, as needs can change after initial screening. Familiar points of contact should provide tailored information and seek feedback about obstacles to obtaining help. Integrating food-access referrals into advising offers a way to reach students while reducing reliance on repeated surveys.

The disability findings show why service design should address preparation time as well as money. One respondent described lengthy shopping and cooking, food spoiling before preparation and a conflict between eating and studying that eased after assistance. This account identifies a practical burden behind the statistical association. Connecting academic advising with food assistance responds to these competing demands; it also gives advisers a route to support students whose academic difficulties coexist with food insecurity.

Housing difficulties warrant coordinated action. Compared with a steady home, concern about losing housing was associated with an odds ratio of 7.91 for low or very low food security (95% confidence interval: 5.67–11.07); temporary or unstable housing had an odds ratio of 12.88 (95% confidence interval: 2.58–94.58). The latter estimate indicates substantial uncertainty about the magnitude. Universities should connect housing, financial and food services so that students seeking help with one difficulty can reach other resources. More affordable housing, dining subsidies and adequate graduate compensation address the cost pressures described in the study. Campus employment provides another option for financial support.

Suitable food and accessible services also matter. One student's account of campus dining restrictions and weight loss illustrates the consequences of options that fail to meet dietary needs. Universities should review food offerings for allergies, religious requirements and other needs, and address transport, service locations and course schedules that constrain access. Student feedback should inform food selection and delivery arrangements.

Assistance accounts reveal benefits and remaining needs. One respondent reached three meals a day with the food service and a friend who cooked; another welcomed relief from worrying about the next meal while academic stress persisted. A third proposed store vouchers because fixed packages did not always match familiar foods, preferences or cooking knowledge. This variation supports asking students what they can use and considering vouchers where packages are unsuitable. The persistence of academic stress alongside relief from food worries further supports connecting food assistance with mental health and academic services.

These recommendations build on a food-service pilot begun in 2020, emergency grants and information about local resources. Formal service launch and additional donor support followed in 2022. That year's graduate compensation resolution committed to a strategic goal and funding plan; its food-security outcomes were not assessed. These data do not establish whether institutional support accounts for the lower prevalence than in studies reviewed by Hall et al.; sampling, populations and methods differ. Pandemic-related nonresponse also remains a possible explanation.

Several limitations shape interpretation. Approximately 6% of invited students responded, with graduate and first-generation students overrepresented, limiting campus prevalence estimates. Single-university, self-reported, cross-sectional evidence leaves causal relationships, including those involving grades, unresolved. Written accounts describe experiences rather than their frequency or average program effects. The modified instrument's 12-month recall could include summer experiences despite spring administration. Longitudinal research should examine transitions in food security, measurement should attend to academic calendars, and program evaluations should test food-security and academic outcomes. Disability-related barriers also require closer examination.

## Conclusion

Among these respondents, food insecurity was connected to financial and housing difficulties and unequal capacities to obtain and prepare suitable food. Universities should coordinate food, housing, financial, mental health and academic support, using student feedback to make assistance usable. Reliable access requires attention to both food provision and the circumstances in which students turn it into meals.
