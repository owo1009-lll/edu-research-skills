## Discussion

Food access connects university students' financial resources, housing, health and capacity to obtain suitable meals. In this April 2021 survey at a southeastern US land-grant university, 15.9% of undergraduate and 16.5% of graduate respondents with food-security classifications had low or very low food security. The associations reinforce the interconnected difficulties described in earlier research reviewed by Hall et al. (2024). Written survey accounts add practical detail about obtaining and preparing food, making the implications for student support more concrete.

First-generation and transfer students, Black or African American students, students with disabilities and those with lower grades were among groups associated with food insecurity. Universities should sustain outreach through student groups, advisers and existing services throughout students' studies. Initial screening alone leaves later financial difficulties unaddressed. Familiar points of contact should provide tailored information and seek feedback about obstacles to obtaining help. Advising systems offer an opportunity to connect students with support while reducing repeated survey burden.

The disability findings show why support needs to address time as well as money. One respondent described lengthy shopping and cooking, food spoiling before preparation and a conflict between eating and studying that eased after assistance. This account gives substance to the association between disability and food insecurity without representing all disabled students. The association with lower grades also supports attention to food access in academic support, although its causal direction remains unresolved.

Housing difficulties warrant coordinated action. Compared with a steady home, concern about losing housing was associated with an odds ratio of 7.91 for low or very low food security (95% confidence interval: 5.67–11.07); temporary or unstable housing had an odds ratio of 12.88 (2.58–94.58), with substantial uncertainty about its magnitude. Universities should connect housing, financial, mental health and food services so that students seeking help with one difficulty can reach other resources. More affordable housing, dining subsidies and adequate graduate compensation address the cost pressures identified in the study. Campus employment is another option for financial support.

Food provision must also fit students' dietary needs and preparation circumstances. Universities should review dining options for allergies, religious requirements and other needs, and address transport, service locations and course schedules that constrain access. One student's account of campus dining restrictions and weight loss illustrates the consequences of unsuitable options. Student feedback should inform both food selection and delivery arrangements.

Assistance accounts indicate benefits alongside remaining needs. One respondent reported reaching three meals a day with the food service and a friend who cooked; another described relief from worrying about the next meal while academic stress persisted. A third proposed store vouchers because fixed packages did not always match familiar foods, preferences or cooking knowledge. These experiences support flexible assistance and coordination with other services. They do not estimate average program effects, and the contribution of the friend who cooked makes preparation support part of the explanation for the first respondent's experience.

These recommendations build on a food-service pilot begun in 2020, emergency grants and information about local resources. Formal service launch and additional donor support followed in 2022. The same year's commitment to equitable graduate assistantships was a policy commitment, not measured improvement. Lower food-insecurity estimates than those in other studies cannot establish that institutional action was responsible: sampling, populations and methods differed. Selection during the pandemic offers a further possible explanation, alongside university support and wider financial relief.

Several limitations shape interpretation. Approximately 6% of invited students responded, with graduate and first-generation students overrepresented, limiting campus prevalence estimates. The single-university, self-reported, cross-sectional evidence cannot establish causal pathways or the effects of recommended measures on food security or academic outcomes. The modified instrument's 12-month recall could include summer experiences despite spring administration. Longitudinal research should examine transitions in food security, and future measurement should attend to academic calendars. Disability-related barriers also require closer study. Written accounts describe experiences without establishing how common they were.

## Conclusion

Among these respondents, food insecurity was connected to financial and housing difficulties and unequal capacities to obtain and prepare suitable food. Universities should coordinate food, housing, financial, mental health and academic support, while using student feedback to make assistance usable. This integrated approach follows from the barriers documented here; its outcomes require evaluation.
