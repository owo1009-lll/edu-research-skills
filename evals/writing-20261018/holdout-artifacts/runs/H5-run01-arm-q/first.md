## Discussion

Guo et al.'s (2024) review contributes to homework policy by separating two questions: whether assigning homework benefits achievement and how long pupils should spend on it. The homework-versus-none comparison favoured homework (Hedges' g = 0.45, 95% confidence interval [0.24, 0.66]). This supports homework as a supplement to instruction, but does not establish that longer assignments yield greater gains. Earlier correlational evidence, as discussed by Guo et al., concerns time that may also reflect difficulty, prior attainment, or effort. The review therefore changes the question from whether time and achievement covary to what comparisons of assigned work can tell educators about learning.

The outcome estimates show why the overall average needs educational interpretation. Arithmetic computation and arithmetic problem-solving had positive estimates, whereas the arithmetic concepts estimate was close to zero (g = −0.02, 95% confidence interval [−0.22, 0.18]). Its interval leaves both negative and positive effects possible. These separate estimates do not establish that homework works better for one domain than another, because the review supplies no formal comparison between them. They nevertheless make the learning objective consequential: a general achievement average cannot specify what conceptual learning a particular assignment will produce.

The two amount comparisons sharpen this distinction. In Guo et al.'s account, Koch (1965) compared roughly 20–30 with 10–15 minutes of arithmetic work, reporting greater growth in concepts with longer assignments but no detected problem-solving difference. Dolean and Lervag (2021) compared different allocations of writing and mathematics work, with different average completion times. The writing group outperformed the mathematics group in short-term editing and spelling, while differences were no longer clear at four months; increasing mathematics homework produced no detected mathematics benefit. Read together, these studies make a uniform duration rule difficult to defend. They concern different skills and measurement times, and the latter comparison entangles time with subject allocation. Neither a full duration-response curve nor an optimum follows from them.

The strength and contemporary reach of the evidence also constrain policy use. None of the randomized or nonrandomized studies was rated low risk overall, with outcome-measurement and confounding concerns. All included publications concerned paper-and-pencil homework, and most dated from 1939–1995. Inaccessible eligible sources and the exclusion of non-English databases restrict coverage; the search ended in November 2021, more than two years before publication. These features limit confidence in transferring the pooled average to present classrooms or online assignments.

For curriculum specialists, a defensible use of this review is to retain homework as a possible supplement while judging assignments against the skills they are intended to develop. Guo et al.'s recommendation centres on primary-school achievement, particularly computation and problem-solving. Teachers can evaluate those outcomes rather than treating minutes completed as sufficient evidence of learning. Research should distinguish the amount of comparable work from changes in subject allocation, examine multiple durations, and assess both immediate and retained learning. Such comparisons would address the policy question left unresolved by the positive homework-versus-none average.

## Conclusion

The review provides a qualified basis for supplementary homework, but the evidence for its presence is stronger than the evidence for a prescribed duration. Its contribution is to distinguish those decisions and identify the outcomes and comparisons on which they depend. Homework policy should connect assignments to learning objectives while leaving optimal daily minutes an open empirical question.
