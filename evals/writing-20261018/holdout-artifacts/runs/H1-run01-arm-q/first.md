# Later school starts, sleep opportunity and educational outcomes

*Secondary writing exercise based on Dunster et al. (2018). No new study is reported.*

## Introduction

School start times shape the conditions under which adolescents arrive ready to learn. In their review of sleep research, Dunster et al. (2018) describe a developmental shift towards later sleep timing that can make early school mornings difficult to reconcile with sufficient sleep. Moving the timetable later offers a practical response, but its educational value depends on whether students actually sleep longer and whether changes in sleep accompany changes in school participation and performance. These questions connect the organization of schooling with students' opportunity to rest.

Seattle's move from 07:50 to 08:45 provided an opportunity to examine these relationships. Dunster et al. (2018) compared independent tenth-grade biology cohorts at two public high schools before the change and seven months after implementation. Their question was whether later starts were associated with longer sleep and accompanying educational improvements. Two weeks of wrist actigraphy provided objective sleep estimates alongside biology grades and school attendance records. This combination adds evidence from everyday school conditions to a policy debate often informed by sleep surveys.

## Discussion

Later starts were associated with longer school-night sleep: the median increased from 6 h 50 min to 7 h 24 min, a 34-minute difference. Median waking occurred 44 minutes later. Sleep onset also tended later, although the difference did not meet the adjusted significance threshold; nonschool-night sleep measures showed no significant between-year differences (Dunster et al., 2018). This pattern is consistent with reduced pressure to wake early on school days. The additional time available in the morning was accompanied by longer sleep, rather than being wholly absorbed by later sleep onset. Lower reported sleepiness further supports the practical relevance of this change, although the measurements do not directly establish a shift in biological clock timing.

The result also contributes a different observation period from some earlier studies. Dunster et al.'s (2018) review describes about ten additional minutes of sleep following a delay at a Singapore girls' school and almost an hour during a one-week controlled middle-school delay. Seattle's measurements show a sleep difference months after a district-wide change. Differences in age, participants, design and observation period prevent treating these contrasts as a direct ranking of policies or as explanations of why sleep gains differed.

The educational findings require distinct interpretations. Median biology grades increased from 77.5% to 82.0%, a 4.5-percentage-point difference. Longer sleep is a plausible contributor, but the study did not identify a causal pathway from sleep to grades. These were teachers' unstandardized marks, and teachers knew about the schedule change (Dunster et al., 2018). The result therefore concerns performance within those biology classes; it cannot be read as an equivalent gain on standardized achievement measures. It nevertheless makes classroom performance a relevant outcome for evaluating scheduling policies.

School-wide first-period records showed significant reductions in absences and tardies at Franklin, which served more economically disadvantaged students, but no significant changes at Roosevelt. These records covered a different population from the actigraphy sample (Dunster et al., 2018). The pattern raises a question about access to the beginning of the school day. It does not establish that socioeconomic disadvantage increased the benefit of later starts: contrasting significance labels are insufficient evidence of a difference between schools, and individual socioeconomic moderation was not estimated.

Comparable teachers, grade level and season strengthen the before-and-after comparison. Nevertheless, independent annual cohorts and the absence of schools retaining the earlier schedule leave cohort differences and other year-specific changes unresolved. A comparison following students over time alongside schools with unchanged schedules would better separate these possibilities. For policy evaluation, sleep, classroom performance and attendance should remain separate outcomes because their measurements and possible explanations differ.

## Conclusion

The Seattle evidence associates later school starts with additional school-night sleep under ordinary school conditions. Higher biology grades and improved first-period attendance at one school extend the educational question beyond sleep duration, while leaving their causal explanation open. Schools considering later starts should treat increased sleep opportunity as a central objective and evaluate learning and attendance alongside it, with attention to the students and settings represented by each measure.
