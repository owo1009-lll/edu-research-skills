## Discussion

This study identifies connected challenges in university students' access to food. Low or very low food security, combined here as food insecurity, was reported by 15.9% of undergraduate and 16.5% of graduate respondents. Associations with housing, finances and mental health accord with earlier studies reviewed by Hall et al. (2024). The written survey accounts add practical detail: having food available does not ensure that students can obtain, prepare or eat food suitable for their needs. The survey identifies who reported difficulty, while the accounts clarify which features of support need attention. Together, they support coordinating food assistance with housing, financial and mental health services.

Housing difficulties were particularly closely associated with food insecurity. Compared with respondents with a steady home, those worried about losing it had higher odds of food insecurity (odds ratio 7.91, 95% confidence interval 5.67–11.07). The estimate for temporary or unstable housing was larger but much less precise (12.88, 2.58–94.58). Financial difficulties also involved borrowing for food or cutting other essential spending. These findings support affordable housing, adequate graduate compensation, dining subsidies and expanded campus employment as priorities for addressing budget pressures alongside immediate food provision. The university's 2022 graduate compensation resolution commits to a strategic goal and funding plan; this provides a policy basis for action on compensation.

The educational implications extend beyond financial resources. Disability and lower academic performance were associated with food insecurity. One respondent with a disability described time spent shopping and cooking, food spoiling before preparation, and conflict between eating and studying. Another student described couch-surfing and stretching meal credits; a separate account described campus dining restrictions and weight loss. Such accounts show how obtaining and preparing suitable food can compete with study. Universities should review dietary provision and consider transport, service locations and course schedules when designing access to food support.

At the time of the April 2021 survey, the food service was a pilot begun in 2020; its formal launch followed in 2022. Assistance experiences show why service design matters. One respondent described reaching three meals a day with university assistance and a friend who cooked. Another welcomed relief from worrying about the next meal while academic stress persisted. A third proposed store vouchers because fixed packages did not match students' preferences or preparation knowledge. Universities should use this feedback to improve food offerings and consider voucher options where fixed packages do not meet students' needs. Emergency grants and information about local resources were also available. The lower prevalence than in studies at other institutions does not establish service effectiveness: differences in sampling, populations and methods leave the contribution of support unresolved.

Outreach should continue throughout students' studies. First-generation and transfer students may be unfamiliar with available support, while unexpected expenses can create needs after entry. Student groups, advisers and mental health services offer routes for communicating resources, especially to groups associated with food insecurity, including students with disabilities and Black students. Listing resources on syllabi and integrating food access into advising can reinforce these routes while reducing reliance on repeated surveys. Student feedback should guide both communication and responses to the barriers behind requests for assistance. Connecting services also preserves attention to mental distress that may persist when food worries ease.

Several limitations shape interpretation. This single-university, voluntary survey reached approximately 6% of invitees, with graduate and first-generation students overrepresented; prevalence estimates therefore need not describe the wider student population. Self-reported, cross-sectional data do not establish whether food insecurity preceded academic difficulty. The modified food-security instrument used a 12-month recall period that could include summer experiences despite administration near the end of spring. Written accounts illustrate experiences rather than their frequency. Repeated observations should examine transitions in food security and disability-related barriers; measurement research should assess recall periods aligned with academic calendars. The proposed institutional responses remain untested for effects on food security, grades or retention and require evaluation.

## Conclusion

At this university, food insecurity was linked to interconnected social and economic circumstances, while written accounts revealed practical obstacles to using food and assistance. The contribution is to connect the identification of students needing support with the conditions that make support usable. Universities should pursue sustained outreach, suitable food provision and coordinated responses to financial, housing and mental health needs as part of supporting students through their degrees.
