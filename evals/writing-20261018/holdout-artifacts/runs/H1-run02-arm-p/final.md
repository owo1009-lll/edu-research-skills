# Later School Starts and the Opportunity to Sleep

*Secondary writing exercise based on Dunster et al. (2018); the research described below was conducted by the source authors.*

## Introduction

School start times help determine whether adolescents have an opportunity to sleep before the school day. In the research reviewed by Dunster et al. (2018), adolescent sleep timing shifts later, creating a potential conflict with early school mornings when bedtime cannot advance correspondingly. A later start offers additional morning time, but its educational relevance depends on whether that time accompanies longer sleep and changes in learning or participation. The policy question therefore concerns both realized sleep and the educational outcomes associated with a different timetable.

Seattle's move from 07:50 to 08:45 allowed Dunster et al. (2018) to examine these questions in two public high schools. They compared independent tenth-grade biology cohorts using two-week wrist recordings of sleep, or actigraphy, before and seven months after implementation. These objective observations extend the survey-based evidence discussed by the authors by documenting sleep under a schedule already in operation. Biology grades and school-wide first-period attendance records connect this question to classroom achievement and participation.

## Discussion

The later start was associated with 34 minutes more median school-night sleep. Waking shifted 44 minutes later, while sleep onset tended later but did not meet the paper's adjusted significance threshold. Thus, the extra morning time coincided with longer sleep despite a tendency toward later onset. Nonschool-night timing and duration showed no significant between-year differences. Together with lower reported sleepiness, this pattern is consistent with reduced conflict between school schedules and adolescents' sleep timing (Dunster et al., 2018).

The Seattle result also adds a useful timescale to the policy evidence. In Dunster et al.'s (2018) account of earlier work, a Singapore girls' school showed a smaller sleep gain after delaying its start, whereas a controlled middle-school study recorded a larger gain during a one-week delay. Differences in ages, participants and observation periods contextualize these findings without establishing why their magnitudes differ. Seattle contributes evidence of longer sleep observed months after a district-wide change, when the policy was part of ordinary school organization.

Educational outcomes require attention to what each measure captures. Median biology grades were 4.5 percentage points higher in the later cohort. A sleep-related explanation is plausible, but the study did not establish that longer sleep accounted for this difference. Grades were unstandardized teacher marks, and teachers knew about the schedule change, leaving learning, grading and cohort differences as unresolved explanations. Subsequent evaluations should therefore pair sleep measurements with a common achievement measure.

Participation raised a further question about who might benefit. School-wide first-period absences and tardies declined significantly at Franklin, which served more economically disadvantaged students, while Roosevelt showed no significant changes. These administrative records covered a different population from the sleep recordings. The pattern warrants examining whether later starts support participation among disadvantaged students, but a difference in statistical significance between schools does not establish a difference in their responses. Direct comparisons of changes and individual socioeconomic information are needed to address that question.

Across outcomes, the independent annual cohorts and absence of a concurrent group retaining the earlier schedule leave cohort differences and year-specific influences unresolved. Comparable teachers, classes, grade level and season strengthen the comparison without removing that uncertainty. Schools considering later starts should monitor sleep and educational outcomes together, using concurrent comparisons where feasible. Because the sleep opportunity also depends on evening behavior, Dunster et al. (2018) recommend advice about sleep habits and screen exposure; the additional benefit of that advice was not tested.

## Conclusion

In Seattle's before-and-after comparison, a later start was associated with longer school-night sleep, alongside higher biology grades and improved first-period attendance in one school. Objective recordings months after implementation make sleep opportunity a concrete consideration in school organization. The educational judgment is to consider scheduling as a condition supporting students' readiness for school, while evaluating whether accompanying changes in learning and participation can be attributed to the policy.
