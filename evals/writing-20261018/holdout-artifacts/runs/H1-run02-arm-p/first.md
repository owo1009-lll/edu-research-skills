# Later School Starts and the Opportunity to Sleep

*Secondary writing exercise based on Dunster et al. (2018); the research described below was conducted by the source authors.*

## Introduction

School start times determine when students must be ready to learn, making the timetable part of the conditions under which education takes place. Adolescents' later sleep timing can conflict with early school mornings when bedtimes do not advance correspondingly, according to the research reviewed by Dunster et al. (2018). Delaying school therefore offers a plausible way to extend sleep. For education policy, the question is whether students actually use the additional morning time for sleep and whether educational outcomes change alongside it.

Seattle's move from 07:50 to 08:45 provided an opportunity to examine this question. Dunster et al. (2018) compared independent tenth-grade biology cohorts in two public high schools before and after the change, using two-week wrist recordings of sleep, or actigraphy. Observations seven months after implementation connect the policy question to students' sleep under an established schedule. Biology grades and school-wide first-period attendance records also allow consideration of educational outcomes alongside sleep.

## Discussion

The later start was associated with longer school-night sleep: median duration was 34 minutes greater in the later cohort. Waking shifted 44 minutes later, while sleep onset tended later without meeting the adjusted significance threshold. This pattern is consistent with students using some of the additional morning time to sleep. Nonschool-night timing and duration showed no significant differences, supporting an interpretation focused on the school schedule, although this comparison does not establish identical sleep outside school nights. Lower reported sleepiness adds to the practical relevance of the sleep observations (Dunster et al., 2018).

The amount of additional sleep should be understood in its setting. Dunster et al. (2018) describe a Singapore girls' school where a delay accompanied a smaller sleep gain and a controlled middle-school study with a larger gain during a one-week delay. These are comparisons from their review. Differences in age, participants and observation periods make the Seattle estimate an addition to this evidence, rather than an explanation of why effects vary. Seattle shows that longer sleep was observable months after a district-wide change.

The educational results are encouraging but require distinct interpretations. Median biology grades were 4.5 percentage points higher in the later cohort. Longer sleep offers a plausible explanation, yet the study did not establish that sleep accounted for the grade difference. Teachers' marks were unstandardized, and teachers knew about the schedule change. Consequently, the difference may reflect learning, grading or other cohort differences. A common achievement measure would make subsequent evaluations more informative about learning.

Attendance also directs attention to school context. School-wide first-period absences and tardies decreased significantly at Franklin, which served more economically disadvantaged students; Roosevelt showed no significant changes. This pattern gives schools a reason to monitor participation when changing schedules. It does not demonstrate that disadvantaged students benefited more, because the study supplied neither a formal between-school comparison of changes nor individual socioeconomic moderation estimates. The attendance records also covered a different population from the sleep recordings.

The independent-cohort design leaves changes between years unresolved. Comparable teachers, classes, grade level and season strengthen the comparison, but there was no concurrent group retaining the original schedule. Future evaluations should include such a comparison and follow sleep and educational outcomes together. Schools considering later starts can also follow the source authors' recommendation to provide advice about evening sleep habits and screen exposure, while recognizing that the added value of this advice was not tested.

## Conclusion

Seattle's later start was associated with longer school-night sleep and higher biology grades, alongside attendance improvements in one of the two schools. Its clearest educational contribution is evidence that a change in the timetable can coincide with more sleep in everyday schooling. Schools considering schedule changes should treat sleep as a policy outcome in its own right and assess whether accompanying changes in learning and participation follow.
