# Rationale and evidence record

## Scope and authorial position, recorded before drafting

This is an attributed secondary revision of Hall et al. (2024), not an author manuscript or an independent reanalysis. The task authorizes merging and shortening the supplied Discussion, Limitations and Conclusions into an English Discussion and short Conclusion of 650–800 words. Only the supplied packet supplies research evidence. References within Hall et al. were not independently read. The skill snapshot supplies writing guidance, not additional empirical evidence.

The original passage asks how universities can identify students experiencing food insecurity and organize useful support. Its argument moves from associated student characteristics to practical barriers and institutional responsibilities: continuing communication; suitable campus food; housing and financial support; links with mental-health services; coordinated food provision; and advising that reduces reliance on repeated surveys. Its substantive position is that food access permeates university operations and should be addressed through connected services. The main contribution is the combination of survey associations and concrete written experiences, which specifies what coordinated support needs to address. It is not proof that the campus pilot lowered prevalence, nor an intervention study of academic attainment.

The compressed argument will proceed from the local prevalence and contribution, through continuing outreach, practical usability of food, linked housing/financial/mental-health support, and experienced assistance benefits, to limitations and a concise institutional conclusion. This retains the authors' practical position while the user-authorized merger removes repeated Recommendations headings and long implementation inventories. These are expression/organization changes under category (a), not newly discovered factual errors. Categories: (a) a problem in the original facts, logic, or expression; (b) a claim adjustment demanded by actual results; (c) unresolved source information. No new analyses were conducted, so category (b) will not be claimed.

## Evidence supporting the central judgment

- Table 4: 213/1,343 undergraduate respondents (15.9%) and 125/759 graduate respondents (16.5%) were classified with low/very low food security. These are table-specific denominators, not the total survey response count.
- Figures 1 and 6 and the original Discussion: associations with disability, academic performance, demographic characteristics, financial difficulty and housing insecurity locate relevant support needs.
- Results B050–B051, B063 and B067: distinct written accounts describe difficulties shopping/preparing food, couch-surfing and accessing suitable campus food. They supply concrete experiences, not population frequencies.
- B076–B080: aid recipients describe relief and improved meal access; cooking help from a friend and continuing academic stress qualify what each account establishes. A preference for vouchers identifies a mismatch with fixed packages.
- B082–B119: the authors already recommend sustained outreach, dietary review, housing and financial support, mental-health referrals, service coordination and advising.

## Evidence that constrains the central judgment

- One university, roughly 6% voluntary response, composition differences and self-report constrain prevalence and generalizability.
- Cross-sectional timing does not establish directions of relationships or intervention effects; low GPA is not a measured outcome of a tested food program.
- The modified 12-month measure may combine summer and semester experiences. Broader access includes preparing suitable food; it is not interchangeable with the binary score.
- Written statements are part of the survey, not interviews; accounts do not establish their frequency or average program effects.
- Other-institution prevalence comparisons differ in sampling, student populations and methods. No attribution of lower prevalence to the pilot or relief payments is warranted.
- The 2022 assistantship resolution is a commitment. The pilot began in 2020 and formally launched in 2022; later decisions cannot explain an April 2021 survey estimate.

## Author queries and source limits (outside manuscript)

1. Abstract invitations total 35,337 and Methods 35,339; Table 3 contains further denominators. Use the approximate 6% response rate without repairing these totals.
2. No raw data or deposited appendices were read or recomputed. Numerical checks use the supplied extraction only; the source packet itself describes which images and tables were audited.
3. GLMs are mentioned but full mutual adjustment is not established. Do not add “independent” or “adjusted” to the associations.
4. The temporary/unstable housing association has a wide interval: OR 12.88 [2.58, 94.58]. If omitted for length, do not replace it with a claim of a precisely established gradient. The principal housing example may use concern about losing housing versus steady housing: OR 7.91 [5.67, 11.07].
5. Disability types were not collected; recommendations should remain attentive to individual preparation/access needs rather than invent condition-specific services.
6. Secondary claims about earlier literature will be attributed to Hall et al.'s review. No outside references, current service eligibility details or unprovided program effectiveness estimates will be introduced.
