## Discussion

Food insecurity at this university was connected with students' financial resources, housing circumstances and ability to obtain suitable meals. Low or very low food security was reported by 15.9% of undergraduate respondents and 16.5% of graduate respondents. Together, survey associations and written accounts explain why university support must address both resources and practical access. The findings are consistent with the interconnected food, housing and financial difficulties described in earlier research reviewed by Hall et al. (2024).

First-generation and transfer students, students with disabilities, Black or African American students, and students with lower grades were among the groups associated with food insecurity. Universities should communicate available resources through student groups and other established points of contact throughout students' studies. First-year screening alone is insufficient when unexpected expenses and changing resources can alter access. Advising and mental-health services offer opportunities to connect students with food assistance without repeatedly surveying the entire student body. The association with lower grades also makes food access relevant to academic support, although the study does not determine the direction of this relationship.

The written accounts clarify barriers that a financial measure alone can overlook. One respondent with a disability described the time needed to shop and cook, food spoiling before preparation, and competing demands of eating and studying. Another described campus dining restrictions that left their dietary needs unmet. Universities should review food offerings for dietary suitability and consider preparation demands, transport, service locations and course schedules when organizing assistance. A student's suggestion of vouchers instead of fixed packages illustrates the value of matching provision to what recipients normally eat and can prepare. Student feedback should guide these decisions.

Housing insecurity provides a strong reason to coordinate material support. Compared with respondents in steady housing, those worried about losing their home had higher odds of food insecurity (odds ratio 7.91; 95% confidence interval 5.67–11.07). Accounts of financial strain and couch-surfing reinforce the need to link food assistance with emergency grants, affordable housing and graduate compensation. Institutions should consider dining subsidies and campus employment alongside these measures. Mental-health services should be linked with food and housing support because respondents described overlapping pressures, including pandemic-related difficulties. The university's 2022 commitment to equitable graduate assistantships represents a policy response, rather than evidence that compensation or food security had improved.

Existing assistance was valuable to some respondents. One described reaching three meals a day with the food service and a friend who cooked; another welcomed reduced concern about meals while academic stress continued. These experiences support maintaining food assistance and improving its fit with students' circumstances. The pilot began in 2020, before the April 2021 survey, and formally launched in 2022. Although food insecurity was lower than in some studies reviewed by Hall et al. (2024), differences in populations, sampling and methods make comparisons uncertain. The observed prevalence cannot be attributed to the service: other support and selective participation during the pandemic also offer possible explanations.

Interpretation is limited by the single-university setting, self-report and an approximately 6% voluntary response rate. Graduate and first-generation students were overrepresented, limiting the sample's representation of the student body. Cross-sectional associations do not establish whether food insecurity preceded academic or mental-health difficulties. The modified food-security instrument's 12-month recall period may combine summer and semester experiences, despite administration at the end of spring. Written accounts identify experiences and variations, not their prevalence or average program effects. Longitudinal research should examine transitions in food security, and evaluations should test whether coordinated services improve access. Measurement aligned with the academic calendar and closer examination of disability-related barriers would help make support more responsive.

## Conclusion

At this university, food insecurity was associated with interconnected material pressures, while written accounts showed how obtaining and preparing suitable meals could compete with studying. Universities should connect food assistance with housing, financial and mental-health support, sustain outreach throughout students' studies, and use student feedback to improve provision. Support should be judged by whether students can obtain food that meets their needs within the practical demands of university life.
