> **Teaching simulation:** This manuscript uses fictional study data and fictional comparison cards [R1] and [R2] supplied for a writing exercise. It reports no real human-participant research.

## Introduction

Replacing a classroom routine requires an explicit judgement about the learning it should support. For lower-secondary science, an immediate advantage in conceptual performance and an advantage in conceptual transfer weeks later answer different teaching questions. Retrieval asks pupils to reconstruct an explanation before consulting a worked example; rereading keeps that explanation available. The educational question concerns whether the opportunity to reconstruct supports transfer after instruction, when choosing a routine is intended to serve durable learning.

The supplied comparison studies support investigating retrieval while leaving this question open. A single-session university laboratory experiment found an advantage in delayed factual recall after individually randomised retrieval practice [R1]. A three-week school comparison found higher immediate conceptual performance under retrieval, but teachers selected their routine and no delayed test was administered [R2]. Factual recall and conceptual transfer concern different outcomes, while teacher choice leaves the school comparison open to selection differences. Together, these studies motivate a classroom comparison with random allocation and a delayed conceptual outcome; they do not establish that benefits already observed will generalise to that combination.

We examined whether a six-week low-stakes retrieval routine improved delayed conceptual transfer compared with rereading worked examples. Three schools participated, each contributing eight classes; classes were randomly allocated within schools, with 12 per condition. Both routines used the same content and 15 minutes of scheduled lesson time, followed by the same teacher explanation. Retrieval pupils attempted an explanation before checking the worked example, whereas rereading pupils studied it throughout. These procedures were planned before outcomes were examined. Delayed conceptual transfer was the primary outcome, and immediate conceptual transfer supplied a secondary assessment of learning nearer to instruction.

## Discussion

The comparison leaves retrieval's superiority for delayed conceptual transfer unresolved. Three weeks after the intervention, the standardised adjusted difference favoured retrieval, d = 0.08, with a 95% confidence interval (CI) of [-0.07, 0.23]. This interval accommodates little difference as well as a benefit relevant to the teaching decision. No equivalence margin was prespecified, so the estimate does not establish equivalence. With delayed transfer as the replacement criterion, the evidence provides insufficient grounds for replacing rereading across lessons on the expectation of a durable advantage.

Retrieval improved the secondary immediate outcome, d = 0.24, 95% CI [0.09, 0.39]. This agrees in direction with the school comparison's immediate conceptual finding [R2], while allocation by class within schools strengthens the present comparison against teacher selection. The analysis respected the class as the randomisation unit and accounted for school and baseline performance. The delayed estimate was numerically smaller, but the difference between effects was not tested. The immediate result therefore supports investigating how an early advantage might be retained; it does not establish either retention or subsequent loss of that advantage. The positive delayed recall result from the university laboratory [R1] concerns a different outcome and setting. It neither resolves the present transfer question nor identifies pupil age as the reason for the different findings.

The implementation records help locate the instructional opportunity. Pupils attempted an explanation before checking the example in 81% of scheduled retrieval activities. This indicates that reconstruction was invited and attempted in most activities; correctness and depth were not assessed by this record. Effortful reconstruction, proposed in the school comparison [R2], remains a candidate explanation rather than an observed mediator. An attempted explanation could differ in its quality while still meeting the recorded completion criterion. The conceptual distinction matters because changing an activity does not by itself specify the learning process it produces. Likewise, pupils' greater reported ease during rereading describes an experience, rather than a validated measure of learning or motivation. Activity, experience and durable transfer therefore require separate educational judgements.

Teachers may retain the retrieval routine where the immediate learning purpose fits the lesson and its preparation demand is manageable. That recommendation follows from the immediate advantage and a specific instructional objective; it does not assume the routines are interchangeable for delayed learning. Teacher-recorded median preparation was 10 minutes per lesson for retrieval and 6 for rereading. These descriptive records identify a practical demand to consider alongside the intended outcome, but without an inferential comparison they do not rank the routines' cost-effectiveness. Schools should keep delayed conceptual transfer central when considering broader replacement, and teachers should assess success against the learning outcome rather than pupils' sense of ease. Subsequent work should investigate how to retain the immediate advantage while keeping delayed transfer as the primary criterion.

The study's scope and missing outcomes also constrain the decision. Of 480 enrolled pupils, 456 completed both post-tests, with 12 missing in each condition. Balanced counts do not show that missingness was ignorable; differences in which pupils were missing could affect the comparison. Evidence comes from three schools and the specified six-week routine, limiting claims about other instructional settings. The randomised allocation and common content, lesson time and subsequent explanation support the comparison within this scope, while leaving its longer-term and wider applicability open.

## Conclusion

For this lower-secondary science comparison, an advantage in delayed conceptual transfer remains unresolved: the estimate includes both little difference and a relevant benefit. The immediate advantage supports retaining retrieval when short-term learning is the explicit purpose and preparation demands fit the lesson. Its educational value should be judged by the intended outcome, with delayed transfer retaining priority in decisions to replace the existing routine. Attempt completion and reported ease help describe instruction, but durable superiority requires evidence from the delayed learning outcome itself.
