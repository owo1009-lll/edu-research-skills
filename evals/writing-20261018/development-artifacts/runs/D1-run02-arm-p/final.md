> **Teaching simulation:** This manuscript uses fictional study data and fictional comparison cards [R1] and [R2] supplied for a writing exercise. It reports no real human-participant research.

## Introduction

Replacing a classroom routine requires an explicit judgement about the learning it should support. For lower-secondary science, an immediate advantage in conceptual performance and an advantage in conceptual transfer weeks later answer different teaching questions. Retrieval asks pupils to reconstruct an explanation before consulting a worked example; rereading keeps that explanation available. If replacement is intended to support durable learning, the central question is whether this opportunity to reconstruct improves delayed conceptual transfer.

The supplied comparison studies support investigating retrieval while leaving this question open. A single-session university laboratory experiment found an advantage in delayed factual recall after individually randomised retrieval practice [R1]. A three-week school comparison found higher immediate conceptual performance under retrieval, but teachers selected their routine and no delayed test was administered [R2]. Factual recall and conceptual transfer concern different outcomes, while teacher choice leaves the school comparison open to selection differences. Together, these studies motivate a classroom comparison with random allocation and a delayed conceptual outcome; they do not establish that benefits already observed will generalise to that combination.

We examined whether a six-week low-stakes retrieval routine improved delayed conceptual transfer compared with rereading worked examples. Three schools participated, each contributing eight classes; classes were randomly allocated within schools, with 12 per condition. Both routines used the same content and 15 minutes of scheduled lesson time, followed by the same teacher explanation. Retrieval pupils attempted an explanation before checking the worked example, whereas rereading pupils studied it throughout. These procedures were planned before outcomes were examined. Delayed conceptual transfer was the primary outcome, and immediate conceptual transfer supplied a secondary assessment of learning nearer to instruction.

## Discussion

The comparison leaves retrieval's superiority for delayed conceptual transfer unresolved. Three weeks after the intervention, the standardised adjusted difference favoured retrieval, d = 0.08, with a 95% confidence interval (CI) of [-0.07, 0.23]. This interval accommodates little difference as well as a benefit relevant to the teaching decision. No equivalence margin was prespecified, so the estimate does not establish equivalence. With delayed transfer as the replacement criterion, the evidence provides insufficient grounds for replacing rereading across lessons on the expectation of a durable advantage.

We found an immediate benefit of retrieval on the secondary conceptual-transfer outcome, d = 0.24, 95% CI [0.09, 0.39]. This agrees in direction with the school comparison's immediate conceptual finding [R2], while allocation by class within schools strengthens the present comparison against teacher selection. The analysis respected the class as the randomisation unit and accounted for school and baseline performance. The delayed estimate was numerically smaller, but the difference between effects was not tested. The immediate result supports investigating how an early advantage might be retained; it does not establish either retention or subsequent loss of that advantage.

The delayed factual-recall advantage in the university laboratory [R1] concerns a different outcome and setting. Its relation to this study is therefore complementary: it addresses recalling facts after a delay, while our comparison addresses conceptual transfer within a classroom routine. The contrast between findings cannot identify pupil age as a causal moderator. The present study adds a randomised classroom comparison to this evidence, making delayed conceptual transfer an explicit test of the proposed instructional benefit.

Pupils attempted an explanation before checking the example in 81% of scheduled retrieval activities. This identifies an opportunity for reconstruction that pupils took up in most activities; the record does not establish the correctness or depth of their attempts. Effortful reconstruction, proposed in the school comparison [R2], remains a candidate explanation rather than an observed mediator. Attempts of different quality could meet the same completion criterion. Likewise, greater reported ease during rereading describes pupils' experience, rather than a validated measure of learning or motivation. These distinctions locate the educational task: teachers need to judge the learning a routine supports, alongside how it is enacted and experienced.

### Instructional implications

Teachers may retain retrieval where the lesson has an explicit short-term conceptual-learning purpose and its preparation demand is manageable. The immediate advantage provides a reason for that bounded use. Teacher-recorded median preparation was 10 minutes per lesson for retrieval and 6 for rereading, a descriptive comparison without an inferential test. Preparation time alone cannot establish which routine is more cost-effective; its practical importance depends on the learning outcome teachers seek and the preparation demand they can accommodate. Schools should keep delayed conceptual transfer central when considering broader replacement, and teachers should assess learning against that intended outcome rather than pupils' sense of ease.

### Limitations and scope

Of 480 enrolled pupils, 456 completed both post-tests, with 12 missing in each condition. Balanced counts do not show that missingness was ignorable; differences in which pupils were missing could affect the comparison. Evidence comes from three schools and the specified six-week routine, limiting claims about other instructional settings. Randomised allocation and common content, lesson time and subsequent explanation support the comparison within this scope. Addressing missing outcomes and testing wider applicability remain priorities for subsequent investigations.

## Conclusion

For this lower-secondary science comparison, an advantage in delayed conceptual transfer remains unresolved: the estimate includes both little difference and a relevant benefit. The immediate advantage supports retaining retrieval when short-term learning is the explicit purpose and preparation demands fit the lesson. Delayed transfer should remain the main criterion for replacing the existing routine. The next educational task is to investigate how to retain the early advantage, using delayed conceptual transfer to judge whether that aim has been achieved.
