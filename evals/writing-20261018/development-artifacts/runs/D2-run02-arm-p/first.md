### Introduction: Response is not yet answerability

A response can arrive promptly and still leave a student unable to ask why a proposed change would improve the work. We call the availability of a reasoned response to such a question answerability. It is a property of the feedback arrangement: a learner can bring a judgement back into discussion and receive reasons for maintaining or revising it. This does not require teachers to approve every objection. It requires a route through which a recommendation can be questioned and corrected where appropriate.

The optional-feedback service in [C1] recorded faster first responses after adding automated comments, but did not examine opportunities to question them. Our study examines arrangements in which students chose automated or written teacher comments, with a teacher question session available in both channels. The distinction connects feedback with an obligation already placed on students: they must explain and defend their academic decisions.

### Discussion: Keep the route back to reasons

Across eight undergraduate courses at one institution, 126 of 214 students chose automated draft comments and 88 chose written teacher comments. Opening was logged for 117/126 and 82/88 students respectively; these clicks record opening, not attentive reading. Substantive revision was coded in 74/126 and 55/88 final submissions using the same rubric. These records describe activity, without identifying which comments prompted changes. A changed paragraph alone cannot distinguish understanding a comment from accepting it uncritically, or reveal why a student kept an original formulation.

The 16 voluntary interviews give this distinction a practical form. Twelve students described a question they could discuss with a teacher; four wanted to ask but reported timetable clashes. These accounts identify reported opportunities and difficulties rather than their prevalence across the courses. A route that exists on a timetable may still be unavailable to the student who needs it.

In the automated channel, one student had a proposed terminology change reversed by the teacher. A different student in the written-teacher channel persuaded the teacher that an unusual paragraph order served the argument. Both cases show how questioning could lead to reconsideration. The latter also makes the teacher's judgement answerable. The educational value of the arrangement rests on access to reasons and correction, rather than an assumed contrast between insensitive automation and infallible teachers.

Students selected their channels, and no adjusted comparison, causal analysis or attainment test was conducted. The study therefore leaves learning benefits unresolved. The question session already existed before the study. Protecting its purpose includes making the route usable for students whose schedules prevent attendance, without treating this extension as a tested intervention.

### Conclusion: Protect answerability

Institutions should protect a route for questioning feedback in assessed work. Receiving a comment, revising a submission and asking for reasons answer different educational questions. Students' responsibility to justify academic choices should be matched by access to reasons for consequential judgements. Choosing a delivery channel should preserve that opportunity, including the possibility that a teacher's recommendation needs correction.
