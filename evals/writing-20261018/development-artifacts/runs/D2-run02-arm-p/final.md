### Introduction: Response is not yet answerability

A response can arrive promptly and still leave a student unable to ask why a proposed change would improve the work. We call the availability of a reasoned response to such a question answerability. It is a property of the feedback arrangement: a learner can bring a judgement back into discussion and receive reasons for maintaining or revising it. This does not require teachers to approve every objection. It requires a route through which a recommendation can be questioned and corrected where appropriate.

The optional-feedback service in [C1], at another institution, recorded faster first responses after adding automated comments, but did not examine opportunities to question them. Our study examines arrangements in which students chose automated or written teacher comments, with a teacher question session available in both channels. The distinction connects feedback with an obligation already placed on students: they must explain and defend their academic decisions.

### Discussion: Keep the route back to reasons

Across eight undergraduate courses at one institution, 126 of 214 students chose automated draft comments and 88 chose written teacher comments. Opening was logged for 117/126 and 82/88 students respectively; these clicks record opening, not attentive reading. Substantive revision was coded in 74/126 and 55/88 final submissions using the same rubric. The records do not link particular comments to revisions. Revision counts cannot reveal whether students understood comments, accepted them uncritically, or had reasons for retaining their original wording.

The 16 voluntary interviews give this distinction a practical form. Twelve interviewees described a question they could discuss with a teacher; four wanted to ask but reported timetable clashes. These accounts identify reported opportunities and difficulties rather than their prevalence across the courses. A route that exists on a timetable may still be unavailable to the student who needs it.

In the automated channel, one student had a proposed terminology change reversed by the teacher. A different student in the written-teacher channel persuaded the teacher that an unusual paragraph order served the argument. Both cases illustrate reconsideration through questioning, with the latter making the teacher's own judgement answerable. These accounts locate answerability in the procedure for requesting reasons and correcting recommendations. Teachers participate in that procedure and remain accountable within it.

Channel self-selection and the absence of adjusted or causal comparisons leave relative learning benefits unresolved; attainment was not measured. The question session already existed before the study. Timetable clashes nevertheless expose a practical gap: institutions should make this route usable for students whose schedules prevent attendance.

### Conclusion: Protect answerability

Institutions should protect a route for questioning feedback in assessed work. Receiving a comment, revising a submission and asking for reasons answer different educational questions. Students' responsibility to justify academic choices should be matched by access to reasons for consequential judgements. Choosing a delivery channel should preserve that opportunity, including the possibility that a teacher's recommendation needs correction.
