### Introduction: Response is not yet answerability

Prompt feedback is valuable, but receiving a comment does not ensure that a student can question its relevance. We call this possibility answerability: a student can ask why a comment matters and receive an accountable explanation, including a correction where appropriate. Answerability belongs to the feedback arrangement; it does not require teachers to accept every objection.

The optional-feedback service in [C1] recorded faster first responses after adding automated comments. It did not examine opportunities to question comments. Our study examines the distinction between receiving feedback and asking for reasons where students chose automated or written teacher comments, with teacher question sessions available in both channels. The educational premise is that students expected to justify their decisions should have access to reasons for consequential judgements.

### Discussion: Keep the route back to reasons

In eight undergraduate courses at one institution, 126 of 214 students chose automated comments and 88 chose written teacher comments. Logs recorded comment opening for 117/126 and 82/88 students, respectively, rather than attentive reading. Substantive revision was coded in 74/126 and 55/88 final submissions using the same rubric. These counts describe revisions, without identifying whether particular comments prompted them. A revised paragraph also says little about whether students understood a comment or could defend retaining the original.

Voluntary interviews with 16 students, nine from the automated channel and seven from the written-teacher channel, made that distinction concrete. One student receiving automated comments had a terminology change reversed by a teacher; a different student receiving teacher comments persuaded the teacher that an unusual paragraph order served the argument. These cases show how a judgement can be brought back into discussion and corrected. Teachers provided this route in the studied arrangement, but answerability is not an attribute unique to teachers.

Twelve interviewees described a question they could discuss with a teacher; four wanted to ask but reported timetable clashes. A scheduled session was therefore not accessible to everyone who wanted it. These voluntary accounts describe opportunities and obstacles rather than their prevalence across all students.

Students selected their channels, no adjusted group comparison or causal analysis was performed, and attainment was not assessed. The record supports neither a learning benefit nor a ranking of delivery modes. The question sessions predated this study. The practical task is to protect that existing route and make it usable by students whose schedules prevent attendance.

### Conclusion: Protect answerability

Institutions should protect a route for questioning feedback in assessed work. This commitment follows from requiring students to explain and defend their academic choices: consequential judgements about their work should also be open to requests for reasons. Comment delivery, revision and the opportunity to question a judgement are distinct features of feedback. Choosing a delivery channel should preserve access to that discussion, including the possibility that a recommendation needs correction.
