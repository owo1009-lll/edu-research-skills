# Rationale

## Author's argument, reconstructed before drafting

The supplied author draft asks what makes a feedback judgement answerable, beyond simply delivering a response. Its central concept is a property of the feedback arrangement: students can request reasons and receive an accountable explanation, including correction where appropriate. Students' obligation to justify their own academic decisions grounds the author's normative claim that institutions **should** protect an accessible route for questioning consequential feedback in assessed work. That claim does not depend on demonstrating a learning effect. The existing headings and their functions will be retained.

The educational contribution is a distinction among comment delivery, revision and access to reasons, made concrete through different students' experiences in both channels and the timetable barrier. [C1] addresses response speed only. It supplies a comparison in what arrangements record, not evidence against automated feedback. No sources will be added or independently verified; the manuscript is a teaching simulation using fictional supplied material.

## What the record answers

- Eight undergraduate courses at one institution offered two self-selected channels, both with an existing teacher question session: automated comments (126 students) and written teacher comments (88); total 214.
- Opening was logged for 117/126 and 82/88. Opening does not identify attentive reading.
- Substantive revision, coded with the same rubric, occurred in 74/126 and 55/88 final submissions. The record does not attribute a particular revision to a comment. There was no adjusted comparison, causal analysis, attainment test or confidence measure.
- Sixteen voluntary interviewees (nine automated; seven written-teacher) provide reported experiences, not population prevalence. Twelve described a discussable question; four reported timetable clashes.
- Two distinct students illustrate reconsideration: the automated-channel terminology recommendation was reversed by a teacher; a student in the written-teacher channel defended an unusual paragraph order to the teacher. These cases support answerability across both initial comment sources; they do not establish an inherent human advantage.

## Evidence supporting and constraining the argument

Supporting material: the stated educational reciprocity premise; shared pre-existing sessions; reported discussions; correction of recommendations originating in both channels. Constraining material: timetable barriers; voluntary interviews; channel self-selection; one institution; opening rather than reading logs; no comment-to-revision attribution or measured learning/confidence outcome. Both the accessible route and the barrier will remain visible. The normative judgement retains its force while empirical causal and universal claims are corrected.
