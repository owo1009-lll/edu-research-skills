### Introduction: Response is not yet answerability

A response can arrive promptly and still leave a student unable to ask why a proposed change would improve the work. We call the availability of a reasoned response to that question answerability. It is a property of the feedback arrangement: students can bring a judgement back into discussion and receive reasons for maintaining or revising it. This does not require teachers to approve every objection.

The optional-feedback service in [C1], at a different institution, recorded faster first responses after adding automated comments. It did not examine whether students could question them. Our study considers this distinction where students chose automated or written teacher comments and both channels retained a teacher question session. Students' obligation to justify academic decisions gives them a corresponding claim to reasons for consequential judgements.

### Discussion: Keep the route back to reasons

Across eight undergraduate courses at one institution, 126 of 214 students chose automated comments and 88 chose written teacher comments. Opening logs covered 117/126 and 82/88 students, respectively; these record opening, not attentive reading. Substantive revisions were coded in 74/126 and 55/88 final submissions using the same rubric. These counts neither connect particular comments to revisions nor demonstrate improved revision capacity. A changed paragraph does not establish whether a student understood a recommendation or had reasons to reject it.

Interviews with 16 volunteers, nine from the automated channel and seven from the written-teacher channel, give questioning a practical form. One automated-channel student had a proposed terminology change reversed by the teacher. A different student in the written-teacher channel persuaded the teacher that an unusual paragraph order served the argument. In both accounts, a recommendation could itself be reconsidered. The teacher session provided this route in both channels; answerability belongs to the arrangement rather than uniquely to a human comment's author.

Twelve interviewees described a question they could discuss with a teacher; four wanted to ask but reported timetable clashes. These are accounts of opportunity and difficulty, not estimates for all students. A session on the timetable can remain unavailable to someone who needs it. Protecting answerability therefore includes making the existing route usable when attendance clashes with students' schedules.

Students selected their channels, no adjusted comparison or causal analysis was performed, and attainment was not measured. These records cannot establish that either channel improved learning. The question session preceded this study: the proposal protects and improves access to an existing procedure rather than attributing a learning benefit to a new intervention.

### Conclusion: Protect answerability

Institutions should protect a route for questioning feedback in assessed work. Requiring students to justify their academic choices entails giving them access to reasons for consequential judgements, including correction where appropriate. The records distinguish delivery, revision and the opportunity to ask for reasons. Choosing how comments arrive should preserve that opportunity, including when the original comment comes from a teacher.
