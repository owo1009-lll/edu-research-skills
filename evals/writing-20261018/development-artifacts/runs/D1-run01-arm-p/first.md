> Teaching simulation: The study, data and comparison cards are fictional. This manuscript illustrates writing from supplied evidence and does not report research involving human participants.

## Introduction

Replacing a classroom routine involves deciding which learning outcome should justify the change. In lower-secondary science, a retrieval routine can ask pupils to attempt an explanation before consulting a worked example, whereas rereading keeps that explanation available throughout. The first arrangement creates an opportunity to reconstruct an explanation. Whether this opportunity improves conceptual transfer after instruction has ended is a separate educational question. If durable learning is the purpose of replacement, delayed conceptual transfer should be the principal criterion for judging the routine.

The supplied comparisons address related outcomes without resolving this question. A single-session university laboratory experiment found an advantage for retrieval on delayed factual recall [R1]. A school study found higher immediate conceptual performance with retrieval, but teachers chose their routine and there was no delayed test [R2]. Together, these comparisons motivate examining retrieval in classrooms while distinguishing recall from conceptual transfer and immediate performance from performance after a delay. They do not establish that the advantage extends to delayed conceptual transfer in lower-secondary science.

We examined a six-week low-stakes retrieval routine against rereading worked examples in three schools, with eight classes each and 480 enrolled pupils. Classes were randomly allocated within schools, with 12 classes per condition. Both conditions received the same content, 15 minutes of scheduled lesson time and the same subsequent teacher explanation. Analyses used the class as the randomization unit and accounted for school and baseline performance. The primary outcome was conceptual transfer three weeks after the intervention; immediate conceptual transfer was secondary. This design asks whether changing pupils' initial engagement with the example improves the outcome needed to justify replacing the routine.

## Discussion

The study leaves the delayed conceptual-transfer benefit unresolved. The standardised adjusted difference favoured retrieval, d = 0.08, with a 95% confidence interval (CI) of [-0.07, 0.23]. This interval accommodates little difference and a benefit relevant to the teaching question. With no prespecified equivalence margin, the result also provides no basis for treating the routines as equivalent. Decisions to replace rereading for durable conceptual learning therefore require stronger evidence on the delayed outcome.

Retrieval improved immediate conceptual transfer, the secondary outcome, with d = 0.24, 95% CI [0.09, 0.39]. The direction agrees with the immediate conceptual-performance advantage in the school comparison [R2]. Random allocation of classes in the present study strengthens this comparison relative to teacher choice of condition. The immediate result gives a reason to investigate how an early advantage might be retained. The smaller delayed estimate does not establish that the effect declined: the difference between the immediate and delayed effects was not tested.

The delayed factual-recall advantage in the laboratory comparison [R1] addresses a different outcome. Remembering facts after a single session and transferring concepts after a classroom routine are distinct instructional achievements. Our result therefore supplements that evidence with a classroom transfer question rather than overturning it. The studies also differ in setting, duration and participants. Their contrast cannot identify pupil age as the cause of different results, or establish a boundary beyond which retrieval ceases to help.

Effortful reconstruction, proposed in the school comparison [R2], is a possible explanation for the immediate advantage: retrieval asks pupils to formulate an explanation before checking it. Pupils attempted this in 81% of scheduled retrieval activities. That record shows an opportunity to reconstruct was taken, not whether the explanation was correct or developed in depth. No mediation analysis was collected to establish the proposed pathway. Greater reported ease in rereading concerns the experience of the activity; it was not a validated learning measure. An instructional attempt, experienced ease and durable learning thus need separate evidential treatment when judging a routine.

Teachers may retain retrieval where immediate conceptual learning is the lesson's purpose and preparation requirements are manageable. Median preparation was 10 minutes per lesson for retrieval and 6 for rereading. These descriptive records identify a resource consideration, without an inferential comparison or a basis for ranking cost-effectiveness. Equal scheduled lesson time therefore does not remove the need to consider teacher preparation. Schools considering replacement to improve delayed transfer should keep that outcome central, and teachers should judge learning against the intended outcome rather than pupils' reports of ease. The immediate advantage supports a conditional instructional use, not a claim that durable transfer has been secured.

The study's scope and incomplete follow-up also constrain interpretation. Of 480 enrolled pupils, 456 completed both post-tests, with 12 missing in each condition. Balanced counts do not establish that missingness was ignorable; the relationship between missing outcomes and learning remains unresolved. The three schools and the three-week delayed test limit conclusions about other settings and longer retention periods. Further classroom studies should address missing follow-up and examine delayed transfer over longer periods, while retaining the distinction between attempted and productive reconstruction.

## Conclusion

The six-week retrieval routine did not establish an improvement in delayed conceptual transfer over rereading, while leaving a relevant benefit possible. Its immediate advantage supports retaining it where the short-term learning purpose and preparation demands fit the lesson. The educational judgement is to align evidence with the outcome that motivates adoption: an attempt to reconstruct and a less effortless activity do not themselves establish durable learning. Delayed transfer should remain the criterion for replacement intended to improve lasting conceptual learning.
