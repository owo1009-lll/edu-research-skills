## Introduction

Replacing a familiar teaching routine requires a judgment about the learning it is intended to support. In lower-secondary science, an immediate improvement in conceptual transfer can justify a lesson activity, but a decision to replace an existing routine for durable learning should depend on delayed transfer. Retrieval asks pupils to reconstruct an explanation before viewing a worked example; rereading keeps that explanation available. This difference changes the opportunity for reconstruction. Whether it changes what pupils can subsequently transfer is an empirical question.

The supplied comparison studies address different parts of this question. A single-session, individually randomised university laboratory study found a retrieval advantage in delayed factual recall [R1]. A three-week school study reported higher immediate conceptual performance under retrieval, although teachers selected their routine and no delayed test was administered [R2]. The former establishes evidence about remembering facts in its setting; the latter connects retrieval with conceptual performance in classrooms. Neither comparison establishes whether retrieval improves delayed conceptual transfer in a class-randomised school comparison. Bringing these dimensions together matters because evidence for one outcome or time point cannot settle a decision organised around another.

We examined whether a six-week, low-stakes retrieval routine improved conceptual transfer three weeks later relative to rereading worked examples. Classes were randomly allocated within three schools, with 12 classes per condition. Both conditions used identical content and 15 minutes of scheduled lesson time, followed by the same teacher explanation. Retrieval pupils attempted an explanation before checking the example; rereading pupils studied it throughout. The planned comparison therefore addresses the instructional routine within otherwise shared lesson arrangements, with delayed transfer as the primary outcome.

## Discussion

The evidence leaves the delayed-transfer advantage unresolved. The standardised adjusted difference favoured retrieval, d = 0.08, with a 95% confidence interval (CI) of [-0.07, 0.23]. This interval includes little difference and a benefit relevant to the teaching decision. With no prespecified equivalence margin, the result provides no basis for treating the routines as equivalent. Delayed conceptual transfer should therefore remain the criterion for replacing rereading; an uncertain answer to that question should not be converted into a choice based solely on the secondary outcome.

The result for immediate conceptual transfer nevertheless supports a distinct teaching purpose. Retrieval had a positive adjusted difference, d = 0.24, 95% CI [0.09, 0.39], on this secondary outcome. This finding is consistent in direction with the immediate conceptual-performance advantage in the school comparison, where teachers chose their conditions [R2]. Class allocation in our study strengthens the basis for evaluating the routine beyond teacher selection. However, the difference between immediate and delayed effects was not tested. The smaller delayed estimate motivates investigation of retaining an early advantage; it does not establish that an advantage diminished over time.

The delayed factual-recall benefit in the university laboratory comparison is also relevant, but it answers a different learning question [R1]. Recalling facts after a single session and transferring concepts after a classroom routine are distinct criteria of success. Our findings add a delayed classroom transfer estimate to these comparisons without establishing a contradiction with the laboratory result. Differences in setting, task and instructional duration are dimensions for future comparison, not demonstrated explanations. In particular, these studies provide no basis for attributing their results to pupil age.

The process record locates the distinction between offering reconstruction and achieving productive reconstruction. Pupils attempted an explanation before checking the example in 81% of scheduled retrieval activities. This documents implementation of the opportunity to reconstruct, while leaving correctness and depth unknown. Effortful reconstruction was proposed in the school comparison, but its mediating role was not measured there [R2]. Evaluating this explanation requires evidence about what pupils produce as well as whether they participate. Greater reported ease during rereading describes the activity experience; the item was not a validated measure of learning. Teachers should evaluate ease alongside evidence on the intended outcome rather than use it to decide which routine teaches more effectively.

For practice, teachers may retain retrieval when immediate conceptual transfer serves the lesson's purpose and the preparation requirement is acceptable. Recorded median preparation was 10 minutes per lesson for retrieval and 6 for rereading. These descriptive medians make workload relevant to that conditional decision; without an inferential comparison or a cost-effectiveness evaluation, they do not establish which routine offers better value. Schools considering general replacement should require stronger evidence on delayed transfer. Because retrieval already included example checking and the subsequent teacher explanation, development work should examine the quality of pupils' explanations within that supported sequence.

### Limitations and future research

The analysis treated classes as the randomisation unit and accounted for school and baseline performance. Of 480 enrolled pupils, 456 completed both post-tests, with 12 missing pupils in each condition. Equal missing counts do not establish ignorable missingness, so attrition remains relevant to the estimates. The evidence also concerns three schools and the specified intervention and follow-up periods. A subsequent study should assess explanation quality alongside delayed transfer and directly evaluate whether changes to the routine preserve an immediate benefit. Such evidence would connect implementation quality to learning more closely than attempt completion alone.

## Conclusion

This classroom comparison supports retrieval for an immediate conceptual transfer purpose while leaving its advantage for transfer three weeks later unresolved. Decisions about replacing rereading should retain delayed transfer as the main criterion. Conditional use of retrieval can still be justified where the short-term purpose and preparation demand fit the lesson. The next instructional question is how to preserve an early advantage, assessed through the intended delayed outcome rather than participation or ease alone.
