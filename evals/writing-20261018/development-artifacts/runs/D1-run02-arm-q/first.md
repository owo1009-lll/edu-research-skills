> **Teaching simulation:** This manuscript uses fictional study materials and fictional comparison cards [R1] and [R2]. It reports no real research or human participant data.

# Introduction

Replacing a classroom routine requires a clear account of the learning it is intended to improve. For lower-secondary science, the decision examined here concerns conceptual transfer that remains available after instruction. Retrieval asks pupils to reconstruct an explanation before consulting a worked example; rereading keeps that explanation available throughout. This distinction offers a reason to investigate retrieval, but the activity itself cannot stand as evidence of durable learning. A routine that supports performance immediately may still leave its contribution to later transfer unresolved.

The comparison evidence addresses parts of this problem. A single-session, individually randomised university laboratory study favoured retrieval for delayed factual recall [R1]. A three-week school study found higher immediate conceptual performance under retrieval, although teachers selected their routine and no delayed test was administered [R2]. Together, these studies motivate asking whether retrieval supports delayed conceptual transfer in classrooms. They supply neither a direct answer nor evidence that age explains differences between settings.

We examined a six-week low-stakes retrieval routine in three schools with 480 enrolled pupils. Classes were randomly allocated within schools, with 12 classes per condition. Both conditions used the same content and 15 minutes of lesson time, followed by the same teacher explanation. Retrieval pupils attempted an explanation before checking the example; rereading pupils studied it throughout. The primary outcome was conceptual transfer three weeks after the intervention. Immediate conceptual transfer provided a secondary view of learning. By matching instructional resources and assessing both occasions, the study connects the choice of routine to the outcome that should govern replacement.

# Discussion

The delayed result leaves retrieval's advantage unresolved. The adjusted difference favoured retrieval, but its magnitude was uncertain (d = 0.08, 95% CI [-0.07, 0.23]). This interval accommodates little difference and a benefit relevant to the teaching question. With no prespecified equivalence margin, the findings provide no basis for treating the routines as equivalent. They also leave insufficient grounds for replacing rereading on the premise that retrieval has established a delayed-transfer advantage.

Immediate conceptual transfer favoured retrieval (d = 0.24, 95% CI [0.09, 0.39]). This secondary result gives teachers a reason to consider the routine when immediate learning is the instructional purpose, and researchers a reason to investigate how to sustain an early advantage. The immediate and delayed effects were not directly compared, so their numerical ordering does not demonstrate that an effect declined. The distinction between these results concerns what each assessment establishes, rather than a tested trajectory of retention.

This pattern refines the question raised by the comparison studies. The immediate finding is consistent in direction with the school comparison [R2], while random allocation here addresses teacher choice as a source of differences between conditions. The delayed factual-recall benefit in the university laboratory [R1] concerns another outcome and setting. It cannot serve as a direct benchmark for delayed conceptual transfer or establish that retrieval works differently by pupil age. The present study adds a classroom comparison of delayed transfer under matched teaching conditions, but the uncertainty around that estimate remains part of its contribution.

The process evidence identifies where explanation should begin. Pupils attempted an explanation in 81% of scheduled retrieval activities, demonstrating that opportunities for reconstruction were frequently taken up. Attempt completion does not establish the correctness or depth of reconstruction. The effortful-reconstruction account proposed in the school comparison [R2] therefore remains an explanation to investigate, rather than a mechanism established by these records. A useful next step is to examine the quality of pupils' explanations in relation to later transfer. Adding an opportunity to check an example is not itself a new response to this problem: checking was already part of the routine.

Reported ease and teacher preparation inform different parts of the instructional decision. Rereading pupils reported greater ease, but this item was not a validated learning measure. Teachers should judge a routine against its intended learning outcome rather than use comfortable participation as a substitute. Median preparation was 10 minutes per lesson for retrieval and 6 for rereading. This descriptive comparison identifies a preparation demand to consider; it does not establish relative cost-effectiveness. Teachers may retain retrieval where its immediate learning purpose and preparation requirements fit the lesson, while keeping delayed transfer as the criterion for a broader decision to replace the existing routine.

The study's scope also shapes that decision. Analysis treated the class as the randomisation unit and accounted for school and baseline performance, aligning inference with the allocation. Of the enrolled pupils, 456 completed both post-tests, with 12 missing in each condition. Equal counts do not establish that missingness was unrelated to learning outcomes. The three-school setting also limits what these findings alone can establish about other settings. Further classroom work should address missing outcomes and examine whether an immediate advantage can be retained on delayed conceptual-transfer assessments.

# Conclusion

The six-week routine supported immediate conceptual transfer, while its advantage three weeks later remains unresolved. The educational contribution is to keep the intended outcome central when interpreting a promising activity: reconstruction opportunities, reported ease and delayed learning answer different questions. Teachers have a conditional reason to use retrieval for an immediate learning purpose, subject to its preparation demands. Replacing an existing routine for durable transfer requires clearer evidence on that outcome; the positive immediate finding makes securing such evidence a substantive next step.
