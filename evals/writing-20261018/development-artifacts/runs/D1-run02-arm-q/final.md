> **Teaching simulation:** This manuscript uses fictional study materials and fictional comparison cards [R1] and [R2]. It reports no real research or human participant data.

# Introduction

Replacing a classroom routine requires a clear account of the learning it is intended to improve. For lower-secondary science, the decision examined here concerns conceptual transfer that remains available after instruction. Retrieval asks pupils to reconstruct an explanation before consulting a worked example; rereading keeps that explanation available throughout. This distinction offers a reason to investigate retrieval, but the activity itself cannot stand as evidence of durable learning. A routine that supports performance immediately may still leave its contribution to later transfer unresolved.

The comparison evidence addresses parts of this problem. A single-session, individually randomised university laboratory study favoured retrieval for delayed factual recall [R1]. A three-week school study found higher immediate conceptual performance under retrieval, although teachers selected their routine and no delayed test was administered [R2]. Together, these studies motivate asking whether retrieval supports delayed conceptual transfer in classrooms. The unresolved issue is whether the routine supports the delayed learning outcome that would justify replacing an existing classroom practice.

We examined a six-week low-stakes retrieval routine in three schools with 480 enrolled pupils. Classes were randomly allocated within schools, with 12 classes per condition. Both conditions used the same content and 15 minutes of lesson time, followed by the same teacher explanation. Retrieval pupils attempted an explanation before checking the example; rereading pupils studied it throughout. The primary outcome was conceptual transfer three weeks after the intervention. Immediate conceptual transfer provided a secondary view of learning. By holding content, lesson time and subsequent explanation constant, the study connects the choice of routine to the outcome that should govern replacement.

# Discussion

The delayed result leaves retrieval's advantage unresolved. The adjusted difference favoured retrieval, but its magnitude was uncertain (d = 0.08, 95% CI [-0.07, 0.23]). This interval accommodates little difference and a benefit relevant to the teaching question. With no prespecified equivalence margin, the findings provide no basis for treating the routines as equivalent. They also leave insufficient grounds for replacing rereading on the premise that retrieval has established a delayed-transfer advantage.

Immediate conceptual transfer favoured retrieval (d = 0.24, 95% CI [0.09, 0.39]). This secondary result gives teachers a reason to consider the routine when immediate learning is the instructional purpose, and researchers a reason to investigate how to sustain an early advantage. The immediate and delayed effects were not directly compared, so their numerical ordering does not demonstrate that an effect declined. The distinction between these results concerns what each assessment establishes, rather than a tested trajectory of retention.

This pattern refines the question raised by the comparison studies. The immediate finding is consistent in direction with the school comparison [R2], while random allocation here addresses teacher choice as a source of differences between conditions. The delayed factual-recall benefit in the university laboratory [R1] concerns another outcome and setting. It cannot serve as a direct benchmark for delayed conceptual transfer or establish that retrieval works differently by pupil age. The present study adds a randomised classroom comparison of delayed transfer while keeping content, lesson time and subsequent explanation constant. Its delayed estimate leaves the replacement decision open.

The process evidence helps locate the instructional contrast. Retrieval placed an explanation attempt before example checking, with teacher explanation still available afterward. Pupils completed an attempt in 81% of scheduled retrieval activities, recording an opportunity for reconstruction without establishing its correctness or depth. The effortful-reconstruction account proposed in the school comparison [R2] remains a candidate explanation. These records leave open whether pupils used that opportunity productively and how productive reconstruction relates to durable transfer. Improving the routine therefore requires attention to what happens within the attempt; simply adding example checking would repeat a step already built into its design.

Reported ease and teacher preparation inform different parts of the instructional decision. Rereading pupils reported greater ease, but this item was not a validated learning measure. Teachers should judge a routine against its intended learning outcome rather than use comfortable participation as a substitute. Median preparation was 10 minutes per lesson for retrieval and 6 for rereading. These descriptive medians, without an inferential test, inform planning but provide no cost-effectiveness ranking. Teachers may retain retrieval where its immediate learning purpose and preparation requirements fit the lesson, while keeping delayed transfer as the criterion for a broader decision to replace the existing routine.

## Limitations

The study's scope also shapes that decision. Analysis treated the class as the randomisation unit and accounted for school and baseline performance, aligning inference with the allocation. Of the enrolled pupils, 456 completed both post-tests, with 12 missing in each condition. Equal counts do not establish that missingness was unrelated to learning outcomes. The three-school setting also limits what these findings alone can establish about other settings. Further classroom work should address missing outcomes and examine whether an immediate advantage can be retained on delayed conceptual transfer assessments.

# Conclusion

The six-week routine supported immediate conceptual transfer, while its advantage three weeks later remains unresolved. The educational contribution is to keep the intended outcome central when interpreting a promising activity: reconstruction opportunities, reported ease and delayed learning answer different questions. Teachers have a conditional reason to use retrieval for an immediate learning purpose, subject to its preparation demands. Replacing an existing routine for durable transfer requires clearer evidence on that outcome; the positive immediate finding makes securing such evidence a substantive next step.
