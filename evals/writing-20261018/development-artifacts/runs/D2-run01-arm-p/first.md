### Introduction: Response is not yet answerability

A response can arrive promptly and still leave a student unable to ask why a proposed change would improve the work. We call the availability of a reasoned response to such a question answerability. It is a property of the feedback arrangement: a learner can bring a judgement back into discussion and receive reasons for maintaining or revising it. Teachers need not approve every objection, but their judgements must also be open to question.

The optional-feedback service in [C1] recorded faster first responses after adding automated comments. That finding concerns delivery, leaving unanswered whether students could question a comment. Our study examines this distinction in arrangements offering automated or teacher comments alongside the same teacher question session. It connects feedback with an obligation already placed on students: explaining and defending their academic decisions.

### Discussion: Keep the route back to reasons

Across eight undergraduate courses at one institution, 126 of 214 students chose automated draft comments and 88 chose written teacher comments. Opening logs covered 117/126 and 82/88 students, respectively; these record opening, not attentive reading. Substantive revision was coded in 74/126 and 55/88 final submissions using the same rubric. These counts neither identify which comments prompted revisions nor establish improved learning. A changed paragraph does not reveal whether a learner understood a comment, accepted it uncritically or developed a reason for retaining the original version.

Among sixteen voluntary interviewees, twelve described a question they could discuss with a teacher; four wanted to ask but reported timetable clashes. These accounts illustrate opportunities and difficulties, not their prevalence across the student population. One student in the automated channel had a proposed terminology change reversed by a teacher. A different student in the teacher-comment channel persuaded the teacher that an unusual paragraph order served the argument. A reason could be requested, considered and used to correct a recommendation.

The latter case also makes the teacher's own judgement answerable. It supports locating answerability in the arrangement for questioning feedback, rather than treating it as an attribute unique to teachers. The relevant distinction is between obtaining a comment and being able to ask for its reasons, whichever channel generated it. Timetable clashes show why formally offering a session does not ensure access to that discussion.

Students selected their channels, no adjusted group comparison was performed, and attainment was not measured. The study therefore does not establish which channel improves learning. Its practical implication is to make the questioning route usable for students unable to attend the scheduled session. That session predates this study; protecting access extends its existing purpose.

### Conclusion: Protect answerability

Institutions should protect a route for questioning feedback in assessed work. The rationale is that students expected to justify their academic choices need access to reasons for consequential judgements. Delivery, revision and the opportunity to ask for reasons address different educational questions. Choosing a delivery channel should not remove that opportunity.
