## Context and design (Introduction; Methods; B003–B008, B025–B058)

- The question concerns whether a deliberately structured AI tutor can support initial learning of physics content better than an established active-learning class, not whether unrestricted chatbot use improves an entire degree program.
- Harvard's introductory physics course for life-science students enrolled 233 students in fall 2023; the main text identifies 194 eligible students in two groups (96/98). Randomization used existing peer groups of two or three students. Two consecutive weeks used a crossover: one group studied surface tension at home with the tutor while the other attended class; conditions reversed for fluid flow the next week.
- Both conditions used the same worksheet content and learning objectives. In-class instruction used live explanations, peer problem solving and instructor/teaching-assistant support. Instructors were experienced and highly rated. AI students worked individually at home, with a recorded introduction and a custom GPT-4-0613 tutor called PS2 Pal.
- The tutor used expert-written stepwise solutions, question-specific prompts and programmed sequencing, with feedback during problem solving. The authors found prompting alone insufficient to guarantee their intended sequence. The comparison thus changes a package of instructional features, location, pacing and peer interaction.
- Topic-specific pre/post quizzes were administered immediately around each lesson. Two versions alternated; a separate team member designed assessment against learning objectives. Credit rewarded participation, not quiz performance. Delayed retention, sustained course achievement and complex synthesis were not evaluated.

## Findings (Results; B011–B022; supplementary Table S1)

- Reported median post-test scores: AI 4.5 (N=142), classroom 3.5 (N=174); pooled pre-test median 2.75 (N=316). Rank-sum comparison: z=-5.6, p<10^-8. These are reported condition-level score counts; they should not be represented as numbers of distinct independently randomized students. The main text does not fully reconcile them with the 194 eligible students.
- The authors' statement about more than doubling gains uses increments from the pooled pre-test median (1.75 versus 0.75). It is not a general ratio for all kinds of learning. Figure 1 instead plots means with standard-error bars; its bars are not the preceding medians.
- A regression controlling for pre-test, midterm, prior physics knowledge, previous ChatGPT use, topic, test version and time reported a standardized AI coefficient of 0.63, p<10^-8. Errors were clustered by student. Clustering by peer group left the estimate effectively unchanged and p<.001. Supplementary S1 contains 0.63*** inside an OOXML content control, confirmed by reading the XML; an initial simple DOCX extraction omitted the digits. That extraction artifact is resolved, not a missing statistic in the publication. R²=.21. No coefficient interval is supplied in this packet.
- AI activity logs gave a median of 49 minutes; 70% spent under 60 minutes. Classroom instruction was assumed to take 60 minutes after subtracting 15 minutes of testing from the 75-minute session; it was not an individual time-on-task measurement. The study does not isolate self-pacing as the cause of higher scores.
- Single 1–5 agreement items: engagement AI 4.1 (SD .98) versus class 3.6 (SD .92), reported t(311)=-4.5, p<.0001; motivation AI 3.4 (SD 1.0) versus class 3.1 (SD .86), t(311)=-3.4, p<.001. Enjoyment and confidence in learning difficult physics with effort showed no statistically detected differences. The last item was labeled growth mindset by the authors, but was not a multi-item scale here.
- Figure 3 calls its tests dependent t-tests; the reported degrees of freedom and crossover accounting need clarification. Do not silently choose a different test or recompute significance. This question does not establish that the reported direction is false.
- 83% rated tutor explanations as good as or better than instructors' explanations. This is student perception, not an independent accuracy audit. Reported favorable subgroup results do not establish equal effects across subgroups or other institutions.

## Interpretation and prior work (Discussion; B024–B030)

- The authors interpret timely feedback and flexible pacing as plausible advantages of their design; individual components were not independently manipulated. Experienced teachers, common content and an active comparator make the observed instructional contrast meaningful, while the package design limits attribution to one feature.
- In their review, Kestin et al. contrast their results with earlier examples of unguided or unstructured ChatGPT use with limited reflection or weaker performance (Krupp et al., 2023; Forero et al., 2023). Their descriptions support distinguishing implementations. The earlier originals were not read for this packet, so any comparison is attributed to Kestin et al.'s review; it does not establish why studies differ.
- Their educational position is purposeful use of AI for initial engagement with material, potentially freeing classroom time for higher-order collaborative work. They do not recommend replacing all in-person instruction. This combined arrangement is a proposed use, not the intervention evaluated as a complete course.
- Expert content and instructional design were substantial: lesson preparation took days and platform development months. A high-quality recorded introduction was part of the AI condition. Cost savings, equitable access and performance in other settings were not tested.
- The main contribution concerns designed tutoring under these conditions and immediate topic learning. The evidence leaves retention, complex transfer, classroom integration and implementation costs open. Do not invent outside citations, baseline equivalence tests, confidence intervals or later follow-up outcomes.
