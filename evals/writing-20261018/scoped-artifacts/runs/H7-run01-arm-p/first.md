## Discussion

This study locates students' growing appreciation of research in the transition from learning methods to using them in supervised projects. In the integrated undergraduate medical research module, the 8A-Model organizes this interpretation into four paired themes: Attend–Acquire, Accumulate–Assimilate, Apply–Appreciate, and Articulate–Affect. The framework connects acquiring knowledge with recognizing its relevance through participation in research. Although depicted sequentially, these processes can overlap and recur as students revisit concepts in new tasks. Its educational contribution is an account of why research learning may become meaningful after initial instruction, with application providing a setting in which previously disconnected knowledge gains purpose.

Students' accounts give this interpretation practical substance. One participant described early biostatistics and software work as confusing and disconnected (6.1), whereas others recognized their relevance during projects (7.2 and 8.2). Communication with supervisors was itself a learning experience (9.2), and a later-cohort account described accomplishment and using presentation skills during internships (14.3). Such accounts fit the authors' situated-learning interpretation: learners participate in authentic activity within a community of practice, supported by more experienced researchers [26,61]. Articulate–Affect extends the framework toward communicating findings and contributing beyond the course. Contributions to medicine and public health remain an ambition of the model rather than measured outcomes.

The grade pattern converges with these accounts at the transition to project work. Mean research-course grade point averages (GPAs) were 3.01, 2.94 and 2.95 in the first three semesters, followed by 3.35 and 3.58 in the fourth and fifth, on a 0–4 scale. The paper reports an early-versus-later course contrast (p < .01) and a similar pattern within the oldest cohort. However, assessment tasks changed across courses, and the cohorts represented also changed. These grades therefore describe performance on successive course requirements, rather than equivalent tests of research competence. The joint display supports a curriculum-level interpretation of application and appreciation; it does not establish that appreciation caused individual grade improvement.

Delayed appreciation also sharpens the theoretical argument. The authors contrast this pattern with their reading of Kirkpatrick and learning-transfer models, which they characterize as treating appreciation as an immediate response to a learning experience [43,53]. Here, students entered medical education after secondary school and encountered unfamiliar research content across an extended module. Their later recognition of its relevance suggests that evaluation should attend to when learners have opportunities to use knowledge. The 8A-Model directs attention to that timing without requiring appreciation to emerge at the same point for every learner.

Undergraduate medical programs should provide opportunities to conduct supervised research as an integral part of research education [59,60]. The rationale is that authentic projects give students a purpose for applying methods and a context for learning research practices with expert guidance. Teaching should connect earlier content to project decisions and support reflection on experience and existing mental models [63]. This approach retains the module's emphasis on guided participation and purposeful revisiting of content. Its implementation requires continuity between methods courses and project work, together with mentors able to support students' questions and developing judgments.

The study's limitations concern how this developmental interpretation was constructed and where it applies. Three cohort focus groups provided different perspectives on the curriculum, rather than repeated interviews with the same students; narratives were not linked to individual grade changes. Member checking by older students supported the interpretation but did not prospectively validate the model. The single institution and nonexperimental design also leave the contribution of project work relative to other influences unresolved. Longitudinal studies across programs should examine how application, appreciation and assessed competence develop together, and follow research participation, careers and clinical practice. Patient outcomes require separate investigation.

## Conclusion

The 8A-Model interprets undergraduate medical research learning as an iterative movement toward recognizing and using the value of research. Students' accounts and course-stage grade patterns place supervised application at the center of that interpretation. Programs should make supported project work integral to research education and connect it with earlier instruction. The model offers a framework for examining when research becomes meaningful and how learners carry that understanding into practice.
