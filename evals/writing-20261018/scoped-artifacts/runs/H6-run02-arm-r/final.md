*Secondary writing exercise based on Kestin et al. (2025). The original authors conducted the study described below; this text reports no new research.*

## Introduction

Teaching decisions about artificial intelligence (AI) depend on the learning activity the system is designed to support. In their review, Kestin et al. (2025) contrast a deliberately structured tutor with earlier reports of unguided ChatGPT use involving limited reflection or weaker performance. This distinction matters when teachers consider assigning students' first encounter with new material to an AI tutor. The relevant question is whether a carefully designed tutor can improve immediate topic learning compared with a class already using active learning.

The study addressed this question in Harvard's introductory physics course for life-science students. Existing peer groups were randomized to conditions across two consecutive weeks, with conditions reversed for the second topic. Both conditions used the same worksheets and objectives. Classroom students received explanations, peer problem solving and instructor support; AI students studied individually at home with a recorded introduction and a tutor providing expert-written steps and feedback (Kestin et al., 2025). Experienced, highly rated classroom teachers made the comparison consequential: any advantage would be over instruction that already offered substantial human guidance.

## Discussion

The AI package improved immediate quiz performance relative to the active-learning class (Kestin et al., 2025). Median post-test scores were 4.5 in the AI condition and 3.5 in the classroom condition. The advantage remained after adjustment for prior attainment, topic and other covariates, with a standardized coefficient of 0.63. These results establish a role for designed individual tutoring in initial topic learning under the studied conditions, extending the teaching options available alongside an established classroom approach.

The design specifies what that option involved. Expert-written solutions, question-specific prompts and programmed sequencing directed the tutor's responses; prompting alone had not reliably produced the intended sequence. Timely feedback and flexible pacing are plausible contributors to the learning advantage. Yet the conditions also differed in location, peer interaction and the recorded introduction, leaving their separate contributions unresolved. The contrast with unguided use described in Kestin et al.'s (2025) review supports distinguishing instructional implementations when interpreting AI outcomes. It does not establish which design differences explain the results across those studies.

The timing evidence suggests a further question about how learning time might be organized. AI activity logs recorded a median of 49 minutes, whereas classroom instruction was assumed to occupy 60 minutes after testing was removed. This compares logged activity with a scheduled allocation, leaving individual learning efficiency unresolved. Comparable measures of students' time would be needed to evaluate that potential advantage.

The learning evidence also has a specific scope: two topics at one university, assessed immediately after instruction. Retention, complex synthesis and sustained course achievement were not evaluated. Subsequent trials should examine these outcomes in other settings and assess the resources required to reproduce the instructional design. Cost savings and equitable access remain questions for those evaluations.

For teachers able to resource the preparation, the positive learning result supports a pilot using selected introductory lessons, expert solutions, sequenced guidance and a suitable recorded introduction. The original preparation took days for lessons and months for platform development. Kestin et al. (2025) propose using tutoring for initial learning and reserving classroom time for more demanding collaborative work. A pilot should assess immediate and delayed learning before teachers commit to reallocating class time, because that combined course arrangement was not evaluated in the study. This makes the instructional design and its integration into teaching the objects of the adoption decision.

## Conclusion

A structured AI tutor improved immediate topic learning compared with an established active-learning class in the studied introductory physics lessons. The educational contribution is evidence for an effective, deliberately prepared tutoring package against a substantial classroom comparator. It provides a basis for bounded teaching trials that preserve the tested instructional supports and examine whether the immediate advantage carries into retained learning and classroom collaboration.
