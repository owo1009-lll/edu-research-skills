*Secondary writing exercise based on Kestin et al. (2025). The original authors conducted the study described below; this text reports no new research.*

## Introduction

Deciding where artificial intelligence (AI) belongs in teaching requires specifying what students are expected to learn and how the technology supports that work. Kestin et al. (2025) examined a structured AI tutor for students' initial encounter with physics topics. Their review contrasted this approach with earlier examples of unguided ChatGPT use involving limited reflection or weaker performance. That comparison raises a teaching question about the organization of learning: can an AI tutor built around expert guidance outperform an established active-learning class on immediate topic learning?

The study addressed this question in Harvard's introductory physics course for life-science students. Existing peer groups were randomized to conditions in a crossover across two consecutive weeks, with the conditions reversed for the second topic. Both conditions covered the same worksheets and objectives. Classroom students received explanations, peer problem solving and instructor support; AI students worked individually at home with a recorded introduction and a tutor providing sequenced problems and feedback. This comparison places the educational value of a designed learning experience at the center of the decision about AI use.

## Discussion

The structured AI package improved immediate quiz performance relative to the active-learning class (Kestin et al., 2025). Median post-test scores were 4.5 in the AI condition and 3.5 in the classroom condition. An adjusted analysis also favored AI, with a standardized coefficient of 0.63 after accounting for prior achievement and other measured differences. Experienced, highly rated teachers and common learning objectives make this a meaningful comparison with established instruction. The finding supports taking designed AI tutoring seriously as a way to teach new material.

The intervention's instructional structure helps explain what educators would be adopting. Expert-written solutions, question-specific prompts and programmed sequencing directed the tutor's responses; prompting alone had not reliably produced the intended sequence. Timely feedback and flexible pacing are plausible explanations for the learning advantage, as the authors suggest. However, the conditions also differed in location, peer interaction and the recorded introduction, so the study cannot assign the advantage to one feature. The contrast with unguided use in Kestin et al.'s (2025) review therefore supports distinguishing implementations, while leaving the reasons for differences between studies unresolved.

The time findings also bear on teaching decisions. AI activity logs recorded a median of 49 minutes, compared with an assumed classroom allocation of 60 minutes after testing was removed. Since classroom time was not measured for individuals in the same way, these figures suggest a possible time advantage without establishing a comparable individual efficiency gain. Higher immediate scores also leave open how much subsequent study students would need.

For teachers, the learning result supports trying this approach for initial engagement with selected material. Such a trial requires the expertise and preparation represented in the tested design: lesson preparation took days, platform development took months, and the recorded introduction was part of the learning experience. Kestin et al. (2025) propose using AI for initial learning and reserving classroom time for more demanding collaborative work. That arrangement offers a practical direction for experimentation, although its effectiveness as a complete course was not evaluated.

The evidence concerns two topics at one university, assessed immediately after instruction. It leaves retention, complex synthesis and sustained course achievement open. Evaluations in other settings should follow learning over time and assess whether gains justify the work of developing and maintaining instructional materials. Cost savings and equitable access would also require direct investigation before large-scale adoption.

## Conclusion

A structured AI tutor improved immediate topic learning compared with an established active-learning class in the studied introductory physics lessons. The contribution is evidence for a deliberately designed instructional package against a credible classroom alternative. Educators can use that evidence to justify bounded trials of initial-learning support, with expert content and instructional guidance in place. The next teaching decision is how this support can fit with classroom collaboration while preserving learning beyond the immediate lesson.
