## Discussion

This study offers an account of how undergraduate medical students come to recognize the value of research through applying it. In the five-semester research module at Mohammed Bin Rashid University, students described a shift from encountering unfamiliar methods to appreciating their relevance in supervised projects. The 8A-Model organizes this experience into four pairs: Attend–Acquire, Accumulate–Assimilate, Apply–Appreciate and Articulate–Affect. These pairs describe a proposed learning process in which knowledge, participation and appreciation develop together. Although presented sequentially, the model allows overlapping and iterative learning, with earlier knowledge revisited as students undertake new tasks.

The accounts give substance to the proposed transition. One participant described early biostatistics and software exercises as confusing or disconnected, whereas others recognized their relevance during project work. Students also described learning professional communication through supervisor exchanges and scrutinizing research quality and reported numbers. These examples connect the module's situated-learning rationale to participation in a community of practice: methods acquire purpose within authentic tasks supported by experienced researchers. Accounts of accomplishment and using presentation skills during internships extend the model toward articulation and perceived transfer. Affect represents the intended wider contribution of research; patients' outcomes and healthcare costs were not examined.

The grade pattern provides a second perspective on this curriculum transition. Mean research-course grade point averages (GPAs), on a 0–4 scale, were 3.01, 2.94 and 2.95 in the first three semesters, followed by 3.35 and 3.58. The reported analyses detected an early/later course contrast, with a similar pattern within the oldest cohort, and no detected differences within either cluster. However, assessment tasks changed from examinations and assignments to project-based outputs, while cohort composition also changed. The later grades therefore measure performance under different assessment conditions. The joint display brings this pattern together with cohort-level perceptions; it does not link each student's appreciation to their own grade change. Application is a plausible interpretive turning point, rather than an identified cause of increased performance.

The model's conceptual emphasis is the delayed recognition of research's value. Otaki et al. contrast this with their reading of Kirkpatrick and learning-transfer models, which they interpret as locating appreciation immediately after a learning experience. Their comparison directs attention to the time needed to find meaning in unfamiliar methods. Students in this program entered after secondary school, making gradual assimilation a plausible explanation for delayed appreciation. The evidence suggests attending to when students encounter opportunities to apply knowledge, without establishing a universal sequence of milestones.

Undergraduate medical programs should include opportunities to conduct supervised research alongside instruction in methods. The rationale rests on what authentic participation makes available: problems that require methodological judgment, exchanges with mentors and occasions to communicate findings. In programs able to sustain expert guidance and project opportunities, educators should connect earlier content with current research decisions and encourage analogical reasoning and reflection as students revise their mental models. This preserves the authors' experiential emphasis while treating the 8A-Model as a guide for curriculum design and inquiry. Early uncertainty about relevance should not be read automatically as failure of research education.

The study's single-institution design limits the contexts in which this interpretation can be assumed to hold. One focus group per cohort reconstructed experiences across curriculum stages; the same individuals were not interviewed repeatedly. Feedback from oldest-cohort participants supported refinement of the framework but was not prospective validation. Longitudinal studies linking individual accounts to comparable assessments would clarify how application, appreciation and performance develop. Research across programs, including comparative designs, should examine how supervision and curriculum structure shape these relationships. Follow-up should assess actual research practice, career participation and patient outcomes, keeping these proposed benefits distinct from the experiences documented here.

## Conclusion

The 8A-Model explains how research application can make previously unfamiliar methods meaningful within an undergraduate medical curriculum. Participants' accounts emphasize developing appreciation through project work, while the grade pattern offers curriculum-level convergence. Undergraduate medical programs should provide sustained, supervised research opportunities grounded in authentic participation and reflection. The framework's contribution is to focus curriculum thinking on how learning acquires purpose through use, with its proposed sequence and longer-term outcomes available for longitudinal investigation.
