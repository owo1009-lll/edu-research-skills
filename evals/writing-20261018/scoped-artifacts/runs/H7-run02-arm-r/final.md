## Discussion

Students' accounts suggest that the value of research learning becomes clearer when they apply methods in their own projects. In this compulsory undergraduate medical research module, the 8A-Model organizes that interpretation into four pairs: Attend–Acquire, Accumulate–Assimilate, Apply–Appreciate and Articulate–Affect. Although depicted sequentially, these processes can overlap and recur as students revisit knowledge through practice. The framework distinguishes acquiring research methods from recognizing their value through use. Its final pair connects communicating findings with an intended contribution to medicine or public health, rather than a demonstrated downstream impact.

The qualitative findings give this progression educational substance. Early biostatistics and software practice could feel disconnected, whereas project work gave students a reason to use previously acquired knowledge. Students described learning professional communication through supervisors, appraising research quality and examining reported numbers more critically. Accounts of accomplishment and applying presentation skills during internships suggest perceived transfer beyond the module. These experiences fit the authors' situated-learning account: participation in authentic research, supported by mentors within a community of practice, gives methods a practical purpose [26,61]. Research participation thus has an educational purpose that extends beyond familiarity with techniques.

The grade pattern places this interpretation within the curriculum. Mean research-course grade point average (GPA), on a 0–4 scale, was 3.01, 2.94 and 2.95 in the first three semesters, followed by 3.35 and 3.58 in the project courses. The reported analysis distinguished the earlier and later course clusters, while detecting no differences within either cluster; a similar pattern appeared within the oldest cohort. The joint display connects this curricular transition with accounts of application and appreciation. However, grades came from changing assessment tasks and cohort compositions, and the integration did not match individual interview accounts to individual grade changes. The findings therefore support a curriculum-level interpretation of the transition to research participation; they do not establish that appreciation caused higher grades or that project work produced a comparable gain in research competence.

Delayed appreciation is central to the authors' comparison with Kirkpatrick and learning-transfer models, which they interpret as emphasizing reactions immediately following a learning experience [43,53]. Here, students described recognizing value after opportunities to apply knowledge. For students entering medical education directly after secondary school, unfamiliar research content may acquire meaning gradually through use. This comparison directs attention to the timing of recognition within a sustained curriculum. The 8A-Model also invites curriculum designers to revisit earlier concepts during projects instead of treating its stages as a fixed ladder.

Undergraduate medical programs should provide supervised opportunities to conduct research as an integral part of their curricula [59,60]. This judgment rests on the opportunities for application, participation and reflection described here and on the module's experiential-learning rationale. Implementation requires authentic projects and accessible mentors who support questions, professional exchanges and the interpretation of findings. Educators should help students connect earlier methods teaching with project decisions, use analogical reasoning and reflect on their developing understanding [63]. The framework can guide attention to these connections across courses. Its practical value lies in organizing such opportunities, without assuming that every learner follows the same sequence or reaches the same endpoint.

The study's scope is shaped by its single-institution setting and cohort-based reconstruction of learning. Three focus groups captured different cohorts rather than following the same students through the module. Four oldest-cohort participants reviewed the framework, providing feedback on its interpretation rather than an independent test of its sequence. The nonexperimental design leaves the effect of project work inseparable from progression through the program. Future studies should follow individuals with comparable assessments, investigate the framework in other programs and examine actual research practice and careers. Patient outcomes and healthcare costs belong to this longer-term research agenda, since neither was measured here.

## Conclusion

In this undergraduate medical module, students described recognizing research's value through application. The 8A-Model connects this recognition with acquiring, integrating, using and communicating knowledge, while allowing learning to be iterative. Its educational contribution is to make the transition into research participation explicit. Programs should integrate authentic, mentored projects with methods teaching and reflection, using the framework to guide curriculum development and longitudinal evaluation.
