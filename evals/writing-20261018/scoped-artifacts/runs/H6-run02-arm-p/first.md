Secondary writing exercise based on Kestin et al. (2025); this text reports a published study, not research conducted by the writer.

## Introduction

Whether artificial intelligence (AI) belongs in teaching depends partly on the learning activity it supports. A tutor that guides students through problems presents a different instructional proposition from unrestricted chatbot access. Kestin et al. (2025) examined this distinction through a deliberately structured physics tutor. Their review describes earlier uses of ChatGPT involving limited reflection or weaker performance, including Krupp et al. (2023) and Forero et al. (2023). This contrast makes the design of an intervention central to interpreting its educational value.

The teaching question was whether structured AI tutoring could improve initial learning relative to an established active-learning class. In a Harvard introductory physics course for life-science students, existing peer groups were randomized to a two-week crossover covering surface tension and fluid flow. Both conditions followed the same worksheet content and learning objectives. The classroom included live explanations, peer problem solving and instructor support; the tutor condition combined individual work at home, a recorded introduction and guided problem solving. Immediate quizzes provided a focused comparison of learning from these instructional arrangements.

## Discussion

The structured tutor improved immediate topic learning relative to the active-learning class. Median post-test scores were 4.5 in the AI condition and 3.5 in the classroom condition. The adjusted analysis also favored AI, with a standardized coefficient of 0.63 after accounting for prior preparation and other measured differences. These findings matter because the comparison involved experienced, highly rated instructors using active learning. The advantage therefore represents a demanding instructional comparison rather than a contrast with an unsupported control.

The tutor's design offers plausible explanations for this advantage. Expert-written solutions, question-specific prompts and programmed sequencing provided a defined path through each problem, while feedback could address difficulties during learning. Flexible pacing may also have helped students allocate attention to difficult steps. However, the intervention changed feedback, pacing, location, peer interaction and the recorded introduction together. The experiment supports the package; it does not identify the contribution of each feature. This also limits explanations for the contrast with less structured ChatGPT activities described in Kestin et al.'s (2025) review: differences in implementation are relevant, but their causal role remains unresolved.

Time and experience provide additional context. The median logged AI activity lasted 49 minutes, compared with an assumed 60 minutes of classroom instruction. This suggests a promising relationship between learning and lesson time, although individual classroom time on task was not measured. Students reported higher engagement and motivation with the tutor, while enjoyment and confidence in learning difficult physics with effort showed no statistically detected differences. These single-item reports describe students' experience; they neither establish durable motivational change nor explain the learning advantage.

For teachers, the findings support considering structured tutoring for students' initial engagement with material. Such use requires substantial instructional preparation: lesson development took days, and the platform took months to build. Teachers evaluating this approach should plan for expert content preparation, explicit sequencing and feedback. The authors' proposal to use tutoring before higher-order collaborative classroom work offers a purposeful division of teaching activities, but that combined arrangement was not evaluated as a complete course (Kestin et al., 2025).

The evidence concerns two topics in one course, assessed immediately after each lesson. It therefore leaves open whether the advantage persists, supports complex transfer or travels to institutions with different learners and resources. Subsequent evaluations should examine delayed learning and classroom integration alongside implementation costs and access. These outcomes would determine whether an effective short instructional sequence can support a sustainable teaching arrangement.

## Conclusion

Structured AI tutoring improved immediate learning on two introductory physics topics relative to an established active-learning class. The educational contribution is evidence that deliberately designed tutoring can compete with a credible classroom alternative for initial learning. Teaching decisions should preserve the instructional preparation behind that result while testing how tutoring and collaborative classroom work can be combined over a course.
