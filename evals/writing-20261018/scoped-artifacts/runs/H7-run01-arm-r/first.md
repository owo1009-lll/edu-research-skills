## Discussion

This study addresses how undergraduate medical students come to recognize the value of research while learning to conduct it. The 8A-Model organizes their experiences across a compulsory five-semester research module at Mohammed Bin Rashid University (MBRU): Attend–Acquire, Accumulate–Assimilate, Apply–Appreciate and Articulate–Affect. Its contribution is an account of how applying research methods can make earlier instruction meaningful. Although presented sequentially, these activities can overlap and recur as learners revisit knowledge through project work. “Affect” expresses the intended contribution of research beyond the learner, including aspirations to improve medical practice.

Students’ accounts give this interpretation substance. Early biostatistics and software exercises could seem disconnected from research; project work later provided a context in which students recognized their relevance. Participants described learning to communicate with supervisors, appraise research quality and question reported numbers. Others associated completing a project with accomplishment and reported using presentation skills during internships. These accounts locate learning in participation in authentic research, supported by mentors and a community of practice, consistent with the module’s situated-learning rationale [26, 61]. They describe perceived development and transfer rather than independently assessed subsequent competence.

The course records show a corresponding distinction between earlier instruction and later projects. Mean research-course grade point averages (GPAs), on a 0–4 scale, were 3.01, 2.94 and 2.95 in the first three semesters, followed by 3.35 and 3.58. The reported analysis distinguished the early and later course clusters; it also found a similar pattern within the oldest cohort. Together, the accounts and grades identify project work as a promising point at which to examine students’ developing appreciation. This integration operates at curriculum stage: interviewed students’ narratives were not linked to their own grade changes. Assessments changed from examinations and assignments to research products, and cohort composition changed across semesters. Higher project-course grades therefore do not establish an equivalent increase in research competence or show that appreciation caused performance gains. All-course GPA includes research courses and provides no independent comparison group.

Delayed recognition of value is central to the authors’ interpretation. They contrast it with their reading of Kirkpatrick and learning-transfer evaluation models [43, 53], which emphasizes appreciation as an immediate response to a learning experience. Here, students entered medical education after secondary school and encountered unfamiliar research content. Appreciation emerging during application suggests that evaluating students’ responses only when content is introduced may miss its later significance. The study thus directs attention to the timing and context of students’ judgments, without establishing that established evaluation models are generally inadequate.

Undergraduate medical programs should provide opportunities to conduct supervised research alongside instruction in methods [59, 60]. The educational rationale is that authentic projects give learners a purpose for revisiting concepts and opportunities to exercise judgment with expert support. Programs adopting this approach should connect foundational courses with mentored projects, support professional communication, and encourage reflection and analogical reasoning across experiences [63]. Such continuity allows the 8A-Model to guide questions about curriculum design: where students encounter ideas, where they use them, and how they articulate their findings. The framework accommodates revisiting these activities rather than treating each as a completed step.

The single-institution, nonexperimental design limits conclusions about developmental sequence and effects. Three cohort focus groups reconstructed experiences across the curriculum rather than following individuals over time. Member checking by four oldest-cohort participants strengthened the framework’s grounding in their experience, while leaving its wider applicability open. Longitudinal studies across programs should examine how individual experiences and performance develop using comparable assessments. Future evaluation should also investigate research practice, research careers and patient outcomes; these are proposed longer-term consequences, not outcomes demonstrated here.

## Conclusion

The 8A-Model interprets undergraduate research learning as a process in which applying methods can reveal the value of earlier instruction. Student accounts and course-stage grade patterns support attention to that transition, while the framework allows overlapping and iterative learning. Undergraduate medical curricula should make supervised research integral to learning methods, with opportunities to reflect on practice and communicate findings. The model provides a basis for organizing those opportunities and investigating their longer-term consequences.
