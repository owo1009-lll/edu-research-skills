Secondary writing exercise based on the published study by Kestin et al. (2025). This text does not report research conducted by the writer.

## Introduction

Teaching decisions about artificial intelligence (AI) depend on the learning activities built around it. For teachers introducing difficult content, the question concerns what guidance students receive and where their initial learning should occur. Kestin et al. (2025) examined a structured AI tutor combining expert-written solutions, programmed sequencing and feedback during problem solving. Could this arrangement improve initial learning compared with an established active-learning class pursuing the same objectives? Answering this question would help teachers evaluate a specific role for tutoring within their teaching.

In Harvard's introductory physics course for life-science students, existing peer groups were randomly allocated to conditions that reversed across two consecutive weeks. Students studied surface tension and fluid flow, either individually at home with the tutor and a recorded introduction or in class with peers and experienced instructors. Both conditions used the same worksheets, with quizzes immediately before and after each lesson. The comparison therefore concerned two instructional arrangements for learning common content, assessed at the point of initial understanding.

## Discussion

The tutor package improved immediate quiz performance relative to the active-learning class: the reported post-test median was 4.5 with tutoring and 3.5 in class. An analysis accounting for prior achievement and other measured characteristics also favoured tutoring (Kestin et al., 2025). The classroom comparator included experienced teachers, peer problem solving and staff support, making this a meaningful instructional contrast. The finding gives teachers a reason to reconsider where initial guided learning occurs when individual study is supported by a deliberately developed tutor.

Timely feedback and flexible pacing are plausible explanations for the advantage, but the study tested them alongside differences in location, peer interaction and introductory presentation. Their separate contributions remain unresolved. The tutor also depended on expert solutions and programmed sequencing; prompting alone had not ensured the intended sequence. Its development illustrates the instructional work embodied in the tested arrangement. Kestin et al.'s (2025) review describes earlier examples of unstructured ChatGPT use with limited reflection or weaker performance. That comparison supports distinguishing implementations when interpreting AI research, while leaving open why outcomes differed across studies.

Student reports add a narrower account of the learning experience. Single-item engagement and motivation ratings favoured tutoring, whereas enjoyment and confidence in learning difficult physics through effort showed no statistically detected differences. Time measurements also require attention to what was recorded. Median logged tutor activity was 49 minutes; classroom instruction was assumed to occupy 60 minutes of the scheduled session. Because classroom time was not measured for individual students, these figures cannot establish comparative learning efficiency.

For teachers able to provide expert content and a carefully built sequence, the immediate learning result supports trialling structured tutoring for initial preparation. The authors propose reserving subsequent classroom time for more demanding collaborative work. This combination would need evaluation as a teaching arrangement in its own right, including whether students arrive prepared to participate in that work. Implementation also requires resources: lesson preparation took days and platform development months. A teaching trial should therefore examine learning alongside the effort required to design and maintain the tutor; the observed score advantage supplies no estimate of financial savings.

The scope of that trial matters. Evidence from two topics in one introductory course leaves delayed retention, complex transfer and sustained course achievement open. Evaluation across institutions should examine these outcomes alongside classroom integration and access. These questions determine whether an immediate lesson advantage can justify a wider change in how a course is organized.

## Conclusion

In this introductory physics course, a structured AI tutoring package improved immediate topic learning compared with an experienced active-learning class. The educational contribution is evidence that deliberately supported individual study can provide an effective setting for students' first encounters with course material. This finding warrants testing how initial tutoring can fit with subsequent classroom collaboration, with teaching decisions guided by both the learning achieved and the instructional resources required.
