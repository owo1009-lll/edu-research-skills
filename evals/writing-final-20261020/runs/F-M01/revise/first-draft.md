# Implications and conclusions

The STEM laboratory traditionally requires students to contribute to a shared learning process (Latour & Woolgar, 2013). In remote laboratories, however, students can belong to a virtual community of practice without posting. Lurkers are legitimate community members, yet their lower reported self-efficacy warrants attention given its relationship with affective and learning outcomes (Kelly, 2016; Schunk & Usher, 2012). Instructional changes should recognize legitimate peripheral participation while supporting students whose lack of confidence hinders contribution. Some may remain satisfied with vicarious learning. Not every student has to become a full-fledged contributor, but all should feel they may do so.

Course design should be intentional and informed by a social cognitive framework and learning situated in a virtual community of practice. Shared disciplinary practice connects this community (Glaze-Crampes, 2020; Wenger et al., 2002). Options include technology-supported collaborative design projects (Marra et al., 2016). Platforms should reflect students' prior experience and comfort, since unfamiliar tools may impede participation. Faculty and teaching assistants may need preparation beyond moderating discussions to recognize how academic experience, physics confidence, and community engagement relate to students' motivation and behavior. Smaller discussion groups may also help lurkers become contributors, as earlier work suggests (Honeychurch et al., 2017). Research should evaluate the cognitive and affective outcomes of these approaches and determine whether support should target students at risk of difficulties participating in communities of practice.
