# Discussion

Social participation in this remote asynchronous physics laboratory extended beyond visible contributions. Students who read discussions without posting formed a distinct group between contributors and outsiders in their social learning perceptions. Contributors reported the most positive perceptions, followed by lurkers and outsiders. This pattern directs attention to how students use a community, rather than treating posting as the sole indication of involvement. With communication optional and laboratory reports completed individually, students could approach the same course through different social arrangements.

The explanatory sequential analysis makes this distinction educationally meaningful. Lurkers described using peers' questions to anticipate experimental difficulties and judge their own understanding, while contributors valued accessible communication and reciprocal help. These accounts support interpreting some observation as participation within the community of practice. They extend to a physics laboratory the account of peripheral participation presented in Rosen and Kelly's (2023) review of prior work. Yet lurking also reflected fear of judgment, uncertainty about giving incorrect advice, or distrust of classmates' information. The same low visibility could therefore express useful observation or constrained participation. Integrating group comparisons with these accounts reveals differences that a ranking of posting frequency would conceal.

Physics laboratory self-efficacy showed a less orderly pattern. Contributors scored higher than lurkers in the reported post hoc comparison, whereas outsiders did not differ significantly from either group. The latter comparisons leave the size of those differences unresolved. Some outsiders described confidence in working independently; others lacked connections or later regretted remaining isolated. Together, these accounts explain why outsiders should not automatically be treated as the least confident students. Conversely, lurkers' concerns about their competence make selection into participation a credible explanation of the contributor–lurker difference, alongside the possibility that peer exchanges support confidence. The end-of-semester survey cannot establish their direction.

For instructors, the findings support making communication channels visible and accessible while attending to students' reasons for silence. Invitations to ask questions and attention to unanswered posts address barriers reported by participants; recognizing the use of existing discussions acknowledges a form of engagement students found helpful. These practices require evaluation before claims about improved self-efficacy or learning are warranted. The evidence comes from self-selected respondents in one pandemic-era course and concerns perceived experiences rather than demonstrated learning gains. Its contribution is to distinguish forms and reasons for participation that instructors would miss by equating engagement with posting.
