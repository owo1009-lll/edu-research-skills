# Discussion

Requiring students to answer practice questions from memory improved delayed performance relative to answering the same questions with learning materials available. In these introductory psychology seminars, the closed-book group scored 45.09% on the surprise quiz, compared with 36.73% in the open-book group. Because both groups answered identical questions, this advantage goes beyond showing that answering questions is preferable to passive restudy. It supports making retrieval an explicit requirement within classroom practice when the educational goal is later performance without access to materials.

The additional module-exam findings extend this pattern to related questions that had not appeared in practice. Closed-book students scored 61.11%, compared with 48% among open-book students (d = 0.86). The advantage remained after adjustment for performance on unrelated exam questions. This weakens a general-ability explanation, although the covariate was measured after practice and the nonsignificant unrelated-question comparison does not establish equal initial ability. The exam followed a shared surprise quiz and feedback, so its results reflect the subsequent learning sequence rather than an isolated, uninterrupted retention interval.

These findings add classroom evidence to a comparison that earlier work had left unsettled. Rummer et al.'s (2019) account describes no consistent closed-book advantage in several earlier comparisons and question-dependent results in Roelle and Berthold (2017). Here, practice continued across seven lessons and the surprise quiz assessed material after one to seven weeks. These features may help explain the advantage, but their contributions were not independently tested. Retrieval could strengthen later access to information; closed-book practice also matched the requirement to answer without materials on later tests. Alternatively, experiencing retrieval difficulties could guide subsequent study, since students retained their materials and were encouraged to study at home. These candidate explanations remain unresolved because retrieval processes and subsequent study were not measured separately.

The study's limitations concern the security and scope of this classroom comparison. Random assignment and a common instructor strengthened the design, but the sample was small, each condition occupied one seminar, and surprise-quiz attendance was lower in the closed-book group. Selection among those attending could therefore affect the observed difference. Exam anonymity also prevented linking individual quiz and exam performance. Replication across more classes with attendance and study behavior recorded would strengthen the inference. For comparable university teaching, brief closed-book practice is a supported option for improving delayed performance on the taught material. The evidence concerns practice questions in this setting; it does not determine the best format for all examinations or learning objectives.
