# Discussion

Motivational effects on subsequent study should be weaker in laboratory studies where learning content lacks personal relevance (Agarwal et al., 2008; Agarwal & Roediger, 2011; Roelle & Berthold, 2017, as cited in Rummer et al., 2019). Noticing retrieval failures would then provide less incentive for extensive study at home. Such motivational differences might explain the contrast with our findings. Gharib et al.'s (2012, as cited in Rummer et al., 2019) field experiment, which detected no difference in delayed retention between test formats, appears to challenge this interpretation. However, their "practice tests" were actual course exams, followed by a surprise retention quiz. If passing the course exam was students' principal goal, they had little reason to restudy afterward; exam format may consequently have had little influence on subsequent study behavior.

These considerations argue against interpreting our findings solely as a direct retrieval practice effect. Nonetheless, the closed-book advantage suggests that test format matters from an applied perspective. Should we therefore strictly recommend closed-book over open-book practice tests? Given the small sample, restriction to two cognitive psychology seminars, and limitations discussed above, we remain cautious. More field experiments should compare these formats using authentic learning materials across school and university populations and different subjects. They should also track time spent studying at home to investigate whether subsequent learning behavior contributes to the observed advantage.
