# Discussion

These motivational effects should be weaker in laboratory studies where learning content lacks personal relevance (Agarwal et al., 2008; Agarwal & Roediger, 2011; Roelle & Berthold, 2017, as cited in Rummer et al., 2019). Negative feedback from closed-book practice would then be less likely to prompt extensive study at home, potentially explaining the difference from our findings. Gharib et al.'s (2012, as cited in Rummer et al., 2019) field experiment, which found no difference in delayed retention between test formats, appears to challenge this interpretation. However, their "practice tests" were actual course exams, followed by a surprise retention quiz. Once students had completed the exam, their principal goal, further study was unlikely; exam format would therefore have had little influence on subsequent study behavior.

These considerations argue against interpreting our findings solely as a direct retrieval practice effect. Nonetheless, the closed-book advantage suggests that test format matters from an applied perspective. Should we therefore strictly recommend closed-book over open-book practice tests? Given the small sample, restriction to two cognitive psychology seminars, and limitations discussed above, we remain cautious. More field experiments should compare these formats using authentic learning materials across school and university populations and different subjects. They should also track time spent studying at home to investigate whether subsequent learning behavior contributes to the observed advantage.
