# Discussion

For the women interviewed, identification with science involved both emotional investment and social recognition. Across the interviews, enthusiasm for scientific work coexisted with uneven acceptance within scientific communities. Molly's account connected supportive peers with feeling more comfortable in physics, whereas Lou described being overlooked despite her expertise. Dora's commitment to physics also persisted alongside experiences that led her to consider leaving. Distinguishing emotional attachment from community attachment therefore explains how women can see themselves as scientists while questioning their place among other scientists. For higher education, increasing enthusiasm addresses only one part of belonging.

The accounts also show that women actively negotiated the identities available to them. Lara described replacing a masculine laboratory image with an understanding grounded in her own computational work. Yet participation did not simply dissolve stereotypes: Diane identified herself as a physicist while continuing to picture physicists as men. These tensions give substance to the study's performative understanding of identity. Doing science supplied resources for redefining who a scientist could be, while masculine images continued to shape who looked like a physicist. Gender identity was similarly negotiated. Dora presented being a woman and a physicist as mutually strengthening, whereas Demi associated fitting into physics with masculine traits while also describing feminine self-presentation. Agency could thus involve both challenging and reproducing gendered expectations within the same account.

These findings suggest that departments should attend to the conditions under which expertise becomes recognised. Lou's account of a discriminatory seminar remark left unchallenged points to a responsibility for staff to respond to exclusionary comments. Shalini's and Kathryn's concerns about appearance similarly warrant questioning judgments that equate femininity with insufficient seriousness. Molly's account gives departments a reason to support peer relationships alongside changes to everyday practices of recognition. This follows from the educational commitment to fair participation; the interviews identify problems and possible responses rather than demonstrate the effectiveness of an intervention.

The study's limitations define the scope of these implications. The purposive, self-selected sample across four Dublin universities provides situated accounts rather than estimates of how common these experiences are. Interviews at different academic levels cannot establish an individual developmental sequence, even when participants recount changes over time. Jill's and Samiya's accounts bring ethnicity, national background and religion into the analysis, but the relatively homogeneous sample limits the range of intersecting positions examined. In settings where women enjoy scientific work yet face judgments about who belongs, distinguishing these attachments can help educators recognise exclusion that enthusiasm alone would conceal.
