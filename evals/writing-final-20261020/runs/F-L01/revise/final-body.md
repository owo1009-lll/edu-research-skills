# Conclusion

I explored women's gender and engagement in science through their narratives. Although influenced by white, masculine images of physics and physical sciences, the participants challenged these images by blurring the boundaries between “scientist” and “woman” in everyday life. Constructing a science identity may be stressful when cultural expectations place it in tension with being a woman. Their struggles to become visible as themselves were both individual and collective. I suggest that, in these accounts, being a scientist involves an interplay of discourses about doing and enjoying science, enthusiasm, competence, participation in scientific communities and gender.

Their concerns about (in)visibility and (under)representation suggest that science education programmes should prepare students to practise science and understand its culture. Gender studies could be incorporated into science education at all levels in Ireland, offering a perspective from which to question masculine norms, stereotypes and prevailing scientific practices. Institutions also need to create more welcoming environments for women from minority backgrounds, whose accounts described having their scientific knowledge and skills underestimated. Students and faculty members can undertake anti-bias training early in their education or careers as one response to these experiences.

Finally, these accounts could inform girls considering a physical science major and women considering academic science careers. They could also guide institutions seeking to build academic cultures and policies that embrace diversity, gender equality and inclusion.
