Silence and influence in teacher meetings: manuscript excerpt
PUBLIC SYNTHETIC MANUSCRIPT. All people, events and quotations below are invented for
behavioral testing; this is not a report of real participants.
Methods. Nine teachers from three programs took part in 12 interviews, including three follow-up
interviews. The researcher also observed three team meetings. Analysis compared how speaking,
waiting and written follow-up were used when teachers tried to influence team decisions. Interview
accounts were considered alongside the meeting records. The supplied excerpt does not describe how
the nine teachers were recruited or how the researcher entered the teams.
Findings. In one observed meeting, Marta did not interrupt a senior colleague who was presenting the
timetable. After the meeting she emailed an alternative arrangement. The next meeting's agenda
included the alternative, and the team adopted part of it. In her interview she explained: 'I wanted the
idea considered, but that moment was not going to help it.' Her account links waiting to an alternative
route for influencing a decision.
A contrasting case concerns Amir. He remained silent during a meeting about assessment and later
said, 'I did not want the manager to think I was difficult.' The observation record contains no later written
proposal from him. His account points to the anticipated consequences of disagreement, rather than a
planned alternative intervention.

Interpretation and implications
These cases make silence analytically heterogeneous. Marta's waiting formed part of a sequence that
included a written proposal and a changed agenda. Amir's account instead concerned the risk of
speaking against a manager. The interpretation concerns how influence becomes possible through
relationships and available channels; it does not equate agency with the number of spoken turns.
Every instance of silence in this study was therefore a deliberate strategy of resistance, showing that
quiet teachers exercised the same influence as frequent speakers. The interviews establish how all
silent teachers intended to intervene.
The distinction between waiting as part of an intervention and silence under anticipated sanction
matters for school leadership. Leaders should provide credible routes for raising concerns outside a
fast-moving meeting and make clear how those contributions enter decisions. Providing a written
channel alone does not establish that concerns will be heard; the route also needs a response. This
recommendation retains the study's position that opportunities for influence depend on how
contributions are received.
