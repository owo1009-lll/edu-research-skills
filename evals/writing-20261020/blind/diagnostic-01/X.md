Juergensen and van Garderen's (2025) findings distinguish access to science talk from the opportunities students receive to develop their ideas. In the observed whole-class discussions, teacher-initiated sequences formed a larger share of the recorded sequences for students identified by teachers as experiencing difficulty than for their classmates. Interviews informed by these observations add teachers' perspectives: talk offered a way to express understanding when writing was difficult, while fear of error and faster classmates could hinder volunteering. This connection places participation within classroom relationships and pacing, while leaving individual students' reasons for speaking unresolved. Evaluative responses were common in both groups, and teachers described confirmation as encouragement. The combined evidence therefore directs attention to what follows an invitation to speak: whether contributions receive approval and whether they become resources for shared reasoning.

That distinction concerns epistemic authority, or whose knowledge is treated as authoritative in classroom sensemaking. Teachers' efforts to encourage participation provide a reason to pair reassurance with opportunities for students to explain and revisit ideas. Where students need longer to respond, teachers should protect processing time and invite elaboration before judging correctness. One teacher described allowing students to work through an idea, illustrating a possible response practice. Confidence and learning gains were not measured, so the recommendation concerns the opportunities teachers provide. Consistent with the study's commitment to equitable learning, teachers should examine both who gets a turn and how students experiencing difficulty can explain, develop and discuss their thinking with classmates.
