Williamson et al.'s (2019) study sharpens our understanding of evidence-generation partnerships by distinguishing project initiation from collaboration across research stages. In interviews about health and related social policy, experienced researchers and policy-makers expressed a shared commitment to impact alongside different expectations about useful evidence. Policy-makers valued the innovation of researcher-initiated work but also described delays and insufficiently actionable recommendations; researchers worried about weak policy engagement. These accounts suggest that a common aspiration for impact can leave the timing and form of useful evidence unresolved. Researchers also valued small policy-initiated projects for learning and relationships despite limited publication opportunities. Taken together, these experiences suggest that tensions concern the terms of mutual benefit, while each group's interests extend beyond a single professional priority.

Differences over sustained co-production further suggest that partnership work involves deciding where collaboration is needed. Some participants favoured collaboration throughout; others considered independent work appropriate after agreeing aims and deliverables. Contextual knowledge was described as particularly valuable in evaluation and implementation and also as useful in interpreting an evidence review. These accounts make the knowledge required at each stage a more informative guide to involvement than project labels alone. Partners should therefore agree deliverables, responsibilities, timelines and recognition of contributions alongside the stages requiring joint interpretation, then revisit these arrangements as work develops. Such agreements also need to reflect available resources and workforce continuity: some policy-makers wanted co-production but had not found it possible. Where capacity restricts participation, partners should revisit the agreed scope and division of work.
