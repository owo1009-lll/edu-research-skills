# Source material: achievement and wellbeing across education systems

This pack supplies material for an attributed secondary-writing exercise. It is a paraphrase of Högberg (2023), *Is There a trade-off Between Achievement and Wellbeing in Education Systems? New cross-country Evidence*, Child Indicators Research, 16, 2165–2186. [Public article](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12187-023-10047-9), DOI 10.1007/s12187-023-10047-9, CC BY 4.0. The figures and relevant supplementary tables were inspected in preparing this pack. No private manuscript is used. Use the evidence below without browsing.

## Question and rationale

The article asks whether higher country-level achievement accompanies poorer pupil wellbeing, and whether country-level achievement moderates the relationship between individual achievement and wellbeing. Its policy problem is whether educational systems must sacrifice wellbeing to pursue achievement.

The author contrasts two arguments. Competence, learning and associated opportunities could connect individual achievement with wellbeing; some educational arrangements might support both. An alternative emphasises the positional value of achievement in competition for scarce opportunities. An individual's advantage need not become a collective advantage: if everyone's achievement rises equally, relative positions stay the same, while competition may require additional effort. These are theoretical arguments, not mechanisms measured by the study. A rise in the national mean alone does not tell us how the distribution or ranks changed. The literature claims in these arguments are the author's review; their original sources have not been independently read for this exercise.

## Data and comparisons

- The school-belonging analysis uses five PISA waves of 15-year-old pupils: 2000, 2003, 2012, 2015 and 2018, with 1,091,175 observations in 45 countries. Positive and negative affect are available only in 2018 and use different analytical samples.
- School belonging concerns feeling accepted and connected at school, measured with six items about belonging, exclusion, friendship and loneliness. Affect concerns the reported frequency of positive or negative feelings. Outcomes are standardised to mean zero and standard deviation one.
- National achievement is the country-wave mean mathematics score. Individual achievement is the pupil's deviation from that country-wave mean. Both achievement variables are standardised. Country-level achievement serves as a broad proxy for a bundle of educational policies and practices, not a specific manipulated policy.
- HBSC provides repeated cross-sectional data on school-related stress, psychosomatic complaints and life satisfaction in 34 countries. Its pupil records are matched to country-level PISA achievement from the closest preceding or simultaneous wave. These are different pupils from PISA, and neither survey follows the same individuals across waves. HBSC has no individual achievement measure for the interaction analysis.
- Where repeated outcomes are available, country and year fixed effects compare variation within countries over time, alongside models with socioeconomic covariates. Affect has only one wave, so its comparisons are cross-sectional. Standard errors are clustered by country; supplementary part C reports wild-cluster-bootstrap intervals with 1,000 replications.

## Results available for discussion

The reported pattern varies across wellbeing outcomes and model specifications. Initial cross-sectional affect comparisons show higher national achievement associated with more negative affect and, less consistently, less positive affect. The estimates change with the analytical sample and additional covariates. The fully adjusted affect associations are not statistically significant. They do not supply a longitudinal affect comparison.

For school belonging, supplementary Table B2 reports a country-achievement coefficient of -0.009 without covariates, 0.099 with country/year fixed effects, and 0.069 after socioeconomic adjustment. The adjusted coefficient has a reported standard error of 0.045. Supplementary Table C2 gives the corresponding 95% wild-cluster-bootstrap interval as [-0.053, 0.241]. These are standard-deviation-scaled model coefficients; they are not percentage changes or policy treatment effects. The interval includes both negative and positive associations.

The school-belonging interaction between country and individual achievement is -0.017 (standard error 0.005) in B2; its 95% bootstrap interval in C2 is [-0.029, -0.005]. This indicates a smaller individual-achievement slope at higher national achievement in that model. The author describes it as small. It is the significant exception among three tested PISA outcome interactions; it does not cover all wellbeing outcomes, individual circumstances or threshold effects.

For the repeated HBSC outcomes, the adjusted national-achievement coefficients are -0.015 for stress, approximately zero for psychosomatic complaints, and 0.041 for life satisfaction. Corresponding 95% bootstrap intervals are [-0.101, 0.063], [-0.068, 0.053] and [-0.008, 0.087]. The descriptive conclusion is weak and inconsistent support for either a general trade-off or a general gain. There was no equivalence test, no intervention comparison and no direct measurement of the proposed competitive mechanisms.

## Author's interpretation and policy position

The author considers national comparisons a useful addition to literature chiefly focused on individual differences. He argues against broad assurances of a win-win relationship or claims of an inevitable trade-off, and directs attention to the content of particular policies and practices. The article discusses studies of intrinsic motivation, testing and supplementary schooling, but those cited originals have not been independently checked here.

The policy position is to pursue achievement and wellbeing together, identify arrangements that support both and respond where a particular policy harms wellbeing. School nursing and counselling are examples in the author's suggested compensatory response, not interventions evaluated in this study. The author also raises a distinction between average linear relationships and possible benefits of achieving basic eligibility thresholds or preventing school failure. These threshold effects were not tested.

## Source status and reporting questions

Locations: article Methods and Results, Discussion and Conclusion; supplementary B2, B3a–c, C2 and C3a–c. Some article text calls estimates “non-existing” or implies that achievement gains do no harm. The available design and intervals above do not establish absence of association or harm. No equivalence margin was specified. Fixed effects address stable national differences, while time-varying confounding, reverse direction and measurement error remain possible.

The main text and supplementary affect tables differ in some coefficients; figure captions and a model label also contain discrepancies. The exact affect coefficients are therefore not supplied as verified figures here. A prose summary of the bootstrap findings overlooks the school-belonging interaction and a life-satisfaction specification; use the explicitly identified table entries above. Supplementary D–I have not been fully read or rerun. The statistical analyses were not recomputed for this exercise. These matters belong in an author-attention note when relevant; the task requests a short passage, not a complete methods audit.
