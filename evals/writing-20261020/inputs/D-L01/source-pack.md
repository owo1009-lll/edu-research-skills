# Source material: evidence-generation partnerships

This pack supplies material for an attributed secondary-writing exercise. It paraphrases Williamson, Tait, El Jardali, Wolfenden, Thackway, Stewart, O'Leary and Dixon (2019), *How are evidence generation partnerships between researchers and policy-makers enacted in practice? A qualitative interview study*, Health Research Policy and Systems, 17, 41. [Public article](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12961-019-0441-2), DOI 10.1186/s12961-019-0441-2, CC BY 4.0. Short participant quotations below are attributed as in the article. No private material is used. Use this pack without browsing.

## Study context

The study examines how partnerships to generate evidence are initiated, valued and sustained by researchers and policy-makers. It addresses health and related social policy, not an education intervention. The authors distinguish evidence generation from simply communicating already completed research to policy audiences.

Eighteen experienced participants were purposively selected: seven university researchers and eleven policy-makers, with seventeen based in New South Wales, Australia. Six policy-makers had a doctorate or previous research experience. Semi-structured interviews took place between October 2017 and April 2018 and lasted approximately 30–60 minutes. The interviewer was herself experienced in research-policy partnerships. Two authors developed themes inductively and discussed the synthesis with an advisory group.

Interviews addressed participants' experience across projects. They were not paired interviews with both members of each partnership or direct observations of a particular project's sequence. The guide prompted policy-initiated and researcher-initiated work and co-production, while allowing other classifications. The authors' claim to inductive analysis does not mean those distinctions were absent from the questions. No independent project-quality, policy-impact or partnership-success measure was collected.

## Findings and participant material

Who initiates a project and how intensively partners subsequently collaborate are distinct aspects. A project initiated by either side can involve limited collaboration or continuing co-production. Co-production refers to collaboration across research stages; it is not a third, mutually exclusive way for a project to originate.

Researchers valued researcher-initiated projects as opportunities to combine scientific ambition with practical impact. Policy-makers could also value the innovation involved. A policy-maker described such work as offering “something quite new in terms of maybe methods or thinking outside the square” (Table 2). Another excerpt attributed to a policy-maker in the same table states that “you don't get the answer you want when you want it.” The table gives role labels without participant identifiers, so it does not establish whether these excerpts come from the same person. They are not presented as two turns in a dialogue or a verified sequence in one project.

Policy-makers reported that researcher-initiated projects were less often immediately relevant to current priorities and could take too long, provide insufficient practical recommendations or fail to acknowledge policy partners' contributions. Researchers, meanwhile, identified limited policy engagement as an important risk. The authors interpret these accounts as suggesting that research priorities may be misaligned even among experienced partners who seek real-world impact. The study does not identify which specific partner disengaged for which reason.

Policy-initiated projects were valued for timely decisions and useful evidence, but could have limited funding or publication opportunities. Researchers sometimes still valued small, low-return projects for learning and relationships. Policy-makers criticised some researchers for promising too much, narrowing questions to their own interests or simplifying complex issues to make the work manageable. These are attributed criticisms, not verified motives of all researchers. Both groups sought impact, and their interests were not entirely opposed.

Some participants considered sustained co-production ideal; others considered its value dependent on the work. The latter thought that, after agreeing aims and deliverables, some analyses of large datasets or evidence reviews could proceed largely independently. Evaluation and implementation projects were described as benefiting particularly from policy-makers' tacit and contextual knowledge in design, conduct and analysis. Table 3 also includes an account of contextual knowledge helping interpret an evidence review. The project labels alone do not determine the necessary collaboration.

Several policy-makers wanted to co-produce research but had not found it possible. Trust, long-term relationships, adequate resources and a stable policy workforce were considered helpful. These accounts include constraints on the ideal, not only praise for collaboration.

Participants described success through learning, mutual benefit and usable evidence as well as eventual policy influence. Practical work included agreeing aims, deliverables, responsibilities, timelines, governance and publication arrangements; allowing revision as the project developed; keeping commitments; and continuing discussion and feedback. These are participants' experienced judgments and the authors' practical recommendations, not experimentally demonstrated effects.

## Source scope and original citation status

The pack draws on the article's Results and Discussion, Tables 1–4 and the supplementary interview guide. The primary developer read the full main text, all four tables and that guide. The original interview recordings and transcripts were not available for inspection. The participant quotations above come from Table 2 and retain its role attribution; omitted surrounding wording is not replaced with invented dialogue.

The attached paragraph, when used for the revision task, is actual author prose from the Discussion. Its numbered citations are the authors' original citations. Their full texts have not been read for this exercise, so retention of a citation does not constitute independent verification. The source publication is attributed; this pack does not ask for new literature searches, a reference list or an audit of all claims in the article.
