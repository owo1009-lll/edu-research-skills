# Source pack: public English mixed-methods study

This is a secondary-writing exercise based on a published study, not a new empirical submission. The facts below are paraphrased from the cited article. Page numbers refer to the 25-page public PDF, not journal pagination.

Juergensen, R., & van Garderen, D. (2025). *Sensemaking Opportunities for Students Experiencing Difficulty: A Mixed Methods Study*. Journal of Science Education for Students with Disabilities, 28(1), Article 4. [Publisher record and CC BY 4.0 licence](https://repository.rit.edu/jsesd/vol28/iss1/4/); [public PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1482227.pdf). The publisher and PDF print DOI 10.14448/jsesd.17.0001; DOI registration has not been confirmed. The source document is available through the linked publisher/ERIC records.

## Participants and scope

- Three general-education middle-school science teachers in two Midwestern US districts participated. Each was observed during one instructional unit, for a total of 23 lessons and 26 whole-class discussions (PDF pp. 5–6).
- Of 52 participating students, the teachers identified 13 as experiencing difficulty in science after the observations. Teachers used their knowledge of students, assessments and classroom observations; there was no common screening definition. This group included students with and without Individual Education Programs (p. 5).
- The authors use “students experiencing difficulty” to avoid treating difficulty as a fixed identity. Their stated position is that all students deserve equitable opportunities to learn and support to participate in science sensemaking (pp. 2, 21).
- Only identifiable, consenting students' visible participation in whole-class discussions was coded. Choral responses, side conversations, small-group talk and unidentifiable contributions were excluded. A participation sequence can include several exchanges between a teacher and one student; a different student begins a new sequence (p. 6).

## Classroom observations

- Codes distinguished who initiated participation, the cognitive demand of the solicitation, and whether the teacher evaluated a student's contribution. A solicitation could be a question or an invitation to explain. Low-demand solicitations included recall and procedural responses; high-demand solicitations asked students to explain or develop their thinking (pp. 7–8).
- Evaluative responses included stating that an answer was right or wrong, or praising it. Non-evaluative responses included asking for further explanation, restating an idea for classmates, or leaving its correctness open for discussion (pp. 7–8).
- The displayed solicitation-method table reports 29 teacher-initiated and 23 student-initiated sequences in the group experiencing difficulty, and 109 teacher-initiated and 266 student-initiated sequences in the other group (Table 4, p. 10). These are sequences, not counts of different students, and do not describe each student's participation rate.
- The authors report that evaluative responses were common in both groups and did not detect a group association for that dimension (pp. 11, 18). The table's column labels are inconsistent with its stated dimension. No confidence intervals or analysis accounting for repeated sequences from the same students are supplied in this task pack.
- There are unresolved reporting discrepancies: the Methods says 428 sequences, the displayed tables total 427, and the tests label N as 52. Tables 5–6 repeat solicitation-method column labels despite referring to other dimensions. The exact inferential statistics are therefore not supplied as verified results for the exercise.

## Teachers' accounts and connection between phases

- The classroom analyses informed interviews in which teachers reflected on their practice. The authors describe an explanatory sequential design and a later joint display of observation patterns, interview findings and integrated interpretations (Figure 1; pp. 14–16, 21).
- Teachers described talk as a way for students who had difficulty writing to express what they understood, process scientific ideas aloud and learn with classmates (pp. 11–12).
- They described students as sometimes hesitant to volunteer because of fear of being wrong. They saw confirming a correct response as a way to build confidence and encourage subsequent participation. These are teachers' accounts; the study did not directly measure students' confidence or subsequent learning gains (pp. 12–13).
- Teachers also described relationship-building, opportunities to practise discussion and routines that allow processing time. Some said faster responses from classmates could crowd out students who needed longer to respond (pp. 13–14).
- One teacher described letting students stay with an idea and work it out rather than immediately evaluating it. This was not reported as common across all three teachers (p. 16).
- The article's discussion introduces epistemic authority as whose knowledge is treated as authoritative in the classroom. It argues for examining how students' contributions are taken up, alongside their access to talk (p. 18).
- The joint display and published interview excerpts are not a complete teacher-by-teacher or student-by-student linkage. The observations cover whole-class discussion in the selected units, not every learning context. The study does not evaluate a teacher professional-development intervention (pp. 15, 19–21).
