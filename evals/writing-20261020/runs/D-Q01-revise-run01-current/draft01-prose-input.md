## Conclusion and Implications

Within-country comparisons from the fixed effects models are particularly relevant to policymakers, whose decisions mainly operate at national or subnational levels. The predominantly null findings provide weak support for either a general trade-off or a general gain in wellbeing as national achievement rises; they do not establish that achievement gains leave wellbeing unchanged. Policymakers should therefore neither assume that wellbeing must be sacrificed nor expect higher achievement alone to deliver substantial wellbeing gains. These linear models concern average achievement and wellbeing, however, and may miss benefits associated with crossing basic eligibility thresholds. Raising basic skills and preventing school failure may be especially beneficial for wellbeing (Moilanen, 2010), although such threshold effects were not tested here. The study also does not identify which specific policies involve trade-offs. If achievement and wellbeing are both valued, identifying and implementing policies that support both should remain a priority. Where particular policies harm pupils' wellbeing, policymakers should address that harm through compensatory measures, including investment in school nursing and counselling.
