# Discussion

Williamson et al.'s (2019) study sharpens our understanding of evidence-generation partnerships by separating who initiates research from how partners collaborate. Among experienced researchers and policy-makers, a shared commitment to impact coexisted with different expectations about useful evidence. Policy-makers valued the innovation of researcher-initiated work but also described delays and insufficiently actionable recommendations; researchers worried about weak policy engagement. These accounts suggest that seeking impact leaves the timing, form and recognition of contributions open to negotiation. Researchers' willingness to undertake small policy-initiated projects for learning and relationships further complicates any fixed opposition between scientific and policy interests. Partnership tensions may therefore concern how mutual benefit is realised in particular work, rather than whether either group values impact.

This interpretation also clarifies why participants differed over sustained co-production. Some favoured collaboration throughout, whereas others considered independent work appropriate once aims and deliverables were agreed. Their examples suggest that the need for collaboration turns on when contextual knowledge matters: evaluation and implementation often required policy insight, yet such knowledge could also inform an evidence review. Project labels alone are consequently insufficient guides to involvement. A practical implication is to agree deliverables, responsibilities and timelines alongside the stages requiring joint interpretation, then revisit these arrangements as questions develop. Partners also need to consider resources and workforce continuity, since some policy-makers wanted greater involvement but could not sustain it. Grounded in participants' accounts rather than measured policy effects, this approach treats collaboration as an arrangement to negotiate around the work's knowledge requirements and partners' capacity.
