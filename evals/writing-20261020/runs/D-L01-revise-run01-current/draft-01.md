Some participants saw co-production, defined as collaboration across all research stages, as the ideal means of improving relevance and impact [37, 38, 39], while others questioned whether its time and resource demands were always justified [40]. Collaborative agreement on aims, goals and deliverables remained essential [41]. Once these were agreed, some analyses of large datasets and evidence reviews could, in these informants’ view, proceed largely independently without losing quality or relevance. Co-production was considered particularly worthwhile for evaluation and implementation, where policy-makers’ tacit, experiential knowledge informed study design, implementation and analysis. However, several policy-makers who wished to co-produce research had found it impracticable. Participants regarded trusting, long-term relationships as a usual precursor to co-production and workforce stability as supportive of those relationships; frequent staff changes are also cited as a barrier to evidence-generation partnerships [31, 42].
