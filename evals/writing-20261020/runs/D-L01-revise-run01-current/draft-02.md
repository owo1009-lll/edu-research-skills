Some participants saw co-production, or collaboration across all research stages, as ideal for increasing research relevance and the likelihood of impact [37, 38, 39]; others questioned whether its time and resource demands were always warranted [40]. Collaborative agreement on aims, goals and deliverables was always considered essential [41]. With this in place, these informants suggested that researchers could complete some large-dataset analyses and evidence reviews largely independently without compromising quality or relevance. Co-production was considered particularly worthwhile for evaluation and implementation projects, where policy-makers’ tacit, experiential knowledge provided critical nuance for study design, implementation and analysis. Yet several policy-makers who wanted to co-produce research had not found it possible in practice. Participants viewed trusting, long-term relationships as a usual precursor to co-production. Workforce stability was seen to support these relationships, whereas frequent staff changes are cited as a barrier to successful evidence-generation partnerships [31, 42].
