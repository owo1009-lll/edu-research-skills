# Discussion

In Juergensen and van Garderen's (2025) study, equitable science participation emerges as a question of both access to talk and the treatment of students' ideas. Teacher-initiated sequences formed a greater share of the observed participation of students experiencing difficulty than of their classmates. Interviews informed by the observations help interpret this pattern: teachers described talk as an alternative when writing was difficult, while fear of error and faster classmates could discourage volunteering. Their accounts locate participation within classroom relationships and pacing, rather than treating difficulty as a fixed student characteristic. Evaluative responses were common in both groups, and teachers regarded confirmation of correct answers as encouragement. Together, these strands suggest a tension between inviting students into discussion and positioning their contributions as ideas to be developed, rather than answers awaiting approval.

This tension concerns epistemic authority: whose knowledge is treated as authoritative in classroom sensemaking. Teachers' supportive intentions make simply removing evaluative responses an inadequate implication. A more useful priority is to combine encouragement with opportunities to explain and revisit ideas. Where students need longer to respond, teachers should protect processing time and invite elaboration before judging correctness, drawing on one teacher's account of letting students work through an idea. Teachers can then examine who gains a turn and whether classmates engage with the reasoning offered. The observations concern whole-class discussion, and the interviews supply teachers' interpretations rather than direct measures of confidence or learning. The practical aim is therefore equitable opportunities to develop scientific understanding, with the success of these response practices still to be examined.
