# Discussion

Marsh et al. (2022) provide evidence that mathematics self-concept—students' beliefs about their mathematical competence—and achievement predict one another over time. Across annual observations of 3,425 Bavarian students from Grades 5 to 9, this reciprocal pattern appeared with both school grades and standardized test scores. It supports treating self-concept as more than a reflection of prior performance, while retaining achievement as a predictor of subsequent self-beliefs. Using both achievement indicators broadens this claim across teacher-assessed classroom performance and standardized attainment.

The models offer complementary perspectives on this reciprocity. A conventional cross-lagged panel model (CLPM) asks whether students' relative standing on self-concept predicts their subsequent relative standing on achievement, and conversely, conditional on earlier measurements. These associations retain both stable student differences and within-student variation. The random-intercept cross-lagged panel model (RI-CLPM) separates stable differences and asks whether being above one's usual self-concept level over the study predicts being above one's usual achievement level subsequently, and conversely. It estimates a common within-person association across students. The same directional pattern across these comparisons strengthens the reciprocal account while preserving the distinct meanings of the estimates.

The model extensions clarify why researchers should evaluate fit alongside the substantive question and parameter interpretation. Adding paths spanning two annual intervals substantially improved CLPM fit while leaving the central reciprocal pattern comparatively stable; the focal RI-CLPM paths changed less under the extensions. A specification can therefore improve the representation of longitudinal data without overturning the main educational conclusion.

The authors' proposed feedback–attribution–effort process offers a plausible explanation: students interpret performance, develop competence beliefs and draw on these beliefs when choosing strategies or investing effort. Those intervening steps were unmeasured. The observational design also leaves reciprocal prediction vulnerable to unmeasured time-varying confounding; limited changes after measured-covariate adjustment provided only a weak test of that vulnerability. Only 39% of students participated in all five waves. Full-information maximum likelihood retained partial records, but its missing-data assumptions remain consequential. Future longitudinal studies should examine sensitivity to missingness and changing confounders alongside measuring the proposed psychological sequence.

For educators, the reciprocal pattern provides a reason to attend jointly to students' attainment and their interpretations of achievement feedback. Whether combining support for performance and self-beliefs yields additional benefits is the intervention question arising from this evidence. The authors' proposed factorial randomized trial makes that question testable by independently assigning the presence or absence of a self-concept intervention and an achievement intervention. Comparing the combined programme with each component and with neither would test the predicted benefit of joint provision. Measuring students' feedback interpretations, effort and strategies within such work would also examine the proposed process connecting competence beliefs and attainment.
