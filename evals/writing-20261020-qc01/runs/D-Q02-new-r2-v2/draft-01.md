# Secondary-writing demonstration based on Marsh, Pekrun, and Lüdtke (2022)

Across annual observations of 3,425 students in Bavaria from Grades 5 to 9, mathematics self-concept and achievement positively predicted one another in both model families and for both school grades and standardized tests. This pattern supports the reciprocal-effects account over an account that gives predictive priority exclusively to achievement or self-concept, students' beliefs about their mathematical competence. Including both achievement indicators matters because teacher-assessed classroom performance and standardized performance provide distinct perspectives on what students achieve.

The agreement between models extends this finding across different comparisons. A conventional cross-lagged panel model (CLPM) asks whether students' relative standing on self-concept predicts their subsequent relative standing on achievement, and conversely, conditional on earlier measurements. It combines stable differences between students with variation within students. The random-intercept cross-lagged panel model (RI-CLPM) separates stable differences and asks whether being above one's usual self-concept level in one year predicts being above one's usual achievement level subsequently, and conversely. Its within-person association is estimated across students rather than separately for each individual. Together, the models support reciprocal prediction at distinct comparison levels; their agreement does not mean that they estimate the same relationship. This gives substance to the authors' view that the perspectives are complementary.

The model extensions also show why fit and substantive interpretation need to be considered together. Adding paths spanning two annual intervals substantially improved CLPM fit while leaving the central reciprocal pattern comparatively stable; the focal RI-CLPM paths changed less under the extensions. Researchers should therefore ask what a specification changes about the educational claim, alongside whether it fits better. Explained variance also refers to differently decomposed variation in the two models and cannot serve as a directly interchangeable measure of explanatory success.

A possible explanation for reciprocity is that students interpret performance feedback when forming beliefs about their competence and draw on these beliefs when choosing strategies or investing effort. These psychological steps were not measured. Nor does the limited change after covariate adjustment establish resistance to confounding: the authors acknowledge that it provides a weak test. Unmeasured time-varying influences remain plausible explanations of the observational associations. Incomplete participation adds another uncertainty: only 39% contributed all five waves, and retaining partial records through full-information maximum likelihood depends on missing-data assumptions. Future studies should measure the proposed psychological sequence and examine sensitivity to missingness and changing confounders.

Educationally, the findings support attending to both performance and academic self-beliefs rather than assuming that one can be understood entirely through the other. For teachers, this means considering how students interpret achievement feedback alongside their demonstrated attainment; a combined programme's benefit remains a prediction. The authors' proposed factorial randomized trial would cross the presence or absence of a self-concept intervention with the presence or absence of an achievement intervention. Comparing the combination with either component and with neither would test whether joint attention produces additional gains. Such a trial would move the argument from reciprocal prediction to evidence about how educators can influence the development of both competence beliefs and achievement.
