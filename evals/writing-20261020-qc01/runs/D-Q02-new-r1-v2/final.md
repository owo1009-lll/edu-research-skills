# Secondary-writing demonstration: Marsh, Pekrun, and Lüdtke (2022)

Marsh et al. (2022) strengthen a reciprocal account of mathematics self-concept—students' beliefs about their mathematical competence—and achievement. Across five annual waves following 3,425 Bavarian students from Grades 5 to 9, self-concept and achievement positively predicted one another. This pattern favors reciprocity over accounts assigning either construct only an antecedent role. Its appearance for both school grades and standardized test scores extends the argument beyond a single achievement indicator. Grades convey teacher-assessed classroom performance, whereas standardized tests provide another assessment of achievement.

The agreement between models adds substance because each frames the longitudinal comparison differently. The conventional cross-lagged panel model (CLPM) asks whether students' relative standing on one construct predicts their later standing on another, conditional on preceding measurements. Stable differences between students remain mixed with variation within students. The random-intercept cross-lagged panel model (RI-CLPM) instead separates stable differences and asks whether a year above a student's usual self-concept level predicts achievement above that student's usual level the following year, and conversely. This is a shared within-person association estimated across students, rather than a causal effect calculated separately for each student. Reciprocity across these comparisons supports the authors' complementary perspective: the models address related questions with distinct meanings.

Specification checks further clarify what changed and what remained stable. Adding paths spanning two annual intervals substantially improved CLPM fit without substantially changing the central cross-lagged pattern; the RI-CLPM's focal paths were less affected by the extensions. Researchers should therefore examine parameter interpretation and the substantive relationships alongside model fit. The limited influence of measured covariates offered only a weak test of confounding, and both approaches remain vulnerable to unmeasured factors that vary over time. Incomplete participation across waves also makes missing-data assumptions consequential, despite retaining partial records through full-information maximum likelihood. These constraints leave causal explanations open while preserving the evidence for reciprocal longitudinal associations.

One candidate explanation connects performance feedback, competence beliefs, and subsequent effort or strategy use. Students may interpret achievement information when assessing their mathematical ability and draw on those beliefs when approaching later tasks. This process gives educational meaning to the reciprocal pattern, although its intervening steps were not measured. For teachers, the findings support attending to how students understand their mathematical competence alongside evidence of their attainment. School grades are relevant to this joint attention because they provide visible feedback within the classroom. The study supplies a rationale for considering both aspects when designing support, without establishing the benefits of a particular teaching practice.

The authors propose a factorial randomized trial crossing a self-concept intervention with an achievement intervention, each present or absent. This design would compare the combined programme with each component and with neither, testing whether joint support offers additional benefits. Outcomes should include both grades and standardized tests to preserve their distinct perspectives on achievement. Measuring feedback interpretations, effort, and strategies would also help investigate the proposed psychological process. The research priority is to determine whether joint support changes learning and self-beliefs, and whether those changes follow the sequence suggested by the observational findings.
