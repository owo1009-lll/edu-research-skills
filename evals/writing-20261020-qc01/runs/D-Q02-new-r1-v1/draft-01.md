# Attributed secondary-writing demonstration based on Marsh, Pekrun, and Lüdtke (2022)

Following 3,425 Bavarian students annually from Grades 5 to 9, Marsh, Pekrun, and Lüdtke (2022) found positive reciprocal relationships between mathematics self-concept and achievement. Students' beliefs about their mathematical competence predicted subsequent achievement, and achievement predicted subsequent self-concept. This pattern extended across school mathematics grades and standardized mathematics test scores. Because grades communicate teacher-assessed performance in the classroom whereas standardized tests provide another assessment of achievement, the agreement broadens support for the reciprocal account beyond a single achievement indicator. It also challenges accounts that assign predictive priority exclusively to beliefs or performance.

The study's contribution lies particularly in examining this relationship through complementary longitudinal perspectives. The conventional cross-lagged panel model (CLPM) compares students' relative standing after accounting for previous measurements, combining stable differences between students with variation within them. The random-intercept cross-lagged panel model (RI-CLPM) separates stable person differences and asks whether a deviation above a student's usual self-concept level predicts a subsequent achievement deviation, and conversely. Its estimated relationship is shared across students, rather than representing a separate effect for each individual. Reciprocal associations in both models therefore show that the pattern persists when the substantive comparison changes, although the models do not estimate the same relationship.

The specification comparisons further clarify how to assess that agreement. Adding paths spanning two annual intervals substantially improved the CLPM's fit while leaving its central reciprocal pattern comparatively stable. The RI-CLPM's central paths changed less under the reported extensions. Model evaluation consequently needs to consider the question answered and the focal relationships alongside overall fit. The limited influence of measured covariates gives only a weak test of resistance to confounding; both approaches remain vulnerable to unmeasured factors that change over time. Nor can their explained variance be compared directly, because each decomposes variation differently.

A plausible interpretation is that students use performance feedback to form competence beliefs that inform subsequent effort and strategy choices. These psychological steps were not measured, leaving the proposed process to be examined directly. Incomplete participation across waves also limits interpretation: retaining partial records through full-information maximum likelihood depends on missing-data assumptions. The longitudinal ordering and model agreement thus support a reciprocal account of observed associations without establishing that changing either construct will improve the other. Research measuring feedback interpretation and subsequent effort would help connect the annual associations to the proposed process.

For educators, this account supports attending to academic self-beliefs alongside performance. In classrooms resembling those studied, considering students' competence beliefs together with their achievement offers a broader basis for deciding what support to investigate. The authors' proposed factorial randomized trial gives this educational position a concrete test: cross the presence or absence of a self-concept intervention with the presence or absence of an achievement intervention, then compare the combined programme with each component and with neither. The predicted advantage of the combination remains an empirical question. This design would establish whether the reciprocal observational pattern can inform a programme that improves self-concept and achievement together.
