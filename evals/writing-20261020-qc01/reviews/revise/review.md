**Comparative judgment**

On factual fidelity, the central argument, citation retention and format compliance, the evidence supports a tie. On deliverable completeness, all four have the same missing accompanying record. There is no justification for assigning a scientific winner or inventing serious faults to separate these close revisions.

For readers unfamiliar with these models, B has a small advantage: “deviations from a student’s usual self-concept predict later deviations in achievement, and vice versa” says what is being related across time, while its competence-beliefs gloss explains the substantive construct. D adds the domain cue “In this mathematics study” and gives a concise distinction between cross-level interpretation and causal inference. Its “lagged relationships between deviations” remains less concrete than B’s example.

That preference has a tradeoff. B turns the original’s general warning about causal interpretation into a more specific claim about “causal processes within students.” This is plausible synthesis, but the supplied materials do not independently establish how common that precise interpretive error is. D changes the original’s criticism of research practice into a general inferential rule. Neither is a demonstrated factual error; A is a reasonable choice if preserving the original’s critical emphasis is the highest priority.

A’s explanation of stable relationships over time and similar relationships across individuals is plainer than C’s “stable parameters” and “homogeneous relations.” C still preserves the distinction accurately and explains more than the original did. These limited differences justify a narrow preference for B, with D close, while leaving A and C acceptable. No preference rests simply on caution, assertiveness, keyword frequency or sentence length.
